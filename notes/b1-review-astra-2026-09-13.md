# The Unfinished Apprentice — manuscript and POV review
September 13, 2026

**Scope:** A focused editorial assessment, not a cover-to-cover line edit or continuity certification. I surveyed chapter openings and endings across the 42-chapter manuscript and closely read selected scenes and chapters, including the opening, first font sequence (7–8), Wick's Kindling (12) and What Holds (27), Kessa's spring encounter (25), the Ash/Quill material (30–31), Cael's waystation chapter (33), and the final confrontation and aftermath (38–42). Some intervening material was reviewed through excerpts. Recommendations about pacing are therefore provisional; the scene and voice observations below are grounded in those readings.

## Overall judgment

There is a compelling book here. The strongest material combines Oscar's fear of being a copy with the ordinary relationships that gradually make that question less decisive. His development from someone who explains everything to someone who can admit uncertainty gives the fantasy plot an emotional purpose.

The distinctive invention is magic as something written, read, revised, and administered. The House's paper, the Redactor, the Setter, and the reuse of Evermere all belong to that same imaginative system. Bureaucratic power is frightening here because it can literally decide what counts as a person.

The main weakness is an excess of explanation and rhetorical emphasis. The prose often makes an effective image or lets a character perform a revealing action, then interprets that action, then adds a compact statement of its meaning. Each sentence may be good in isolation, but the accumulation makes some passages feel rehearsed. An editorial pass that trusts the image and the reader more would help considerably.

## The four focal voices

### Oscar: verbal control used to manage fear

Oscar sounds scholarly, socially practiced, proud, and quick to turn discomfort into an argument. He notices workings, grammar, provenance, and inconsistencies. His elaborate qualifications and defensive wit belong to him.

The Quill scene in Chapter 31 is especially successful. His description of himself as the original man's “errata” is both character-specific and painful. The unsuccessful invitation to perch makes the identity problem physical: a held-out arm can carry more weight than another explanation of being a copy.

His late refusal to promise Kessa a rescue in Chapter 41 is another strong turn. He still speaks at length, but he now spends those words establishing the limit of his knowledge. That is recognizable growth within the same voice.

**Revision:** Keep his elaborate syntax, but reduce occasions when the narration explains that a joke conceals pain immediately after we have seen it do so. Let an abandoned sentence, an unnecessary adjustment of his sleeve, or silence do some of that work. When every reaction receives an elegant metaphor, even his loss of control can sound too controlled.

### Wick: immediate, bodily, defensive, hungry

Wick is the most immediately distinguishable voice. Her thoughts attach to heat, hunger, physical discomfort, embarrassment, and being useful. The self-interruptions and repetitions make sense as a mind trying to cope while it is still having the feeling.

The opening of Chapter 30—eating a third bowl of stew while resenting its hospitality—does several jobs at once. She is young and hungry; she wants to maintain her anger; her body refuses to cooperate. That is characterization through experience rather than description.

Chapter 27 also gives her real expertise. Understanding that wanting to stop and actually stopping are different things grows from her daily experience of containment. She is not merely the unstable child whom adults manage.

**Revision:** Watch the density of “stupid,” “worst,” “whole life,” and statements about nobody having understood her before. These are useful ingredients, but repeated at high intensity they narrow her range. Let her occasionally be observant, unfair, amused, or competent without arriving at an emotional superlative. Preserve moments where she notices something the adults miss.

Her colloquial language reads younger than Oscar's formal register. That distinction works. The risk is not that she sounds insufficiently old-fashioned; it is that her least articulate moments sometimes become as neatly phrased as everyone else's.

### Kessa: love expressed as practical responsibility

Kessa notices positions, exits, tracks, numbers, equipment, and the next action. Her tactical observations change what happens. In Chapter 25 she updates her understanding of Oscar's capabilities during a fight rather than pausing to admire him. That is a convincing focal lens.

The counting motif works best when circumstances alter it. In Chapter 41, trying to make four become five is devastating because counting has been her method of keeping people safe. The grey stone and the ordinary rat are strong concrete anchors for grief. Her care also comes through in “Cael's cold and I'm not carrying two of you”: she gives Oscar a practical reason to stand when an emotional appeal might fail.

**Revision:** Reduce labels such as “flat” and “not unkindly” where the dialogue already establishes her manner. Inside her POV, give slightly more room to plain observations and slightly less to beautifully resolved interpretations of those observations. She does not need to translate every practical act into an account of what love or grief means.

The count should remain, but reserve its fullest ceremonial form for moments when it changes or costs her something. Ordinary checks can be brief. That contrast will protect the ending.

### Cael: tactile attention and moral responsibility

Cael's Chapter 33 is one of the strongest close-read chapters. He knows the waypost through his hands, repairs a boot with ordinary thread, recognizes the men approaching as people with homes and wages, and measures his ward by what it will do to their bodies. These details distinguish him from both Oscar and Kessa.

His decision to kill Corwen carries weight because his usual means of helping are still available and cannot solve this particular problem. The warmth answering him is more interesting than a simple loss of magical power would have been. Afterward, the return to triage is credible and painful.

**Revision:** Let more of his thinking settle into complete, steady sentences. His deliberate physical movement can be reflected in his prose without making him dull. Some of the shared fragments and verbal reversals make him sound closer to Oscar than his attention to the world suggests.

I would consider one modest earlier opportunity for Cael's private doubt or irritation. That need not become a new subplot or a large increase in POV chapters. The purpose would be to let readers know him from inside before his greatest moral crisis. He should have a self beyond being useful and merciful to the others.

### Tarn, Ash, and the antagonists

Tarn reads as a distinct supporting presence in the passages reviewed, rather than as a separate focal voice. His short answers, physical competence, and language of debt work. “Ask me a different question” during the final fight is effective because it refuses reassurance without explaining itself. A further ordinary preference or want beyond debt and fighting could widen him without changing that economy.

Ash's family connection makes some verbal resemblance to Oscar appropriate. However, her explanations can become too comprehensive and too polished, making her sound like an authority on the novel's theme. A little more evasiveness that is recognizably personal would make her stronger.

Corwen and the Setter share a restrained administrative manner. That supports the House's character, but their differences deserve protection: Corwen can sound like someone making a case for an institution; the Setter is most disturbing when it reaches a genuine absence in itself. “I no longer have the part that keeps things” is more unsettling than another elegantly sinister maxim would be.

## Where the voices overlap

The cast has different priorities, but the narration repeatedly uses the same devices:
- Defining something through a succession of rejected alternatives.
- A short fragment followed by a stronger corrective fragment.
- Explaining that something quiet or kind is worse than something overtly threatening.
- Ending a passage with an aphorism.
- Repeated images of doors, holding, warmth, paper, and the body understanding before the mind.

Several of these motifs are fundamental to the book. Removing them indiscriminately would damage it. The useful distinction is between a recurrence that changes meaning and a recurrence that repeats the same explanation.

A good revision exercise would be to take a few internal paragraphs from each POV and remove the names. Ask whether the sentence rhythm and choice of detail identify the observer. Dialogue is already relatively distinct; the surrounding narration needs more variation.

A working guide:
- Oscar asks how something works and what its failure says about him.
- Wick asks what it feels like and whether she can bear or control it.
- Kessa asks what changed, who is exposed, and what can be done next.
- Cael asks who is hurting and what his help would require.

These are tendencies, not restrictions. Characters should surprise readers.

## Story and structure: priorities

### 1. Clarify the status of Ash's explanation for leaving

In Chapter 31, Ash says that another hand on the choice makes it someone else's choice. Yet Chapter 8 explicitly makes Oscar's successful action possible through the other three, and the final confrontation again depends on the crew and Quill.

This could be a productive disagreement between Ash's lonely history and Oscar's experience. It becomes awkward if her claim is meant as an unquestioned law of the world.

I would make her reasoning more clearly hers: a fear of directing him, a limitation on what she can do, or an interpretation shaped by how she escaped. The story's existing distinction between helping somebody choose and choosing for them is richer than a rule that help invalidates agency.

### 2. Give the two acts of refusal different dramatic jobs

The first font sequence already gives Oscar a major act of self-definition through generosity. The final refusal needs to move beyond restating that achievement. The manuscript does supply further developments: accepting connection, calling Quill, admitting limits, and facing a consequence his refusal does not solve.

Emphasize those developments in the middle. The road should keep changing his understanding of freedom and responsibility, rather than repeatedly establishing that he is made and deserves to choose. A full pacing edit would need a continuous read of the intervening chapters before deciding cuts.

### 3. Make the climax's mechanics clear before its emotional peak

The final sequence combines a fault opened by a question, Quill's response, the Setter's destruction, the uncontrolled font, and Wick's relationship to the far side. All have emotional logic, but the reader is also doing substantial conceptual work under pressure.

Check the earlier setup for the precise distinction between a working contradicted from within, a body injured from outside, and a source of power drained. The aim is to let the climax spend established rules without pausing to explain all of them anew.

### 4. Preserve the aftermath's concrete detail

The bread, stone, rat, and uncertain funeral words are some of the strongest material. They show what the victory cost in terms the characters can actually experience. Protect them during any attempt to shorten the ending.

Chapter 42 immediately lets the reader know Wick survives in some form. That changes Chapter 41 from shared uncertainty into dramatic irony: the reader knows something the crew cannot. I would keep the final chapter if Book Two's invitation is Wick's predicament, while keeping that predicament frightening and materially costly. Her inability to simply open the door home already helps.

## Suggested order for a revision

1. Clarify Ash's rationale and the intended relationship between help and agency.
2. Verify the setup for the final magical cause and effect.
3. Differentiate internal narration by attention and rhythm.
4. Trim repeated interpretations after strong actions or images.
5. Review motif placement, preserving the most consequential repetitions.
6. Then do a continuous pacing and continuity pass before final copyediting.

I would not rebuild the cast. Oscar's defensive intelligence, Wick's insistence on being useful, Kessa's practical care, and Cael's difficult mercy already come across. The opportunity is to give their strongest moments more space by asking fewer neighboring sentences to announce their significance.
