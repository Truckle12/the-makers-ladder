## Chapter Thirty-Eight — The Gates of Everset

They came into the city that was built to finish him through a drain, waist-deep, in the dark, and Oscar would think about that afterward with a certain grim satisfaction, because of all the ways a made thing might be brought home to be completed, the one nobody had written down was *wading.*

Tarn had been right. Cities leak. The channels that carried all that bright water down out of Everset went somewhere, and where they went there was an old brick throat under the west wall with a grating across it that had been made by people who assumed nobody would ever be interested. Oscar was extremely interested. He put his hand on the grate's fixings and read them, and they were old honest ironwork with no cleverness in them at all, and he took two of them apart while Kessa held a lamp with her hand cupped over it and said nothing, and the whole business was so *easy* that his stomach hurt.

"That's it?" Wick whispered. "That's the back door of the mouth of the world?"

"That's it." He was already crawling. "This is the thing nobody tells you about great and terrible places. Somebody has to drain them."

The water was cold and the tunnel went on a long time. And then the brick under his hands stopped being brick.

He felt the change before he could see it. The wall went from cut-and-laid to something older and *smoother,* not built but grown, a single unjointed surface with a faint slow warmth in it — and under his palm his gift, which he had not even opened, sat up like a dog hearing a name.

He stopped dead in the dark with the water running past his knees.

"Oscar." Kessa, behind him. "Move or explain."

"The deep current," he said. His voice came out strange in the pipe. "It's not under this city. It's *in* it. This tunnel wall is drawing on it. A drain, Kessa. Somebody built the sewer out of the same stuff you'd build a — " he stopped, because the true end of that sentence was *a god,* and he did not want to say that in a pipe. "It's like finding a cathedral holding up a cowshed. Nobody does this. Nobody has this much."

"Is it dangerous?"

"It's a *drain,*" Oscar said. "That's what's frightening about it," and he crawled on.

---

They came up in a wash-yard behind a fuller's, an hour before dawn, and lay in the straw until the city woke, because Kessa would not put five wet strangers into an unknown street in the dark.

So Oscar was awake, and cold, and lying on his back in somebody's laundry, when Everset started its day.

A shutter went up. Then another. Somebody down the lane began sweeping and did it badly and sang while doing it, off-key, cheerfully, a song about a man who married three sisters in succession and regretted the middle one. A dog barked and was told to shut up by a voice that plainly loved it. Bread came out of an oven somewhere south of the yard and the smell of it went over the wall and hit Oscar in the chest like a fist.

He had spent a season building a monster in his head.

The monster smelled of bread.

"Well," Tarn said, from the straw, "that's inconvenient."

---

They walked into it at full light, and Everset was beautiful, and — worse, much worse — Everset was *happy.*

That was the blow he had not braced for. He had braced for cold. He had braced for silence, uniforms, a grey population moving in step, all the shapes a nightmare takes when a clerk builds it. What he got was a market.

Pale stone and water running everywhere in cut channels; lamps still burning on the bridges in the daylight because nobody had needed to trim them; gardens on the roofs, spilling over. And under all that, people. A woman arguing with a fishmonger with real artistry, both of them enjoying it. Two boys running a hoop between the legs of the crowd and being cuffed for it by strangers who then laughed. An old man asleep sitting up in the sun with a cat on him. Somebody's grandmother holding court on a doorstep about the disgraceful state of somebody else's grandson. A wedding party, or the wreckage of one, four young people in yesterday's flowers walking home barefoot along a wall.

Wick stopped in the middle of the street with her mouth open.

"It's *nice,*" she said, and it came out like an accusation. "Oscar. It's really nice. There's a *pie stall.*"

"I noticed the pie stall."

"There's *three* pie stalls."

"I have noticed all three of them and I would like it recorded that I am finding this considerably harder than the drain."

Because he was. He had come five hundred miles to a place he was ready to hate, and the place had handed him a morning: warm stone, a good smell, children being nuisances, a man selling something on a stick. He kept waiting to catch the seam — the flat note, the performance under it — and it wasn't there. Nobody was pretending. These were people who had woken up in a town they liked.

"Kessa," he said quietly. "Read it for me. I don't trust myself here."

She had been doing nothing else since the wash-yard. "Nobody's afraid," she said. "That's the first thing. Not of us, not of each other, not of the guards — and there's guards, four so far, and one of them just bought a bun." Her eyes went along the roofline and came back. "Everyone's fed. Nobody's limping. That woman put her purse down on a barrel to count fish and left it there." A pause. "It's the best-run place I've ever stood in, and I want you to know I'm saying that with my whole chest and I still don't like it."

"Why not?"

"Ask me in an hour," Kessa said. "It's not lying to me yet. It's just not saying enough."

---

It was Cael who worked out the first true thing, and he did it standing still in the middle of a street with his eyes shut and his hands open, which is a conspicuous thing for a very large man to do at market hour.

"Cael?"

"It's *warm,*" he said.

He opened one hand, and the hearth-light came up in it low and gold and absolutely steady, throwing shadow up a pale wall in broad daylight, and Oscar saw two women glance at it and glance away with the practised incuriosity people give a stranger's prayer.

"In here. Right in the belly of it." Cael's plain face was doing something complicated. "It's the same as it was at the waystation. Not a flicker in it. I've been afraid for two days that I'd walk in here and go out like a candle, and instead — " He stopped, and his mouth moved, and Oscar watched a slow man arrive somewhere fast. "Oh."

"Say it."

"They *pray* here." He said it with wonder, and then, immediately after, with something much heavier. "Oscar, listen. What I answer to — it isn't a river that runs everywhere. It's a hall, and the hall is short where there's people and long where there isn't, and out on the heath it's so long I can barely shout down it. That's not a thing about *me.* That's a thing about *them.* The hall's short where somebody's keeping a hearth and meaning it." He looked around at the market, at the bread, at the grandmother on her doorstep. "This is the shortest that hall has ever been in my life. Which means there are more people in this town keeping a fire and believing in something at the end of it than anywhere I have ever walked."

"In the font?" Oscar said. "They pray to the thing in the middle of the city?"

"Some do. I've heard them say so twice this morning." Cael closed his hand and the light went out of it. "But that's not what's answering. I'd know. Whatever's got hold of the other end of my hall, it doesn't live in a great building. It's never once lived in a great building." Something in him settled, and hardened. "It lives in kitchens. It's here because *they're* here. That's the mercy of it and I'm going to hold onto it, because I don't like where the rest of this morning is going."

Oscar put that somewhere safe, and had cause to go back for it before the day was out.

---

The rest of the morning went where it was going about a quarter of an hour later, and Kessa was the one who put a name on it.

She had crouched at the edge of a yard with her fingers in the gutter-mud, and her face had the flat set it got when the ground lied.

"There's no rats," she said.

"What?"

"No rats. No mice. No pigeons on that roof, no droppings on that sill, no weeds in this crack, and there is *always* weeds in a crack." She stood, wiping her hand. "I've read a hundred towns. A town's mostly the things living in it that nobody invited. This one's got none."

And the morning turned over.

Because once she'd said it he could not stop seeing it, and it was not the absence of vermin, it was the *shape* of the absence. No beggars — and he had been in enough towns to know a town without beggars is a town that did something about them. No one very old and no one very ill. No accents. The arguing woman and the fishmonger had exactly the same vowels, and so did the grandmother, and so did the guard with the bun. Everyone dressed a little bit the same, in a way that no one had ordered, in a way that came from below rather than above.

And there was one more thing he owed her, and he made himself say it out loud at the next corner, because he had promised.

"I can read all of this," he said. "Grammar, clauses, purpose. I could stand here a year and take notes and probably understand the whole of it. And I want to be very clear about the next part, so nobody builds a plan on the wrong hope." He looked at the calm lovely working running away in every direction under the market. "I can't *hold* any of it. Reading isn't lifting. If this thing turns round and asks whether I belong, knowing exactly how the question is phrased will not help me survive the answer. I'm a man who can read a river. I still drown in it."

Kessa took that the way she took everything, which was as something to be spent later. "Then reading's for finding the door," she said. "Not the fight."

"Yes."

"Good. Say that to yourself twice a day."

He stood in the best-run place he had ever seen and understood at last what he was standing in.

It was clean. Not tidy. *Clean,* in the way a page is clean when someone has been going over it with a pen and striking out what doesn't belong — and the striking-out never stops, and it is never loud, and the city simply *stays* lovely. They were happy. He believed the happiness completely. That was the horror: it was not a lie. It was an *edit,* and it had been running so long and so gently that the people inside it had never once had a bad day they could name.

"It's not a city," he said. "It's a fair copy of one."

And he heard the word come out of his own mouth and went cold to the wrists, because it was Ash's word, and he had not meant to use it.

And then the name arrived, the way they always arrived, too late and all at once.

"Oh," he said.

"What," said Kessa.

"The *name.*" He laughed, and it was not a good sound. "Ever*set.* It isn't a place-name at all, it's a *state.* It's what you call a thing that has been fixed in its final form and cannot be altered any more. You set type. You set a stone. You set a thing so it stays." His hands had gone cold. "And the font at the middle of it isn't a spring. Or it is, but that's not why they call it that. A font is the case you cast the letters out of. You break the old ones up and pour them again into whatever you want to say next." He looked up the hill toward the pale crown of the city. "Somebody built a town on a font and named it Everset, and nobody has thought twice about it since, and I want it noted that this is exactly the sort of joke a man makes when he is quite certain nobody clever will ever read it."

Nobody said anything. Down the street, somebody's grandmother was still holding court.

---

The other thing arrived by degrees, and he tried very hard not to look at it, and it kept not letting him.

He had felt it first at the third crossing and told himself it was the tiredness. By the sixth he had stopped telling himself anything. The city's great working sat on top of something *earlier* — foundations that did not match, a street whose line ignored the buildings on it and obeyed some other plan, a doorway bricked up in a wall that had never had a building behind it.

And the older bones were *familiar.*

Not Evermere-familiar. That was the trouble. He had been braced for Evermere, had been braced since Marrow, had a whole apparatus assembled in himself for the moment the borrowed memory said *home.* This was a different door and it opened on a different room and he was not braced for it at all.

He knew the stone because he had woken on it.

Under the market, under the lovely pale skin of the place, Everset had the same bones as the buried city five hundred miles north — the one he had come up out of under the ice with a stranger's memories in him and no name for the cold. The same unjointed stone. The same way a wall met a floor with no seam and no apology. The same *habit* — recognizable across a room, like handwriting, before you have read a word of it.

He stopped walking, and put his palm flat on a perfectly ordinary bollard, and read it, and got back the exact texture of the thing he had been lying in when he first opened his eyes: a basin, cold as a grave, in a hall full of cradles, under script that brightened when it spoke.

*The master has fallen. Ascend. Be set. Inherit what is yours.*

"Oscar." Wick's hand was on his sleeve. "You've gone the colour of the wall."

"The place they woke me in," he said. His mouth was dry. "It's the same building."

"What is?"

"This. All of this." He took his hand off the bollard and it shook, so he put it in his pocket. "The dead city under the ice. I've been calling it a *tomb* for a season because that's what it was doing when I met it — but it wasn't built as a tomb, Wick, it was built as *this,* and it stopped. Same stone. Same maker. Same — " he went looking for a better word and turned up a worse one — "same *trade.* One of them failed and went under the ice with a hall full of cradles in it. The other one is standing here selling pies."

Nobody said anything. Down the lane the man with the bad broom was still singing.

"Which one am I from?" Wick said.

"You aren't from either. You're from a village that was cruel to you and a wood that wasn't." He said it too fast and too hard and meant every syllable, and saw it land. "That's mine to carry, not yours. But I'd like our crew close together for the next hour, and I would like it very much if nobody wandered."

*Our crew.* He heard himself say it and did not take it back. There had been a time — Marrow, the pit, most of the road — when he'd have said *your crew* to Kessa, or *the crew,* keeping the pronoun at arm's length like a coat he was only borrowing. He was too tired to do that today. They were his, and he was theirs, and the city that made him had no line in its book for either half of that sentence.

Kessa heard it too. She didn't remark on it. She just moved a half-step in, and so did the others, without being told, and the five of them went up the hill as one object.

---

And that was when the city noticed him.

It began with a girl of about nine carrying a basket of cut flowers, who stopped in front of Oscar and looked up at his face and went absolutely still.

"It's you," she said.

"I'm sorry?"

"It's *you.*" She turned around and shouted it down the street with the full lung capacity of a nine-year-old. "IT'S HIM! HE'S COME!"

Kessa's hand went to Doss's knife. Tarn stepped wide. And Everset — the market, the pie stalls, the grandmother, the barefoot wedding party, the guard with the bun — turned around and looked at Oscar Alberich, and began, warmly, delightedly, entirely without malice, to *cheer.*

It was the worst thing that had happened to him since the ice.

They came out of the shops. Somebody put a cup of something hot in his hand and he took it because his body took it. An old man got hold of his other hand in both of his and would not let go and said *thank you* with tears standing in his eyes, and could not or would not say what for. A woman lifted a baby up so the baby could see him, and the baby, uninterested, tried to eat its own fist. The flower girl emptied her basket over him. He stood in a fall of cut stems in a beautiful street with a hot cup he had not asked for and forty strangers smiling at him and he had never in his life, in either of his lives, been so frightened.

"They know my *face,*" he said, to no one. "Kessa. They have never seen me and they know my *face* — "

"Walk," Kessa said, very low, at his shoulder. "Don't run. Walk, and smile, and don't take your hand off that cup, because if you drop it they'll all look at the ground and there's nothing on the ground I want them looking at."

So he walked, and they came with him — not herding him, and that was what undid him; they were *escorting* him, the way a town escorts a bride. People fell in on both sides. Somebody started a song and it was taken up badly. A man ran ahead to tell people further along. The crowd got thicker and the street got wider and Oscar understood, distantly, through the roaring in his ears, that he was not choosing his direction any more and had not been for two streets.

"Where are they taking me?"

"Up," Tarn said. "Same place all of it points. Have a look at the gutters."

He looked at the gutters. Every channel in Everset ran *out* from the crown of the hill, and every street bent toward it, and every roof-line stepped up to it, and he had been reading kerbstones for an hour without once lifting his eyes to see that the entire city was a single figure with one enormous clause at the middle of it, and the clause was a building.

"What do you call it?" he asked the old man still holding his hand. "That. At the top."

The old man beamed at him as though he'd asked something charming.

"The Fair House," he said.

---

Oscar did not say anything for the rest of the climb, which the crowd took for reverence.

The Fair House stood at the crown of Everset behind a court of pale stone, and it was, without any argument that Oscar could raise, the most beautiful building he had ever seen. Not the vastness that bullies. The vastness that *welcomes*: a long low front, a flight of shallow steps worn into dishes by feet, three great doors standing open because they were always standing open, and above them no statue, no seal, no name — just a smooth stone tympanum where a name plainly used to be and had been taken off.

The crowd stopped at the edge of the court. All of them. In one motion, like water finding a level, and not one of them came a step further.

"They don't go in," Kessa said.

"No," said the old man, still beaming, still holding Oscar's hand. "Not us. We keep it lovely." He said it with enormous pride. "You go in. That's what you're *for.*"

And he let go of Oscar's hand, and patted it twice, and stepped back into the crowd, and the crowd, all of it, went quiet and waited with their faces shining to watch the heir go home.

---

Inside, the Fair House was one room, and the room went on.

Colonnades ran away on both sides into a soft standing light that came from nowhere he could see. Galleries above. The floor was a single sheet of that unjointed pale stone, and it was worn — genuinely worn, in tracks, by feet, over a length of time his mind slid off. Water ran in a cut channel around the walls with a sound like breathing. There was no altar, no ranked seating, no images. Whatever the citizens of Everset believed in, they had not been given anything to look at.

And at the centre, where a cathedral keeps its holiest thing, the deep current came up out of the world.

There was no way to make it small. It rose in a slow standing column of cold written light, up past the galleries, up into a roof he could not see the underside of, silent, unwavering — the substrate of everything, the cold under-thing he had spent his whole borrowed life brushing at with his fingertips, hauled up into a building and put to work. Around its foot lay the basin. The font. A great shallow ring of that same seamless stone, and the light came up through it and spread out under the floor in threads too fine to see unless you knew to look, and Oscar knew to look, and once he had looked he could not stop seeing it. Every street. Every lamp. Every clean crack with no weeds in it. The pie stalls. The grandmother. All of it hanging off this.

*It reads the place and strikes out what doesn't belong.* Ash had told him what waited and he had made himself a picture, and the picture had been far too small, because he had been imagining a monster and this was a *utility.*

And beside the basin, set on the stone, plain and low and entirely unremarkable, there was a chair.

His knees wanted to fold. He locked them and stayed standing, and the horror came up through his body first and arrived at his mind about a second later carrying a small tidy card that read: *this is what you were fetched home for, and it is not even angry with you.*

"Oscar," Kessa said. She had her back to him and Doss's knife out and low, and her voice had gone very flat. "There's someone at the water."

There was someone at the water.

A figure had come around the curve of the basin, walking without haste, and stopped at the rim perhaps forty paces off. It did not shout. It did not summon anyone. It stood with its hands folded in front of it and its head tipped very slightly, and it looked at them across the pale floor with the patience of a thing that had been standing in that spot on and off for a very long time and had no particular objection to standing there a while longer.

Oscar's gift went out to it before he could stop it, pure reflex, the oldest thing he owned.

And it came back *full.*

That was the wrongness. Under all the other wrongnesses this morning, that was the one, and it dropped through him like cold water. A living body gave his looking nothing. That was the rule, the whole rule, the one his whole life was built on. He had read a man's coat and never the man. But this thing at the water's edge came back to him *made* — worked, written, composed, a thing with an author and a grammar and a beginning — and the grammar was in a hand he had spent a month reading by firelight, and the shape of the argument was one he could have finished himself.

He did not understand what he was looking at. His body understood perfectly and tried to take him backward a step; he stopped it, mostly.

"You're late," said the figure at the font.

Its voice was pleasant and unhurried and carried across the stone without effort, and it was not the voice of a monster, or a king, or a god. It was the voice of a clerk who has kept an appointment open past closing as a courtesy.

"They've been sending for you," it said, "for a very long time. Come in off the cold floor. There's no call to stand about by the door."

And then, mildly, as though it were the most ordinary observation in the world. And it was. It *was* ordinary, and Oscar would carry the ordinariness of it a great deal further than any threat:

"You'll want to be finished. Everybody does, at the end."
