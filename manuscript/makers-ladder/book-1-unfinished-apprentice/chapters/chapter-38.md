## Chapter Thirty-Eight — The Gates of Everset

They came into the city that was built to finish him through a drain, waist-deep, in the dark, and Oscar would think about that afterward with a certain grim satisfaction, because of all the ways a made thing might be brought home to be completed, the one nobody had written down was *wading.*

Tarn had been right. Cities leak. The channels that carried all that bright water down out of Everset went somewhere, and where they went there was an old brick throat under the west wall with a grating across it that had been made three hundred years ago by people who assumed nobody would ever be interested. Oscar was extremely interested. He put his hand on the grate's fixings and read them, and they were old honest ironwork with no cleverness in them at all, and he took two of them apart while Kessa held a lamp with her hand cupped over it and said nothing, and the whole business was so *easy* that his stomach hurt.

"That's it?" Wick whispered. "That's the back door of the mouth of the world?"

"That's it." He was already crawling. "This is the thing nobody tells you about great and terrible places. Somebody has to drain them."

The water was cold and the tunnel went on a long time. And then the brick under his hands stopped being brick.

He felt the change before he could see it. The wall went from cut-and-laid to something older and *smoother,* not built but grown, a single unjointed surface with a faint slow warmth in it — and under his palm his gift, which he had not even opened, sat up like a dog hearing a name.

He stopped dead in the dark with the water running past his knees.

"Oscar." Kessa, behind him. "Move or explain."

"The deep current," he said. His voice came out strange in the pipe. "It's not under this city. It's *in* it. This tunnel wall is drawing on it. A drain, Kessa. Somebody built the sewer out of the same stuff you'd build a — " he stopped, because the true end of that sentence was *a god,* and he did not want to say that in a pipe. "It's like finding a cathedral holding up a cowshed. Nobody does this. Nobody has this much."

"Is it dangerous?"

"It's a *drain,*" Oscar said. "That's what's frightening about it," and he crawled on, and behind him he heard Wick say, very quietly, "seven," and Kessa say, "marked," and it was the most frightening exchange he had ever heard two people have.

---

They came up in a wash-yard behind a fuller's, at night, in a city that did not sleep so much as idle.

And Everset was beautiful.

It was the first blow and he had not braced for it. He had spent a season building a monster in his head, and what he came up into was a city so lovely it made his chest ache: pale stone, water running everywhere in cut channels, lamps burning on the bridges with a clean steady light nobody had to trim. Colonnades. Gardens on the roofs. Everything in good repair. And *quiet* — not empty-quiet, ordinary-quiet, the low hum of a large town at night with all its people in it, minding their business, going home.

He stood dripping in a stranger's wash-yard and looked at the most civilized place he had ever seen and could not work out for a full minute why the back of his neck had gone tight.

Then Kessa said, "There's no rats."

She was crouched at the edge of the yard with her fingers in the gutter-mud, and her face had the flat set it got when the ground lied. "No rats. No mice. No pigeons on that roof, no droppings on the sill, no weeds in this crack, and there is *always* weeds in a crack." She stood, wiping her hand. "I've read a hundred towns. A town's mostly the things living in it that nobody invited. This one's got none."

And that was when Oscar's neck let go of its secret, and he looked up at the lovely bridges and the unguttering lamps and understood what he was standing in.

It was clean. Not tidy. *Clean,* in the way a page is clean when someone has been going over it with a pen and striking out what doesn't belong — and the striking-out never stops, and it is never loud, and the city simply *stays* beautiful. No rats. No weeds. No beggars, he noticed, sweeping the long lamplit street. No accents. Everyone dressed a little bit the same. A man had come apart at the gate this morning in four heartbeats and the queue had stepped around the spot, and this was where that man had been going, and this was what he had been failing to belong to.

"It's an edit," Oscar said. "It's not a city, it's a — " and there it was, the thing he'd been circling since the valley, arriving as they always arrived, too late and all at once. "Oh."

"Wizard," Tarn said.

"The name." He laughed, and it was not a good sound. "Ever*set*. It's not a place-name at all, it's a *state.* It's what you call something when it's been fixed in its final form and can't be altered. You set type. You set a stone. You set a thing so it stays." His hands had gone cold. "The font at the middle of this town isn't a spring, Kessa. Or it is, but that's not why they call it that. A font is where you *cast the letters from.* It's the case the type comes out of. They built a city on a font and named it Everset and nobody has thought twice about it in three hundred years, and I want it noted that this is exactly the sort of joke a man makes when he's certain nobody clever is ever going to read it."

Nobody said anything. Down the street, the beautiful lamps burned on.

"Right," Kessa said at last. "So we're inside the machine."

"We're inside the machine."

"Then we find the middle of it, and we look at it once, and we get out and decide." She was already reading the street for exits and finding, Oscar could see, that the street would not read: too regular, too repaired, no wear to tell direction from. "Wick. Number."

"Seven," Wick said. "Still seven. It doesn't get worse in here, it just gets — " she made a helpless shape with one hand "— *closer.*"

---

They went in toward the middle by the small streets, and the city let them, and every step of it Oscar read what he could and did not like the shape of what came back.

The whole place was one working. His gift kept insisting on it and his mind kept refusing: not a city with workings *in* it — a single continuous figure the size of a valley, laid down street by street, everything joined. He could follow it. That was the horror and the seduction together. He could stand at a corner and put his attention on the kerbstone and read the argument the kerbstone was making, and the argument was elegant, and it agreed with the argument the bridge two streets over was making, and both of them served a clause he couldn't see the end of. He had never in his life read anything this good. He had never wanted so badly to keep reading. Somewhere behind his sternum an apprentice-shaped part of him was standing with its mouth open going *look what you can do with it, look what it can be* — and he had to keep taking his own attention off it, the same as taking your tongue off a broken tooth, and he was tired, and it was very hard.

And under it, everywhere, the older bones.

He'd first felt them at the third crossing and told himself it was nothing. By the sixth he'd stopped telling himself anything. The city's great working sat on top of something *earlier* — foundations that did not match, a street whose line ignored the buildings and obeyed some other plan, a well-head of dark stone in a courtyard that the new pale city had grown politely around without touching. Old work. Very old. And every time his looking brushed those bones it came back with the same non-answer his own memory kept giving him, that flat familiar *this is yours* with no when attached, and his stomach rolled over, and he made himself walk on.

He would not go and look at the well-head. He noted, precisely, that he would not, and that he could not have said whether it was fear or courtesy, and that neither was a good reason.

"You keep stopping," Wick said, at his elbow.

"I keep nearly recognizing things. It's like being introduced to someone at a party a second time." His mouth did the joke automatically. His hands were shaking. "I'll manage."

What he could *not* manage was the other thing, and he made himself say it out loud to Kessa at the next corner, because he'd promised.

"I can read all of this," he said. "Grammar, clauses, purpose. I could stand here for a year and take notes and probably understand it. And I want to be very clear about the next part, so nobody builds a plan on the wrong hope." He looked at the endless calm lamplit working running away in all directions. "I can't *hold* any of it. Reading isn't lifting. If this thing turns and asks whether I belong, knowing exactly how the question is phrased will not help me survive the answer. I'm a man who can read a river. I still drown in it."

Kessa took that the way she took everything, which was as something to be spent later. "Then reading's for finding the door. Not the fight."

"Yes."

"Good," she said. "Say that to yourself twice a day."

---

Cael was the one who kept them upright, and none of them realized it until the fourth street, when he stopped and stood in the middle of the road with his eyes shut.

"Cael?"

"It's warm," he said. He sounded surprised, then a good deal more than surprised. He opened one big hand and the hearth-light came up in it, low and gold and absolutely steady, throwing shadows up the beautiful pale walls. "In here. Right in the belly of it. It's the same as it was at the waystation, exactly the same, not a flicker in it." He looked around at the city, and his plain face did something complicated. "This whole place is stitched out of that cold written stuff. Every stone we've passed. And it hasn't touched me. Whatever I answer to, it isn't in the room — it's a long way off, minding roofs and roads and hearths, and it can hear me perfectly well from here." He closed his hand. "I thought I'd come in here and go out like a candle. I've been afraid of it for two days."

"It's not on the same current," Oscar said slowly. He was looking at the priest with a feeling he could not have named except that it was the first good feeling he'd had since the valley. "It never was. Different river entirely. Which means — " and this was the piece, this was the thing to keep, he could feel it clicking into a slot he'd been holding open, "— this place has an answer for everything it can read, Cael, and it cannot read you. Everything else in my crew this city can look at and price. Your fire is the one light in Everset that isn't written in its book."

"That's a comfort," Cael said, "or a target."

"Both," said Tarn.

Wick, who had been silent for two streets with her hand jammed in her pocket, said: "Eight."

They all turned.

She was standing very straight and her eyes had gone wide and fixed the way an animal's do when it hears something above the range of everyone else in the room, and she was pointing, without seeming to know she was pointing, up the long slope of the beautiful streets toward the crown of the city.

"Eight," she said again. "It's not the town. It's *up there.* Whatever's up there, the wall's practically not even — " She stopped. Swallowed. Got herself back with a physical effort Oscar could see. "It's thinnest where that is. It's thinnest right where we're going."

---

They went up.

He could give a good account of the streets afterward and no account at all of the last hundred yards, because at the last hundred yards the working stopped hiding what it was for.

The city opened into a plain of pale stone, and at the center of the plain the deep current came up out of the world.

There was no way to make it small. It rose in a slow standing column of cold written light, higher than the towers, silent, and it did not flicker or flare; it simply *stood* there being more power than Oscar had believed the world contained, the substrate of everything, the cold under-thing he had spent his whole borrowed life brushing at with his fingertips — hauled up into the open air and put to work. Around its base was the basin. The font. Not a spring at all: a great shallow ring of that same unjointed stone, and the light came up through it and spread out over the whole city in threads too fine to see unless you knew to look, and Oscar knew to look, and once he had looked he could not stop seeing it. Every street. Every lamp. Every clean crack with no weeds in it. All of it hanging off this.

*It reads the place and strikes out what doesn't belong.* Ash had told him what waited and he had made himself a picture, and the picture had been far too small, because he had been imagining a monster and this was a *utility.*

His knees wanted to fold. He locked them and stayed standing, and the horror came up through his body first and arrived at his mind about a second later carrying a small tidy card that read: *this is what you were fetched home for, and it is not even angry with you.*

"Oscar," Kessa said. She had her back to him and Doss's knife out and low, and her voice had gone very flat. "There's someone at the water."

There was someone at the water.

A figure had come out around the curve of the basin, walking without haste, and stopped at the rim on the near side, perhaps forty paces off. It did not shout. It did not summon guards. It stood with its hands folded in front of it and its head tipped very slightly, and it looked at them across the pale stone with the patience of a thing that had been standing in that spot on and off for a very long time and had no particular objection to standing there a while longer.

Oscar's gift went out to it before he could stop it, pure reflex, the oldest thing he owned.

And it came back *full.*

That was the wrongness. Under all the other wrongnesses tonight, that was the one, and it dropped through him like cold water. A living body gave his looking nothing. That was the rule, the whole rule, the one his whole life was built on. He had read a man's coat and never the man. But this thing at the water's edge came back to him *made* — worked, written, composed, a thing with an author and a grammar and a beginning — and the grammar was in a hand he had spent a month reading by firelight, and the shape of the argument was one he could have finished himself.

He did not understand what he was looking at. His body understood perfectly and tried to take him backward a step; he stopped it, mostly.

"You're late," said the figure at the font.

Its voice was pleasant and unhurried and carried across the stone without effort, and it was not the voice of a monster, or a king, or a god. It was the voice of a clerk who has kept an appointment open past closing as a courtesy.

"They've been sending for you," it said, "for a very long time. Come in off the stone. There's no call to stand about in the wind."

And then, mildly, as though it were the most ordinary observation in the world. And it was. It *was* ordinary, and Oscar would carry the ordinariness of it a great deal further than any threat:

"You'll want to be finished. Everybody does, at the end."
