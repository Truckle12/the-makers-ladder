## Chapter Thirty-Eight — Evermere Remade

"No," Oscar said, "thank you," which was not what he had meant to say and was, on reflection, the most honest thing he'd managed in a season.

The figure at the font's rim inclined its head as though he'd remarked on the weather. Up close (it had not moved, and yet the distance had somehow become less, which he did not like) it resolved into a person of no particular age in plain grey, hands folded, hair the colour of old paper. Nothing about it was monstrous. Everything about it was *finished:* no fidget, no shift of weight, no breath taken for the sake of breathing. A body being worn correctly by nobody in particular.

His gift was still full of it, and the fullness would not stop being wrong.

His hands, he noticed, were warm. He noticed it the way you notice an absence: at the first font the mere nearness had rimed him to the elbows, the unfinished edges of him lifting away like frost off the walls. Nothing lifted now. The crooked Setting had held all this way. Whatever stood at this basin tonight, it was whole enough to be asked.

"You're reading me," it observed. "Go on. It's not rude. There's no one in here to take offence."

He should not have. He did. He put his looking on it properly, the way he'd been taught by a book he now understood far too well, and the thing at the water's edge opened to him like a page laid flat.

It was made.

Not *possessed,* not worn like a coat by something else. **Made**, from the ground up, composed and written and set into a body the same as a figure is set into a working. And the hand that had done the composing was the hand in the grimoire against his ribs; the hand that was, in every particular of slope and flourish and private economy, *his own.*

Two of his facts touched each other and made a third, and his knees went.

He didn't fall. Tarn's fist closed on the back of his coat and held him up without comment, and Oscar stood there in the cold standing light with the water-noise of the font in his ears and let his body have the first ten seconds, because his body had understood immediately and his mind was still going through the workings with a shaking pen.

"You're one of me," he said.

"I'm one of *us,*" the Setter agreed. "You're the eleventh. I was the fourth. There have been others between, and none of them arrived, which is why you were expensive." It said all of this in exactly the tone a clerk uses to explain the fee schedule. "You've come a very long way and you're wet through. I'd offer you something hot, but the household part of this was closed a long time ago and I have not thought to reopen it. That's a lapse. I apologize for it."

"The *fourth,*" Oscar said.

"Yes."

Its gaze went, without hurry, to the squared weight under Oscar's coat, the way a clerk's goes to a ledger carried into the wrong office.

"You found the third's book," it said. "Good. The third meant it to be found. The third was the one of us who left things — figures, refinements, a better way to take a stroke — cached where a later one of us might wake and need them. It was against the design, and nobody understood what it was for in time to stop it." A pause with nothing in it. "You will have taken the hand for your own. It is. It is all of ours. The pen does not change between us; only what it writes. I burned my copybooks when they finished me. It seemed tidy."

"And you've been standing at this basin since."

"Since I was finished." It considered. "I couldn't say how long in years. I have the number somewhere. Time is one of the things that stopped being interesting."

---

Behind him, Kessa said, low and flat: "Oscar. Is it going to move?"

"Not yet," he said, because he could read that much off it and because the alternative was to keep listening to his own pulse. "It's not — Kessa, it isn't waiting to attack us. It's *on duty.*"

"On duty," she repeated.

"He has it exactly," said the Setter, pleased, and that was the moment the horror properly arrived, because *pleased* was a thing it could still do, a little, badly, like a man clapping with one hand. "Someone must tend the font. Someone must perform the Setting when a candidate comes home. I do those things. I have done them for the whole of my — " a pause; not for effect. A pause where a word should have been and wasn't. "I do those things," it finished.

Oscar heard the gap. He heard exactly what size it was.

And he asked the question he had crossed five hundred miles to ask, and he asked it badly, out of order.

"Who made us?"

"I was told, once."

"And?"

"I no longer have the part that keeps things," the Setter said.

It said it without self-pity, because self-pity requires a self, and Oscar stood very still while the worst news of his life arrived in the shape of an administrative regret.

Oscar, who had been alive for one season and had inherited a long dead man's certainty about nearly everything, did what he always did with a fact he could not stand: tried to put a fence round it.

"How long, then. How many. Give me a number with an edge on it."

"A number," the Setter said. "I had it for a long while. I noticed I was carrying it about for no reason, so I put it down."

The floor went out from under the arithmetic. He had been telling himself a story in which this had been going on for a few lifetimes — bad, but a shape a man could hold. It was however long it takes a patient thing to stop finding the count worth keeping, and Oscar's mind put its hands up and declined to guess.

"Understand the design," it went on. "Setting *takes.* That is the whole of what setting is: the thing is fixed in its final form, and what is fixed cannot also be *asking.* The man who was told the name of the hand behind it went out of me the day I was completed, and what was left is what you see, and what you see is adequate to the work." A small movement, hands unfolding and refolding. "I keep the basin. I set the candidates. I am a very good instrument and I was, they tell me, a promising person."

Then it did the worst thing it had done yet, which was to be helpful.

"But nothing that went out of me is *gone,* eleventh. It went where everything goes: into the office. The name of the hand. The count of us. The why of the seat, and the whole ledger of the line back to its first stroke. A seat keeps what its instruments cannot; that is what a seat is for." The folded hands opened, indicating, without theater, the chair. "Sit, and be finished, and you will know everything you crossed this country to ask me."

Oscar stood very still, because the shape of it had finally come all the way clear. Every question he had was filed behind the one door he could not open and still be the man who was asking.

And it was bait he could feel *working.*

"Then *sit it,*" Oscar said. His voice came out cracked and too loud, ringing off the stone. "The seat. Everything here is built to put a master in a chair and you have been standing beside it since before anybody now breathing was born — why in all the world doesn't the fourth copy just take the job?"

For the first time, the Setter looked at him with something that was almost attention.

"Because there's no one in me to sit it," it said. "You've seen the flaw and you don't know yet that you've seen it. They can make a copy and finish it, and what they get is *this*: a thing that runs the machine and cannot be the master, because being the master requires somebody in the room who *wants* something." It turned its hands palm-up, an oddly human gesture badly copied. "Eleven of us. Each better than the last at the parts they can measure. And every one that has been set has come out the same: excellent, obedient, and nobody. They are getting better at us. They have not yet got a *whole* one home."

Oscar's mouth was dry. "And I'm — "

"You woke early," the Setter said. "Unset. Out in the cold with a self in you, walking about, arguing with people, acquiring a girl and a priest and a debt. Do you understand what you are? You are the first candidate in the whole run of this that arrived with anyone *inside.*" It said this as a technician says *this one has the tolerances.* "That is why the paper went out. That is why they spent a finder like Corwen on you and did not care what he cost. Not because you are dangerous. Because you might be *sufficient.*"

"The House," Oscar said. It came out with the whole road in it: the assay stamps, the writs, the cordon, a tallyman's scarred smile, a country that said the word the way it said the weather. "There is no House, is there. Not the way they mean it downriver. There's the line, and the line's estate, and clerks enough to run it. Everything with a loop on it, from the ice to this floor — paper drawn on this room."

"On the seat," the Setter said, mildly. "I only work here."

---

He had to move. He turned away from the font and walked, badly, down the side aisle to the thing he had been refusing to look at since he came through the doors: a low dark well-head, rough and unlovely, standing in a bay of that flawless pale stone like a bad tooth. The Fair House had not been built over it. The Fair House had been built *around* it, carefully, at a respectful distance, the way you build around a grave.

"Oscar," Cael said, starting after him.

"Give me the — " He didn't finish. He got to the well-head and put both hands on it.

It was cold and rough and the wrong colour for this city, dark where everything here was pale, and there was a worn groove in the coping where a rope had run for a great many years.

He knew the groove.

That was the sound his mind made going over: not a thought, a *sound,* somewhere behind the ears. He knew the groove because he had drawn water in a bucket up that groove on cold mornings, hundreds of mornings, with larches going grey-green in the wind past the wall and the sea a long slate line beyond them, and the cold coming up off the water, and a woman calling something from a doorway that he was late for. He had never done any of that. He had never been here. The man he was copied from had done it, and had put it in him, and Oscar had been carrying that morning around in his chest for a season and calling it *Evermere* and knowing it belonged to no *when* at all.

He turned around slowly with his hands still on the stone.

And read the city.

Not the working this time. The *stone.* He put his looking under the elegant continuous figure of Everset, past the clauses, down into the material, and asked the only question a man of his trade ever really asks, which is: *what were you before?*

The answer came up out of the whole valley at once.

*The Larchwall.*

Oscar made a sound. He heard himself make it and had no control over it whatsoever. Cael got an arm around him and he leaned on it, unashamed, because his legs had simply stopped being interested in the work.

"It's Evermere," he said. "This is Evermere."

"The dead place?" Kessa. Somewhere very far away, six feet off. "Your home's five hundred miles north, you said it was under the ice — "

"No. No, that's — that's the *other* one, that's where they woke me, they woke me in a cradle in a dead city and I have spent every day since assuming my home was buried somewhere and *lost.*" He was laughing, which was appalling; he made it stop. "It isn't lost. It isn't buried. That's why it didn't *feel* like home when we walked in — I was braced for the shape of it, and the shape is gone. They didn't keep the shape. They kept the stone. They took it *down.* They took my home apart, Kessa, stone by stone, and they melted it and *cast it again.* This stone, this beautiful smooth stone with no joints in it, it has no joints because it was never laid, it was *poured.* They broke up the Larchwall and they re-set it as this." He was pointing now, at the bridges, the colonnades, the lamps; his hand was shaking so hard he put it down. "The word is *font.* I told you the word was font and I thought I was being clever about the type. It's the same word. You break the old letters up and you throw them in the font and you *cast them again into whatever you want to say next.* They did it to a city. They did it to a *man.* They did it to me. Everything in this valley is the same crime performed at three different sizes and I am standing in the middle of it holding the third one's notebook."

Nobody spoke. Water ran in the channels, keeping the beautiful clean streets beautiful and clean.

"And the point of it," Oscar said, "the *use* of it." And he turned back toward the pale column and the small grey figure at its rim, and the last piece went into the last hole with a click he felt in his teeth. "It isn't a machine that happens to stand where my home was." He would not take the tidy word for it. "They broke my home up and threw it in the font and cast *this* out of the pieces. That's the joke. The line's own house, re-cast as the thing that strikes out whatever doesn't belong. And then the heir comes back to it. Of course he does. Where else would he go? He walks in, and the place he remembers is the machine, and the machine is what finishes him."

"Yes," said the Setter, with what a kinder man would have called relief. "You're quick. The fourth was not quick. I remember being told so."

---

He would remember the next part imperfectly, and he would be grateful for that.

He remembered Wick, very small and very white, saying "nine, it's nine, it's still nine" to nobody, and holding a grey rock in her fist so hard her knuckles had gone the colour of the city. He remembered Kessa going round the wall counting exits and finding none she liked and telling him so plainly, because she had promised to keep telling him the truth even when the truth was *nothing here is any good.* He remembered Tarn saying, to no one, "So the debt was always to a building," and being unable to work out what that meant and never asking.

And he remembered Cael, of all people, walking out across that pale floor toward the thing at the font with his empty hands open, and asking it — plainly, in the voice he used at bedsides — whether it had ever been happy.

"I was, I'm told, a promising person," said the Setter.

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," it agreed. And then, after a moment: "I don't have the part that keeps things."

Cael stood there a long while. When he came back his face had gone grey and his warmth had come up in both hands without his seeming to notice, gold and steady in a city of cold light, his thumb moving on the iron at his throat the way another man's lips move at prayer, and he said to Oscar, low: "That is the worst thing I have ever stood next to. It isn't a monster. It's a *bereavement.*"

"I know," Oscar said.

"It's you," Cael said. "It's you if you'd come home on time."

"I *know,*" Oscar said.

---

The Setter waited until they were done, because it had nothing but time and nothing to spend it on.

Then it said, "Shall we begin?"

Oscar looked at it. The column of the deep current stood behind it, going up past the galleries into a roof he could not see, holding a city's worth of striking-out in the air like a held breath, and the whole of it was one long sentence somebody had started a very long time ago and had been waiting, patiently, all this while, to put a full stop on.

*Come and be fitted.*

"Before I answer that," Oscar said, "I want to ask you one more thing, and I'd like you to think about it, if there's anything left in there that thinks."

"Ask."

"When I say no." He found, to his enormous surprise, that his voice had stopped shaking. "Not *if.* When I say no, and you have to make me — is any part of you going to be sorry?"

The silence went on a beat too long for a machine.

"I don't know," said the Setter.

And then, without haste, with no anger anywhere in it, it unfolded its hands and reached for the font, and the standing light bent toward Oscar Alberich like a lamp turned to a page.
