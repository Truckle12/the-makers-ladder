## Chapter Twenty-Nine — The Gate That Walks

They came out of the wild like men wading up out of deep water, and for a hundred steps Oscar let himself believe the hard part was behind them.

He knew better. He always knew better; the trouble was that knowing never arrived in time to stop the feeling that came first. And the feeling was enormous, and he was too rested to argue with it. Which was strange. Days ago he had stood at the rim of the grey with his looking shut and his hands empty and no idea whether he had the thing the moment asked of him. Since then he had slept. The wild had fed him back into himself over the long walk out, quiet mile by quiet mile, until the man stepping over the last of the edge-stones was whole again, and full, and — he caught the word and disliked it — content. A wrung-out crew at his back. Doss still an empty seat among them. Wick on her own feet because she'd sworn never to be carried out of anything again. And Oscar in the middle of it, content.

He should have read the contentment for what it was. A thing that full has a reason to be full, and out here the reasons were seldom kind.

---

The road did him no favors, because it was a real road.

It shocked him, that. After days of the wild's paths, which were not paths, which were the wild agreeing to be walked on for as long as it felt like agreeing, here was a graded southern road with wheel-ruts and a broken milestone and the dried print of an ox's foot pressed into old mud. An honest made thing that went where it claimed to go. He could read it without trying: the grade cut by a work-gang two generations back, the stone quarried north and hauled down, the whole patient grammar of a country that built things to last and expected to collect on them. He'd missed it. He was appalled to find he'd missed it. Weeks off the map, and some clerk-shaped part of him had gone hungry for a surface that stayed where you put it.

"Ground's true again," Kessa said.

She'd crouched at the verge without his seeing her do it, two fingers spread in the dust, her head tipped in the listening set she got. In the grey the ground had lied to her: trails run cold, tracks doubled into nothing, down not where down should be, and she'd hated it more than any of them, because the world refusing to hold still was the one insult her whole life had no answer for. Now the dust told her the truth, and he watched her shoulders come down half an inch. She read the road the same as she read everything, for footing, for who'd passed, for where a body could go and where it couldn't.

"Cart came through yesterday. Heavy, southbound. Two mules." She stood, wiping her fingers. "No one on foot since the rain. We've got clean road and open sky." She almost smiled, which for Kessa was weather. "I'll take it."

And there was the error, laid down so quietly that not one of them heard it fall. She read the road for what was on it. None of them, Oscar included, thought to read it for what it *was.*

Cael had stopped a few paces on, big and still, his broad face lifted south. He wasn't looking at anything in particular. He was a shade better than he'd been in days.

"You feel that?" he said, to no one, to the country. "There's a town down there. Not close. But it's there." He pressed the heel of his hand to his sternum, the old gesture, the place he reached down through to call the warmth up. In the deep wood he'd rationed it to two, and then done, reaching back and back down a hall that only grew longer the farther from people he went. Here the hall was shortening. Oscar saw it work in him: the warmth coming easier, the well filling from somewhere south and lit. "First time in weeks I can feel the other end of it," Cael said, and rolled his shoulders like a man shrugging into a dry coat. "Didn't know how cold I'd got till it started coming back."

Oscar noted it, and shouldn't have relaxed at it, and did.

Wick walked at his elbow, which she'd done since the hollow, closer than she used to and pretending she wasn't. The heat had come back into her on the walk out — the coal climbing again, her color up, the mark at her throat gone from that terrible snow-coin white to its ordinary sullen red. She looked half-asleep on her feet and entirely unwilling to admit it.

"When we hit a town," Oscar said, "I'm going to sit in the hottest bath in the south until I've forgotten I have a body. Then I'm going to eat something that was never a root. And then —"

"You don't even like baths," Wick said. "You said washing's for people who think the dirt's the problem."

"I've grown as a person."

"You've grown a smell." She didn't sell it; she was too tired, and it came out flat, and he let it die, because a joke that dies is still better company than the quiet had been. She hitched her shoulder at the empty country ahead. "It's too open," she said, lower, and that wasn't a kid complaining. "I don't like it. In the wood you could put your back on something."

"Tarn said as much at the treeline."

"Tarn's right a lot."

Tarn was twenty paces up, where he'd put himself without being asked, walking the point he'd held all the way out. He didn't like the open either; Oscar had learned to read that much of him, the set of the shoulders, the head that kept turning the road over as though it were a debt owed him. A wild-fetcher's habit. In the deep country you were the only hunter that mattered. On an open southern road you were a small figure a long way from cover, and Tarn's forty years had taught him which of the two kept a man breathing.

"Don't like leaving the trees at our backs," Tarn said when Oscar came up level. He didn't look around. "In the wood, a thing wants you, it's got to come find you. Out here" — he tipped his chin at the wide bright emptiness rolling south — "out here a man only has to know where the road is. And wait on it."

"There's no one on the road. Kessa read it. No one's come this way since the rain."

"Aye. No one's come." Tarn walked a few steps. "That's the road." He finished the thought and stopped, and Oscar felt the small cold thing that always came after one of Tarn's finished thoughts, the sense of a line he'd read a half-beat too late.

Because Kessa had it now too.

She'd stopped again, a dozen steps on, and this time she wasn't crouching. She stood very straight in the middle of the true clean road, turning slow on her heel, reading it at last for the thing it was.

"It funnels," she said.

"What?"

"The country." Her voice had gone flat and quick, the voice she used when the tally came out wrong and there was no time to check the sum. "Look at the land, not the road. Ridge to the east, marsh to the west, the road threads the seam between. Anything coming down out of the north — off the map, out of the wild, wherever — comes out here. There's nowhere else the ground lets you walk." She turned to him, and he watched her arrive at it a breath behind him, the two of them a beat too slow, both of them fed and rested and stupid with open sky. "We didn't slip a cordon by going into the wild. We picked the one gap it never bothered to watch. It only had to watch where the gap came out."

---

The figure was already there.

He would not forgive himself that, later. Not that they'd walked into it. That he'd looked straight at the man for three full seconds and read him wrong.

There was a figure on the road ahead, where the seam between ridge and marsh pinched tightest, and Oscar's first rested, content read was: traveler. A lone southern gentleman in a good grey coat, standing beside a plain cart, unhurried, a man waiting for someone with the ease of a person who has never once in his life had to run. Ordinary. His gift gave him nothing off the man himself — the living always returned him silence — so he used his eyes, and his eyes, lazy with rest, said merchant.

Then the man lifted his hat, a small courteous nothing of a gesture, and Oscar's stomach dropped through the true clean road, because he read the hat before he read the fear. It was the tipped hat from Kessa's story, the civil hand, the finder who bought the house a round and left a note in good paper sealed with a private man's wax.

"Corwen," Oscar said.

He didn't hurry. Kessa had promised them that in a taphouse a lifetime north — a man who's certain doesn't hurry — and here it was, made flesh on a road. The finder came a few steps toward them and stopped, well out of any reach, and let the distance do his walking, because he had all the distance in the world and none of the doubt.

"You made good time out of the north," Corwen said. His voice was pleasant and carried without effort. "I confess I hadn't looked for the wild. That was resourceful. I don't say so often." He took his own count of them, five figures on a road, reading past the total to the single line that concerned him. His gaze moved over Wick, Cael, Tarn, Kessa with a mild disinterest worse than any threat, and settled on Oscar, and did not sharpen. There was the horror of it. Sarn had wanted him. Hauk had priced him. This man only meant to collect him, the same as winter means to come, with no more heat in it than that.

"The rest of you are not on my paper," Corwen said to the crew, unhurried. "I'll say it once, because I said it in the note and I keep my word. Part from him and go home. No one follows you. I bear you nothing." His eyes went, briefly, to Wick. "The girl especially. I've no line for you at all, and a long road ahead that doesn't run through me. I'd not have you waste it here." He glanced at the sky as though remembering an errand. "I did say I'd have wine. I hadn't thought to pour it on a road. But the wild is the wild, and one adjusts."

Oscar opened his mouth to answer him, to buy a breath, because clever was the one tool he never ran dry of. And Corwen, still not hurrying, lifted two fingers off the head of his cane and set the working loose.

Oscar felt it before he understood it, which was the wild's one lasting lesson: the body first, the sense second. Cold closed on his wrists. Cold laid a line across his throat, precise as a tailor's chalk. His breath came up short against something he couldn't see. Then his gift caught up and told him what had him, and for once the thing had him by the right handle. It was *made.* It was worked. It was the finest piece of writing he'd read since the buried city, and he could read every stroke of it. A finder's binding. A loop-mark grown teeth. Not thrown at him blind but cut to his exact measure, set down by someone who had studied the grammar of Oscar Alberich from very far off and written, in cold clean strokes, a sentence that meant *stay.*

He reached for its seam to unpick it, the same as he'd unpicked a hundred lesser workings, and found the maker had been there before him. Every place he'd have begun, the binding had already closed. It was built by a hand that knew precisely how he would read it and had written the counter to his reading into the reading itself. He got a fingerhold on one loop, and it cost him more than he had loose, and a second loop drew tighter for the trying. He understood, with the flat clean despair of a man finally handed the right tool and told it will not be enough, that he was going to lose this. Not for want of skill. Because the sentence was better than he was, and it had been written for him, and Corwen had never once had to hurry.

Then the marsh stood up.

That was how it read, in the first half-second, before his eyes caught the sense of it: the pale reeds along the western verge came apart into men, four of them, low and mud-streaked, who had been lying in the wet since before the crew ever crested the rise. And the ridge shed two more down its scree. Six. Kessa's cordon, sprung all at once, and Oscar understood the cart for what it was — not conveyance, bait, a plain honest thing set on the road to hold the eye while the trap lay in the reeds. Corwen hadn't come to fight them. He'd come to *stand there* while the fight happened somewhere he didn't need to watch.

Oscar's gift gave him nothing off the men and everything off their kit. House-issue, all of it. The cudgels weren't clubs; they were made, each one carrying a small mean loop-mark worked into the head, the kind of writing that let a blow land heavier than the arm behind it. Retainer-work. Licensed. He read a House brand pressed into a strap and it told him these were not thugs off a wharf but the ordinary muscle of an institution that did this often enough to have a supplier.

"*Reeds,*" Kessa said, one word, and the crew was already moving on it because a word from Kessa in that voice was an order.

Tarn met the first man before he cleared the verge.

Forty years in the bowl and all of it in the hands. He didn't reach for a weapon; his hands were the weapon, and he'd learned in a place where losing a fight meant not eating that the whole art was in the half-step. He gave up the ground he was standing on, took the man's rush at the one angle that turned it into nothing, and put a short brutal thing into the join of the jaw that dropped the House hand where he stood, cudgel and loop-mark and all, unused. The second came in high with the mark-heavy club already swinging, and this one Tarn couldn't turn, only wear — he got a shoulder up and the blow came down worse than a blow that size had any business being, the loop-work adding a foot of drop that wasn't there, and Oscar heard the meat of it, heard Tarn grunt low through his teeth. The arm sagged. Tarn used the sag. He let the club carry the man past him and hooked a heel and put an elbow into the back of a skull on the way down, workmanlike, no heat in it, a man doing the job he'd always been for.

But there were four more, and Tarn was one man, and the cold clean loop across Oscar's throat was cinching tighter every second he had no answer for it.

Cael's warmth came up like a wall.

He'd got in front of Kessa and Wick, both hands out and down, and the gold came *ready* — here was the reversal the wild had promised, the well filling unrationed with a town somewhere warm at the end of the shortening hall — and where in the deep wood he'd have spent everything for a flicker, here he built a full ward, a curved warm nothing between the crew and the reeds, and a House hand ran his shin into it and folded like the air had teeth. It cost Cael, even here. Oscar saw it cost him. But it cost him one of many instead of the last of nothing, and Cael planted his boots and held the line and shouted, hoarse, "*Go — I've got the west —*"

Two of the ridge men came around behind him for Kessa.

She didn't fight them so much as read them off the ground. Oscar watched her do the thing she did, the thing that wasn't magic and was worth more than most of it: she saw the footing before the men were on it, saw that the scree behind her ran loose and downhill and that a man committed to a downhill rush can't stop being committed. She gave the first one the downhill. Stepped inside his line, guided the shoulder, and let the loose scree finish him — he went past her and down and kept going, a long ugly slide into the mud with the marsh at the bottom of it. The second she took with Doss's knife, short and low and without any ceremony at all, a working cut, not a killing one, a slice across the meat of the arm that made the club drop and the man reconsider, and she was already off him, already back at the sum, already turning to the only calculation that mattered.

"*Oscar.*" She wasn't coming for him. He watched her run it and hate it: six down to three standing, Tarn bleeding from the shoulder, Cael's ward eating what it could, and past all of them the finder who hadn't moved a boot, who'd let his kit take the punishment because his kit was a thing he could replace and his time was not a thing he'd spend. "The paper," she said, fast, to the whole crew and to Oscar most of all. "He doesn't care if we kill these. He's got more. He *always* has more. And I can't cut what's on your neck." Her eyes flicked to the binding, to the thing she had no read for. "We can win this and still lose you. If we stay, we all stay. You hear me. We fall back, we take him somewhere the ground's ours, and we come back for you whole. That's the only sum that closes."

"Then *go,*" Oscar got out. The loop had his throat now; the word came thin.

Wick would not move.

She'd got herself between the reeds and Oscar without deciding to, small and lit and shaking, the coal up so hard the air around her had gone to shimmer, and she was staring at the cold sentence written across Oscar's throat like she'd found the one thing in the world she was finally allowed to burn.

"Let him go." Her voice cracked down the middle. "He's ours. You don't get him, he's my —" and she didn't say the word, the one she used in her own head and never out loud, but Oscar heard the shape of it and it undid him worse than the binding did. "I'll burn it off him. Oscar, I can burn it, tell me I can burn it —"

"You can't." It was the cruelest true thing he had, and he gave it to her flat, because a kind lie would get her killed. "It's made for the maker's reasons, not yours. Fire feeds it. Wick — *don't.*" And then, because Corwen was watching the girl with that mild collecting interest, weighing whether the one not on his paper was about to make herself a line on it: "Go with Kessa. That's the job. You want to help me, you stay you, and you stay alive, and you come get me. Halfway and home. You hear me? *Come get me.*"

She screamed something at him that tore as it came, no words left in it, and Kessa got both arms around her and hauled, and Cael dragged his ward with them like a man carrying a door on his back, and Tarn came off the last standing hand and covered the withdrawal the same as he'd covered the point all the way out, last off, walking backward, one arm hanging wrong, his eyes on Corwen and not on the men, because the men were finished and Corwen was the fight and Corwen had never entered it. The crew went off the road into the ridge-scrub, the only direction the country left them, up into the loose grey stone with the wounded arm and the wrung well and the girl still fighting the arms that held her. Oscar lost sight of them fast, which Corwen had surely meant: split the herd off, keep the goods, and don't so much as raise your voice while it happens.

"There," Corwen said, mild, as though a small chore were done. He came on without hurry, drawing a second square of folded paper from his coat, and Oscar had the wild irrelevant thought that the man meant to make him sign for himself. "It needn't have been loud. I did ask." He looked, once, without much in it, at the two men Tarn had dropped and the one Kessa had put in the marsh. "I'll bring more of them next time. It's what they're for."

Oscar read the paper, because reading was the one door of his that never shut, and nothing else in reach was worth spending on. Good paper. Private wax, unbroken. And set into the wax, fine as a hair, a made mark that was not the House's. He knew the House's brand on sight; it was on half the paper in Marrow, a fat certain stamp that said *licensed* the same as a boot says *floor.* This was other work. Older-fashioned, cut deep and close, a small cold figure that did not explain itself and had plainly never needed to. Corwen carried it like Cael carried the shield. Not a tool. A thing he answered to.

"Whose mark?" Oscar managed. The loop at his throat gave him about that much.

"Not mine to open," Corwen said, and it was the first thing he'd said all day that sounded like the bottom of anything. He turned the paper once in his fingers, incurious. "I fetch what the paper names, whole, and I hand it up to the seat that wants it. What the seat does with what it wants back isn't in my line, and men in my trade who ask don't stay in it." He came on. "You'll find out before I will. I wouldn't hurry toward it, in your place. But then you won't be hurrying anywhere."

He didn't finish reaching.

The light changed. Oscar had no better word for it, and he was a man who prided himself on better words. Between one of Corwen's unhurried steps and the next, the road acquired a person who had not walked onto it. She was simply *there,* at his shoulder, a woman he had never seen, near enough to touch, with the settled ease of someone who had been standing there all along and was mildly surprised no one had thought to look.

Corwen looked. For the first time in the whole encounter something moved in the finder's face. Not fear. Something nearer to a man revising a certainty he'd have staked his career on.

"You," he said.

"Me," the woman agreed.

She glanced at the cold sentence across Oscar's throat — the once, the glance of someone who had seen the type before and had never lost sleep over it — and whatever she made of it she didn't say. She looked at Oscar next, quick and thorough and unreadable, taking her own count of him, and if there was anything in her face past a professional's interest, he was in no state to catch it.

"This is going to be strange," she told him. "Don't fight it. You'll want to. You're the type."

"Who are —"

She took his bound wrist in one hand, almost gently, and the world creased.

That was the only word he would ever get to keep from it. The road *creased,* like a page folded once across the middle, and Corwen and the cart and the ridge and the far thin screaming in the scrub all began to slide down into the fold. His gift threw itself at the folding to read how it was done — reflex, the only prayer he owned — and closed on nothing, or on something already finished; he would spend a long time failing to decide which.

The fold came up over the sky and took the light.

Then it took the road out from under him.

Then it took him too.
