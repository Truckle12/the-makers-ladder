## Chapter Eighteen — South

Marrow tried to give them everything it owned, which was the problem with freeing a town: it wanted to thank you, and it had only just remembered it was allowed to want things at all.

For three days the gifts came up the steps of the boarding house in a steady, embarrassed flood. Salvagers who had nothing pressed nothing on them anyway: a knife, a coil of good rope, a child's drawing of four stick figures standing on a dead monster. A scale-clerk who two days before would not have met Oscar's eye now wept and tried to give him a coat off his own back. The widows of the good claim came in a quiet line and left things on the sill without a word: a carved button, a twist of dried meat, a single coin worn smooth from being held. Oscar took almost none of it, and what he took he took because Cael caught his eye and made the small patient face that meant *let them, it's for them and not for you,* and Cael, in matters of mercy, was almost never wrong.

What they could not refuse was Brida.

She found them on the morning of the fourth day, when the packs were filled and the light was coming grey and cold up the long valley and there was nothing left to do but go. Her bad arm was strapped tight—the day in the square had cost it—but she carried a bundle anyway, balanced against her hip, and she set it down on the step between them with the finality of a woman who has decided a thing and will not be argued out of it.

"Cloaks," she said. "Southern cut. Off the haul you handed back to this town, so don't you dare tell me you can't take them." She pulled the wrap open. Wool, oiled and dense, the deep dull green of country Oscar had never seen. "You'll freeze in northern leathers the first week and you'll be marked as northerners the first hour, and down there the second one's worse than the first." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry now, all the weeping spent in the square. "There. Now you look like people instead of refugees."

"Brida—"

"I'm not coming, before you ask, and you weren't going to ask, so don't pretend." A flicker of the old humor. "This town doesn't know how to be free yet. Somebody has to teach it before it sells itself to the next clever hand that comes up the valley promising to take the danger off its books. That's me. I'm the somebody." She said it plainly, but Oscar saw what it had cost her to find—a use for the rage she'd carried four winters, a thing to do with it besides bleed. "I buried a husband in that deep on a lie. I'm going to spend what's left of me making sure nobody buries another one the same way."

Wick, who had said almost nothing in three days, stepped forward and hugged her, fierce and wordless, as she did everything that mattered. Brida held her one-armed and looked over the girl's head at Oscar, and her face changed.

"Listen to me," she said, low, while Wick had her face hidden. "I don't know what you are. I told you I don't care, and that's true. But I know the south, a little—I was born three valleys down before I came up here chasing a man and a wage. And I know how being owed works down there, and you should hear it from someone who paid." She waited until he was looking at her. "Up here, the Tithe owned this town with fear. Cord and debt and a monster in the dark. It was ugly. Down south they don't bother with the ugliness. Down south they're *polite.* They smile, and the paper is in order, and the man who comes to collect what you owe brings good wine and asks after your children by name, and you don't even understand you've been taken until you wake up one morning and find you belong to somebody." Her grip tightened on his sleeve. "Whatever's hunting you—and something is, I'm not a fool—it won't come at you like Sarn did. It'll come kind. The kinder it gets, the closer it is. Don't you forget that."

"The only way out of being owed," Brida said, releasing him, stepping back, "is straight through the ones who hold the paper. There's no running it off. I know. I tried for four years." She managed a smile, crooked and real. "So go south, marked man—go to Everset. That's where the House sits, and where the paper's kept, and where the answer to whatever you are is written down in somebody's ledger. Go find whoever thinks they own you, look them in the eye, and don't you dare be polite back."

Then she turned and went down the steps into her town, and did not look back.

Something had turned over in him at the name, though, and stayed turned.

It was nothing—a word from a woman born three valleys south of a town he'd only just woken under, a place he had no possible reason to know. And yet for half a breath *Everset* had sat in him like a key tried in a lock a single tumbler off. He'd have sworn he had never heard the word in his life. He found, turning it over, that he couldn't quite swear it after all. He filed the feeling away with the slanting hand in the grimoire—and said nothing, because there was nothing to say about it that wouldn't have sounded mad.

They went south within the hour.

---

The land changed slowly, and then all at once.

For the first two days the north did not let go. The valley narrowed and rose and the cold rode their shoulders, and the bones of the old world still broke through the snow at the roadside: a span of fluted pillar, a stretch of road so old and straight it could only have been laid by the makers, half-swallowed by ice and going nowhere now. They walked the maker-road where they could; it ran south, dead level, indifferent to the centuries. He had woken at the bottom of a city these hands had built. The road was the same hands, reaching south. He kept catching himself wondering whether some buried version of him had walked it once.

On the third day the cold began, grudgingly, to lose.

It came off by degrees—a south-facing slope where the snow had gone to slush, a stream running open and loud instead of locked under ice, the wind softening until it merely chilled instead of cut. And then, on the fourth afternoon, Wick stopped dead in the middle of the road and pointed and said, "*What is that.*"

It was a tree.

A living one—not the grey dead spars that stood in the north like the masts of drowned ships, but a true tree, low and broad and stubborn, its bare branches furred all over with tight red buds that meant, Oscar realized with a strange small lurch, that somewhere ahead of them it was nearly spring. Wick walked to it like a person approaching something holy or dangerous and put one hand flat against the bark, and stood there, and did not say anything at all.

"It's just a tree, Wick," Kessa said, but not unkindly, and even she had slowed to look.

"It's *alive,*" Wick said, which was the whole of it, and pressed her ear to the trunk as if she might hear it.

Cael laughed—the first real laugh Oscar had heard from him since the square—and tipped his head back to the thin warm sun. "The hearth keeps a fire even where you can't see the chimney," he said, which was a thing his faith said, and which Oscar had always privately found a little smug, and which today, with the buds red against the grey sky and the girl with her ear to the bark, he could not bring himself to argue with.

They camped under the tree that night because Wick asked, and because none of them quite wanted to leave it.

---

It was at that fire, while the others slept, that Oscar took out the grimoire and did the thing that frightened him.

He had been doing it most nights since Marrow, in the last watch, alone—taking the heavy old book onto his knees and opening it to the figures he had not yet learned and making himself look. It was the only school he had. Whatever he was, he had woken with the bones of this craft already in him and most of the meat missing, and the grimoire was meat: figure after figure, patient and exact, more than he could learn in a year, set down in a slanting careful hand.

A hand he knew.

That was the part that frightened him, and the part he had told no one. He knew the hand the way you know your own gait in a shadow on a wall before you understand you're looking at yourself. The slope of it. The way the writer crossed certain strokes and not others, the small impatient hook at the end of a long figure as if even setting it down they'd already moved on to the next clever thing. He had spent four nights trying to decide whether he was imagining it, and had stopped trying, because the truth had arrived quietly and would not leave: he could have written this book. The hand was *his.* Or it belonged to whoever he had been copied from, which his mind kept insisting was the same thing and his gut kept insisting, with rising dread, was not.

He learned from it anyway. There wasn't another choice, and refusing to grow because the schoolbook was haunted struck him as exactly the sort of luxury a hunted man didn't have.

Tonight he made himself study the figure he wanted most and could not yet hold—a guard, a snap of written force meant to be thrown up between you and a blow in the half-instant you saw it coming. He could read every stroke of it. He could not yet *make* it; he'd tried, twice, on the road, and both times the figure had come apart in his hands and left him gasping and cold, his reserve all but empty. New figures cost. The binding he'd scored in the square had cost him badly and that one had only had to grip three men's boots. A figure fast enough to catch a falling sword would cost more, and he was not strong enough yet to pay it, and he knew he would need it before he was ready. He filed it where he'd filed the lance once, in the long buried tower, when it had failed him too: *not yet. Soon. Keep at it.*

Then he set the book aside and did the other thing, the thing that was his and not borrowed from any haunted hand.

He looked.

It was getting easier. The gift he'd half-distrusted as a parlor trick was *sharpening,* like a muscle that finds its strength only once you're made to use it every day. In the buried city, looking had shown him seams: how a thing was made, where it would give. He turned it on the tree now, eyes half-closed. The tree gave him nothing, the same as every living thing had ever given him. Bark and bud and the warm shut fact of it; no edge to take hold of, no rule written down, nothing made in it for him to read. He'd stopped resenting that a while ago. But the looking didn't die where the tree turned it away. It went *past* the tree—down, below the roots and below the road, to the cold under-thing that ran beneath everything standing in the world: the deep current, the Script that the living and the made were alike set down upon. He grazed it, a fingertip's worth, careful as a man stepping onto ice he didn't trust. And where a season ago he'd have caught only a faint cold pressure and lost it, tonight he held it clean and deep for a long quiet moment before he let it go. He could read the page the tree stood written on, and he read it better tonight than he had a month ago.

He kept that too, the fierce private joy of it, the same as in the square.

The last thing he did before he woke Kessa for her watch was reach south.

He didn't decide to. He never quite decided to; it just happened, the way you test a sore tooth with your tongue. Somewhere down the dark valley, fainter than a sound and surer than a thought, the bond was there—the old thread that ran out of him and away, the absence shaped like a thing that had known every version of him there had ever been. *Quill.* It was nearer than it had been in Marrow. South. Always south, as if the bird and the answers and the danger were all the same direction.

He reached for it as you'd reach for a hand in the dark.

And couldn't close it. The thread was there and his fingers passed through it; he was not whole enough, or strong enough, or near enough, and the failure had a texture he was learning to recognize and hate—a name on the tip of a tongue that wasn't quite his. *Not yet,* the same as the guard-figure, the same as everything. *Soon. Keep at it.* He let the thread go before the wanting could turn into something that kept him awake, and he woke Kessa, and he slept under the first living tree he had ever seen, and dreamed of a careful slanting hand setting down figure after figure, patiently, for someone exactly like him to find.

---

The first sign came at a place called the Throat.

It was a waystation, the first real one they'd struck—a low stone inn squatting where the valley pinched between two cliffs, the only sheltered crossing for a day in either direction, the kind of chokepoint that grows an inn the way a wound grows a scab. Oscar would have walked around it if there'd been a way around it, and there wasn't, which was rather the point of a place called the Throat.

They went in with the green cloaks up and Oscar's loop-marked sword wrapped in sacking and his silver hair under a hood, and for an hour it was only what it looked like: warmth, and watered stew, and the low murmur of travelers who'd learned not to ask each other questions. Cael bought a round with town-gift coin. Wick fell asleep sitting up with her hand in her pocket. It was almost pleasant, and Oscar had almost let his shoulders down, when the innkeep set the second pitcher down and said, easy and conversational, like a man remarking on the weather:

"You'd be the third lot through this week asking south. Busy road, for the back end of winter." A pause, wiping a cup. "Course, you're the first that's been *asked after.*"

Oscar kept his face still. Across the table Kessa did not look up from her stew, which was how he knew she'd gone to stone too.

"Asked after," Oscar said, mild.

"Gentleman came through. Six days back, going south same as you." The innkeep was enjoying having something to say, in the way of men who watch the same valley for a living. "Polite sort. Southern, by his coat—good cloth, no swagger. Paid for his bed in proper minted coin, not salvage scrip, which you don't see up here, so I marked him." He set the cup down. "Bought everyone a drink. Asked his questions friendly, like a man asking after a lost cousin. Whether anyone'd seen a fellow coming down out of the north. Tall. Silver hair—proper silver, he said, not old-man grey. Young face under it. Carrying an old sword with a mark on it like a knot tied in a circle." The innkeep mimed it in the air with one finger, a loop, and Oscar felt the cold open under his ribs as it had when Sarn smiled. "Said he'd pay well for word. Said he wasn't any trouble to the man—just owed him something, and meant to put it right. Left coin with me against the chance you came through." A shrug, almost apologetic. "I'll not lie to you, friend. It's good coin."

The room had gone very quiet at their table, though the rest of the Throat murmured on, oblivious.

"And what did you tell him," Oscar said, "this polite gentleman."

"That I see a hundred faces a week and remember none of them." The innkeep met his eye, and there was something old and dry and faintly amused in it. "Which is true, mostly. And which is what I'll tell the next one that asks, and the one after, because a man who watches a chokepoint for thirty years learns the difference between a fellow owed something and a fellow *hunting* something, and that one was hunting, however soft he asked it." He picked the cup back up. "I don't sell people to hunters. Bad for the road. Word gets out, folk stop stopping." He turned to go, then paused. "But I'm the only innkeep on this stretch with that particular failing, and there's a lot of road south, and he's six days ahead of you and asking at every door. Whatever you owe him—settle it or outrun it. You'll not hide from it. He's already in front of you."

He went back to his bar, and left them with it.

Wick had woken. Cael had set down his cup. Nobody spoke for a moment.

"Someone's asking after me by name," Oscar said finally, keeping it light because the alternative was not. "Six days ahead, on the road we have to walk, paying good coin for my face. Brida said it would come kind." He turned his cup on the table. "She didn't mention it would come *early.*"

"Then we go careful," Kessa said. Flat. Already turning the bad fact into a route, he could see it. "Off the main road where we can. No chokepoints we can't help. And we find out who this polite bastard is before he finds us."

"Or," Wick said, small and worn and not entirely joking, "we just stop being on a road he's standing on."

"One road south, kid," Kessa said. "He's on it."

Oscar pulled his hood down lower and let it lie there. There would be time later to wonder who the polite man was, and how he'd come armed with a face he could only have gotten from someone who already knew it. Tonight it was enough to know the shape was out there: somewhere south, on the only road there was, pouring strangers a friendly drink and waiting, in no hurry at all, to hear about a silver-haired man.
