## Chapter Forty-Two — Set Aside

The city didn't fall down.

Kessa had expected it to. She had stood on that plain with her palm burning and watched the light go out of the ring, and some part of her that had spent two days reading a town built out of murder had assumed that when the murder stopped, the town would go with it. It seemed only fair. A thing held up by a working ought to come down when the working goes.

It didn't. The lamps on the bridges stayed lit. The water kept running in the cut channels. Somewhere off east a bell went, twice, the way bells go in any large place at any hour, and a dog barked, and the beautiful pale streets of Everset lay there in the dark being beautiful, and did not know.

She stood at the rim of a dry basin and put the count together.

Cael on his back on the stone with Tarn's coat under his head, breathing, grey, out cold. Tarn on his heels beside him, unhurt, waiting for something to lift. Oscar out in the middle of the empty ring on his knees with his hands flat on a slab of pale stone.

Three.

*Four,* she thought. *Four with me. And that is going to be the count every time I do it for the rest of my life.*

She had done it wrong twice. Once with Doss and once in a grey hollow, and both times she had told herself the ground had lied to her, and both times it hadn't; she had just read it late. Tonight the ground had told her the truth from the first street. *No rats. No weeds. Nothing living in it that nobody invited.* She had read it right, said it out loud, and walked her people into it anyway, because there had been no other line of country and she had known that too.

She looked at her palm. It was a hand's width of ruin across the middle, already going tight, and she was going to lose the use of it for a month and never lose the shape.

Good, she thought. Then I won't have to remember on purpose.

---

The eleven clerks were still there.

That was the part nobody had a plan for. They had stood about on the pavement all night with broken sticks in their hands, waiting, and when the ring went dark they had gone on waiting, because whatever had been in the middle of them telling them what belonged was gone and nothing had come to replace it. Kessa walked a slow circuit and read them the way she'd read a herd: no drift, no interest, no aggression. One of them had turned its head to follow her and then forgotten to.

"Do we kill them," Tarn said, when she came back.

"Can't. He said so." She crouched. "And there's nothing to kill. They're a pen with no hand."

"They'll stand there."

"They'll stand there." She watched them a moment longer. "Somebody's going to have to work out what this town does with eleven of those, and it isn't going to be us, and I want to be over the hill before anybody decides we're the reason."

Tarn grunted. It was the closest thing to agreement he did.

Then, without looking at her, in exactly the voice he used for money: "I was his. The debt. Since the bowl." He nodded once at Oscar, thirty feet out in the empty basin. "It's hers now."

Kessa did not ask what that meant. She knew what it meant. It meant that if there was a road, Tarn was on it.

"Marked," she said.

---

She had to go out and get Oscar, in the end, because he wasn't going to come.

The basin was dry. That was the strangest walk of her life, eleven paces over a floor that had been coming apart under a girl two hours ago and was now just stone, whole, cold, faintly warm in patches, with dust already settling on it. The gaps had closed. She looked for them and couldn't find where they'd been.

He was kneeling on the slab with both hands flat and the owl hunched on his shoulder out of the wind, and he was reading. She could tell because of his face, which went somewhere else when he did it.

"Oscar."

"It's dry," he said. "I want you to understand what that means, because I've been sitting here for an hour and a half getting it wrong and I've only just got it right. It isn't broken. Nothing's broken. There's nothing *left.*" He turned one hand over. "This whole valley is one working and the working is still here, all of it, every clause, perfectly written, running on what's in the pipes. It's a mill with the river gone. The lamps will burn until they don't. The channels will run until they don't. And then in a year, or five, or forty, there'll be weeds in the cracks of the most beautiful city anybody ever built, and rats in the granaries, and nobody alive here will know why it went wrong."

"That's not the worst news I've had tonight."

"No." He almost laughed. It came out badly. "No, I suppose it isn't. It's — Kessa, it's *good* news, and I can't make it be good news, and I would like it noted that I have been trying."

She let that stand. Then she said the thing she'd come out to say.

"Get up."

"In a minute."

"Now. Cael's cold and I'm not carrying two of you."

He got up. It took him three goes and she let him do it himself, because there are men you help and men you insult by helping, and she'd learned which he was on a road outside a burned village a long way north.

Then he saw the hollow, and went for it.

She got there first. She was faster than him on her worst day and this was not her worst day; she put her boot on the slab beside the little grey disc and bent and took it up before his hand was halfway there.

It weighed nothing. It felt like nothing. She'd handled a hundred stones in a hundred stream-beds and every one of them had told her something — where it came from, what water had done to it, whether it had been shaped. This one told her not one single thing, and after tonight that was the most valuable property she had ever held in her hand.

"Kessa."

"She said don't lose it." Kessa put it in her inside pocket, the one with the flap, the one that had carried Doss's whetstone for two years and nothing since. "She said it looking at me."

Oscar stood there with his hand still half out.

"She said it looking at *all* of us."

"Then it's decided by who's got a pocket," Kessa said, "and I've got the pocket."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him arrive at the fact that she wasn't going to argue and there was nothing in it to win. And then something in his shoulders let go about a quarter of an inch, and she understood, with a small sick pity, that he was *relieved.*

He'd been kneeling out here an hour and a half looking for a thing to hold and had been frightened of being given it.

"Right," he said.

---

Cael woke a little before dawn and was no use to anybody for an hour, and then he was Cael again, which was worse.

She would have preferred him broken a while longer. Broken men are easy; you feed them and put them where the wind isn't. What he did instead was sit up, and get his bearings, and ask after the four of them one at a time, and take her hand and turn it over and look at the burn for a while without saying anything, and then reach for the pot and heat water for it with his own two ordinary hands, because his warmth had gone all the way down and there was nothing in the cup at all.

"It'll come back," he said, of the fire, when she looked. "Give me a week and a hearth. It always comes back." His big face didn't move much. "It came when I called it in there. Every time I asked. Right in the belly of that thing, it came, and it kept coming, and I had to be the one that ran out." He put her hand down, very carefully, as if it belonged to somebody he owed. "That's a hard piece of news to carry, Kessa. That the fire had plenty and the fault was the man."

"You held four people together in a room that was taking their names," Kessa said. "Say the rest of it out loud if you want, but don't say that part again. It's a lie and I heard the truth with my own ears."

Cael was quiet.

"There's a thing I say," he said at last. "Over people. You've heard it."

"Dozens of times."

"I couldn't say it. Last night. She was standing right there and I opened my mouth and there was nothing in me at all, and she — " His voice went. He waited it out. "She told me it was all right. She *let me off.* Sixteen years old, going out on that stone, and she looked at me and gave me my absolution instead of the other way about."

"That sounds like her."

"It sounds exactly like her." He wiped his face with the flat of his hand, once, businesslike. "It's come to me now. The words. Sat up out of the dark about ten minutes ago whole and easy, the way they always do." A short ugly breath. "Ten hours late. And there's nobody to say them over. She isn't *dead,* Kessa, that's the whole trouble, I've got a benediction and no ground to put it in."

Kessa thought about it. She was not a woman who did much with words; she read ground, and there was ground here, and the reading was plain.

"Say it over the stone," she said. "Not for her. She'd hate it. Say it for you, and I'll stand for it, and then we'll go and get her and you can be embarrassed about it later."

So he did. He went out into the dry basin at first light, and Tarn went with him and stood off two paces with his hands hanging, and Oscar came out last and stayed at the rim because he could not make himself walk that eleven again, and Cael put his hand flat on the seat-stone and said the three plain lines he'd said over Doss and over a woman in a burned village whose name he never learned.

Kessa stood for it. She had never once in her life wanted that particular thing, and she wanted it now, and she made a note of the change without examining it.

---

The gate was open when they came down, and there was a queue.

That was the last blow and none of them had braced for it. Carts. A woman with a basket. Two boys arguing about a sack. Ordinary people waiting their ordinary turn, filing under a forty-foot arch of white stone that had struck a man out of the world in four heartbeats the previous morning — and it did nothing. They passed under it and paused out of habit and went on through, and nobody so much as looked up, because nothing had happened, because nothing was ever going to be told to them.

Kessa stood in the dust off the road and watched about forty strangers not know.

"That's them saved, then," Tarn said.

"That's them saved."

"They'll never hear it."

"No."

Tarn considered the queue. "Best kind of debt," he said. "One nobody knows they've got. Can't be collected. Can't be argued over." And then, being Tarn: "Doesn't pay, either."

She went and found the street.

She didn't tell the others why and none of them asked, because she had spent this whole road teaching them not to ask what she was reading until she was done. It took her two turns of the lower town and one wrong guess, and then she was standing in a lane of pale houses with washing across it, reading the ground as she'd read a hundred yards of thin country, and there was nothing to read at all: no scar, no ash, no gap. The street was exactly a street. Whatever had come off it had come off clean, the way everything in this town came off clean.

There was a bucket standing in a doorway with the rope coiled beside it.

She stood there a while. Down the lane a shutter went up and a woman leaned out and shook something and went back in, and Kessa put the man with the bucket into the column that was supposed to make the night bearable, and stood there, and found the column didn't add. It never had. She'd known that since Doss. You cannot set a stranger's morning against a girl and get a number that means anything; the two things aren't the same kind of thing and never will be, and the only people who tell you otherwise have not yet lost anybody.

She did not knock. There was nothing to say to him that wouldn't be a kind of collecting.

She went back and got her people.

---

They took a room over a bakehouse on the low side of town, on Tarn's money, which turned out to exist, because of course it did.

It was the first roof any of them had been under since the waystation, and it was warm from the ovens below, and it smelled so much of bread that Kessa's eyes stung the moment she came up the stairs and she was furious about it. There was a table. Tarn went out and came back with two loaves still too hot to hold and set them down, and broke one, and the crust cracked all the way along with a sound like a small door opening.

Oscar looked at it.

"Eat," Cael said.

"In a minute."

"Wizard," Tarn said, "eat the bread."

So he tore a piece off and put it in his mouth and chewed it and swallowed it and sat there with his hand over his eyes, and Kessa read it off him plain: he had said something about bread on that plain last night, to the thing that was going to finish him, as his reason for staying a person. She hadn't caught what. She caught it now, watching him fail to enjoy it, and she looked away and let him have it, because there is a kind of watching that is theft.

Then she put the token on the table between the loaves.

"Right," she said. "Sums."

---

"Tell me what we know," she said, "and then tell me what we don't, and don't dress either of them."

Oscar took his hand off his eyes. It was the first thing all morning that had got anything out of him, which she had counted on.

"We know somebody has been making me for three hundred years," he said. "Eleven of us. Numbered. He said I was the eleventh and he was the fourth, and *he* was made by the same hand that wrote the book against my ribs, which is my hand, which is the hand of the man they cut me off. We know they take a copy home to Everset and finish it, and finishing it empties it, and an empty one can work the machine and can't be the master, which is why they keep making more. We know they wanted a whole one and they thought I might be it." He turned his cup around without drinking from it. "We know that whoever set all this running had a reason to want a man in that chair forever, and I do not know what the reason is, and I would like to be extremely clear that I am not going to guess, because I have spent this entire road guessing and it has cost us Kessa's hand and a girl."

"What don't we know."

"His name." Flat. "The fourth was told it once. They took the part of him that keeps things when they set him — that's what setting *is* — so the only man alive who could have told me who made me had the answer burned out of him three hundred years before I woke up, and stood next to a basin holding the hole where it used to be, and apologized to me for the lapse." He was very steady about it, and she didn't trust the steadiness at all. "There's one other person who might have it. She poured me tea, gave me a bird, told me exactly as much as she'd decided to, and walked out of a manse into open country, and I have no more idea where she is than you do."

"Will she come back?"

"She'll come back when it suits her and not one hour before."

"Then she's not a plan," Kessa said, and moved on, because unfindable people go at the bottom of the list. "The bird."

Oscar's mouth did something complicated.

Quill was on the back of the other chair, which was where Quill had been since the plain, and had been there when Kessa woke twice in the night to check the count. It had killed something in the small hours and eaten it noisily and stared at all of them while it did.

"It comes when I call it," he said. "Every time. I've tried it four times since the ring went out and I'm ashamed of every one of them, because I wasn't calling it for a reason, I was calling it to see whether it would still — " He stopped. "It doesn't mind. That's the thing I can't get past. It plainly doesn't mind being asked. It seems, as far as I can read a bird, which is not at all, to be *fond of me*, and I have absolutely no idea what to do with that."

"Get used to it," Cael said.

"I'm trying. I've been a made thing since I woke up under ice and it turns out a made thing being loved on purpose is a great deal harder than a made thing being hunted." He rubbed his face. "I'll manage. It's on the list."

"It's not on the list," Kessa said. "It's the *reason* for the list. Keep up." She tapped the table once beside the grey disc. "Now the last thing. Her."

Nobody spoke.

"I'll say it, then," she said. "Taken is not dead."

"Kessa — "

"No, you'll hear the reading and then you can pick holes in it, that's the arrangement and it's worked since the heath." She put one finger on the token. "I stood at that rim and I watched. She wasn't taken *apart.* She wasn't struck out, and I have now seen a man struck out and I know exactly what it looks like and it does not look like that. She went *somewhere.* Something opened, and she went through it, and it shut. That's not dying, that's *travelling,* and travelling has two ends."

"You don't know it has ground," Oscar said, quietly.

"No. But everything I have ever walked on has had ground under it, and I'm not going to start assuming otherwise to make myself miserable early." She left her finger where it was. "And there's this. She put it down on purpose. She had about a minute left in the world and she spent part of it setting a rock in a hollow and telling us not to lose it. That's not sentiment. She was sixteen and frightened out of her life and she stopped to *hand us a thing.*"

Oscar looked at the disc for a long moment.

"It reads as nothing," he said slowly. "That is its entire property. It's the one object I've ever put my looking on that comes back with a flat blank, which is why I ignored it for a season like an idiot. And she worked out at the gate that the thing behind her door couldn't find it either. Same blankness. Same — " he made a small helpless movement with one hand, which from him was the equivalent of shouting, "— *kind.* Kessa, it's the same kind of nothing that she is now. It came out of a dead claim on this side of the world and it does not belong to this side of the world, and I have been sitting in a dry basin for two hours trying to make that mean something and I can't do it yet."

"Yet," Kessa said.

"Yet."

"Then that's the job." She sat back. "You'll learn to read it. You've got a dead man's workbook full of clever handwriting and you've got years, and you can go and be insufferable at every hedge-witch and licensed reader between here and the ice until one of them tells you something useful. Cael goes home to a hearth and gets his fire back, because we'll need it and because he's owed it. Tarn drinks. I find out where the far edge of this country is and what's past it and who's ever come back from anywhere they shouldn't have gone, and I will do that on foot, which is what I'm for."

"And then?" Cael said.

"And then we go and get her."

She said it the way she said *there's water at the third bend,* because it was that sort of fact and she was not going to have it turned into a feeling on this of all mornings. It landed in the room anyway. She let it.

"It might take years," Oscar said.

"Then it takes years."

"It might not be possible."

"Then we'll find that out at the end of a great deal of walking, and not from this chair." She looked at him until he looked back. "You want to know what I've got, Oscar? Because you keep waiting for me to say something clever and I'm not going to. Here it is. Ground talks. Everything is somewhere. Every somewhere has an edge, and every edge has been crossed by something, and I have never once in my life failed to find a way in — I've only ever failed to like the way. That's it. That's the whole of what I've got and it's held so far."

"It's held," Oscar said. And then, with the first thing in his voice all morning that wasn't broken: "Then I pity whatever's on the other side of that door."

---

They were three days getting out of Everset, because Cael couldn't walk far and nobody said so.

On the second evening Kessa was up on the flat roof of the bakehouse, doing what she did, which was reading the country she was about to be in: the road west, the ridge lines, the two passes and which would still be open, the way the light fell so she'd know it again from the far side. Below her the beautiful city went about its evening. The lamps came on along the bridges. The water ran in the channels. Nobody had told them anything and nobody was going to.

Something moved on the parapet six feet away.

She went still out of pure habit, and looked, and it was a rat.

A perfectly ordinary rat, wet-nosed, filthy, disgraceful, sitting up on the coping of a building in the cleanest city in the world with its whiskers going. It looked at her. She looked at it. It decided she was uninteresting and went along the parapet and down a drainpipe and out of her life.

Kessa put both hands on the coping — the burned one hurt and she made it hurt — and put her head down and made a sound she had not made since Doss, and got through it in under a minute, because that was what she had, and then she wiped her face and finished reading the passes.

The rats were coming back.

*You'd have liked that,* she thought. *You'd have said something disgusting about it.*

---

They went out through the west gate at dawn, four of them and a bird, past a queue of people who did not know and never would.

At the last rise before the road turned, Kessa stopped and looked back, because you always look back; you fix the shape of the place you're leaving so you can find it again in the dark. Everset lay in its green valley being the most civilized thing she had ever seen, with a dry ring at the middle of it and eleven grey figures standing about on the pale stone waiting for an instruction that was not going to come.

She put her hand in the inside pocket with the flap and felt the little cold nothing sitting there where Doss's whetstone used to be.

Then she turned around and gave her crew the count, out loud, first, not after — the way they had done it every hundred paces on the road down into this valley, because a girl had needed the practice, and then because they all did.

"Five," Kessa said.

Nobody moved.

"Four here," she said. "One out. Five."

There was a pause about three heartbeats long, and it was the longest of her life.

"Marked," said Tarn.

"Marked," said Cael.

Oscar Alberich stood in the road with an owl on his shoulder and a dead man's book against his ribs and his home behind him, unfinished, refusing to be finished, and he got it out on the second try.

"Marked."
