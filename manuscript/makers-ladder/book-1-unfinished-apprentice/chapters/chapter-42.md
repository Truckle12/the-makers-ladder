## Chapter Forty-Two — The Far Side

Wick woke up cold.

It took her a while to get past that. She'd been too hot her whole life. She was the warm one, the one you didn't sit next to in a barn, and she was lying on her back with her arms out and her fingers open and she was *cold.* Plain cold. Cold like a person. For about ten heartbeats it was the best thing that had ever happened to her.

Then she remembered.

She put a hand flat on her chest and went looking for the coal.

Nothing. Not banked. Not low. She'd had it since before she could talk and she'd never once had to go looking for it, and now there was nowhere to look. Her hand just lay there on her shirt like anybody's hand.

"Right," she said, out loud, to nobody. Her voice came out thin. "Right. Okay."

She got up, because lying there wasn't going to be any warmer.

---

There was ground. It held her. There was a down and so there was an up, and there was a sort of light, low and grey and coming from nowhere in particular.

There was no sky. She looked for a long time. Then she stopped, because it was making her feel sick.

The ground was the worst bit. It wasn't stone and it wasn't dirt. When she crouched and put her palm on it, it wasn't *anything,* and she knew that feeling, she'd had it in her fist for weeks: the little grey rock had felt exactly like this. You pressed and nothing pressed back. It went out flat as far as she could see, which wasn't far, and it wasn't mist stopping her seeing.

Her ears kept lying. They kept saying *huge* — the sound a barn has in the dark before you strike the light. But when she said "hello" the word went about four feet and stopped, like she'd said it into a coat.

She didn't say hello again.

Her stomach did its thing. She told it there wasn't anything. It didn't believe her.

---

It was here.

She'd known that with her eyes still shut. You know when somebody's in the house.

Not in front of her, not behind. She gave up on *where* fairly fast. It was just near. The thing. Near and full. She could feel it being full, like a stove through a wall. And it was pleased with her. She sat down with her arms round her knees. It was *pleased with her.*

Not grateful. She knew grateful. People had been grateful at her before and there was always something after it. This was a dog that had got at the ham and wasn't sorry.

"You ate the thing that was eating that city," she told it. Her voice shook. She made it keep going. "That's what that was. Biggest meal anything ever got. And I gave it you. *Me.* So don't act like you did that."

It didn't say anything. It never had. It didn't need to.

But something in the grey leaned at her, glad.

And the ground under her leaned with it.

She took her hand off it. She took the whole of herself off it, up onto her feet, and there wasn't anywhere else to put her feet.

There wasn't any ground. There never had been. She'd been sitting on it.

"Yeah," Wick said. Her voice came out very small. "Well. I'm not staying."

The wall was back, too. She couldn't see it or touch it. In the Fair House there'd been no wall, ten, nothing between her and it but her, and then she'd felt the whole place fold shut round her like a fist. The lid was back on the pot. She was in the pot.

She reached for her pocket, for the rock, because that was what her hand did when it was all too much, and the pocket was empty. She stood there with two fingers in it.

"Course," she said. "Course it is."

---

It hadn't eaten her.

It took her a while to get to that, sitting there. She wasn't quick like Oscar. She got there anyway.

It could have. It had come through her like a dog through a gap in a hedge and it had eaten a whole engine and gone back, and it had *not eaten her.* It had never wanted her. It wanted the door. She was just what the door was in.

You don't eat the door. You keep the door.

She put her hand on her chest again, and there it was. Not the coal. The other thing. The shut place. Her door, right where it had always been, behind her ribs, closed, and *hers.* Course it had come with her. Where else was it going to go.

And it wasn't cold.

Everything else in her was cold. Her hands, her feet, her face, the whole of her, cold for the first time in her life. And under her palm, at the shut place, right along the bottom edge of it, there was a thread of warm.

Like a draught under a door.

She knew. She didn't want to. All her life the door had faced this. Eight years with her back against it, keeping this out. And she was in this now. So what was on the other side of the door now was the world. The bread ovens and the pie stalls and the grandmother on her step and Cael's stupid enormous hands. All of it, an inch of her away, leaking warm under the door like a kitchen at night when you're the one sleeping in the yard. No sound in it. No smell. Just warm.

She put both hands over it and just breathed for a bit.

---

Then her hand moved.

She felt it start to do the thing, the letting-go, the thing she'd never done once in her life, because it was the obvious thing, wasn't it, you're cold and you're the wrong side of a door and it's warm in there, so you open it —

She stopped. She sat with both hands on her chest and her heart going and made herself be not stupid.

She'd never gone through it. Not once. Things came *in.* It was a mouth's door. If she opened it now she wouldn't go anywhere.

*It* would.

Through her, into the warm. One glad swallow. And it wouldn't stop at the pie stalls.

"No," Wick said.

And it asked. Straight off, sweet as anything, the same as every single night since she was eight, only from every side now, and taking its time about it, because it had her and it had nowhere to be. *Rest. Set it down. You've held it long enough. It's warm in there.*

It could feel the draught as well as she could. Better. So that was it. Not the meal. This. It had never once had a kitchen.

"No," she said again. She said it to the ground.

She'd always had a fire to say it with. Now she had nothing and a wet nose, and it'd be every night. Here. With the warm coming under the door the whole time.

She wiped it on her sleeve and got up.

---

There was one more thing, and she nearly missed it, because it was small and she was busy not crying.

Away off in the flat grey, a long way, in the direction her feet had already picked without asking her, there was a mark on the ground.

It took her a long time to walk to it. She couldn't have said how long. She counted steps and lost the count twice and then didn't bother.

It was a scuff.

That was all. A drag in the not-ground about as long as a boot, and another one after it, and a third. Then a fourth, further on, pointing away toward wherever it all stopped.

Somebody had walked here.

Bigger boots than hers. A lot bigger.

Wick stood over it with her heart going like a bird in a box, and there wasn't anything to work out, there was just the one enormous fact, and she said it in her head so it would stay put:

*Somebody got here before me. And they didn't stop.*

She looked back the way she'd come, once, at nothing.

And it was nothing. There wasn't a mark behind her. She'd walked a long way over that ground, she'd dragged her feet like a kid the whole last bit, and there wasn't one scuff of hers on it, all the way back to wherever she'd woken up. It hadn't kept one. It hadn't so much as noticed her.

She put her boot down next to the fourth scuff, hard, and made a fifth, and stood there and watched it.

It went. Not fast. Like breath going off a window. And then there was the ground not being anything, and the big scuffs pointing away across its back, and no fifth.

The big ones were still there.

She looked at them a long time. She didn't know why hers went and theirs didn't. But she knew what it looked like when something was being held. She'd been doing it since she was eight.

Somebody was holding those.

"Four," she said out loud. Kessa made her promise. "Four of them. Breathing. Mine."

Nothing answered.

All round her it was glad and it was full and it wasn't going anywhere. It was waiting for her to get tired.

Wick put her head down and went after the boots.
