## Chapter Thirty-Three — The Waystation

The waypost had good bones and Cael spent two days learning them with his hands.

It was old work, older than the House, older than any road he'd ever prayed beside. The walls were a single grey stone without joint or mortar, cool in the sun and never cold at night, and when he laid his palm flat against them he could feel a low steadiness in the rock, not alive, not warm, but *kept*. Oscar had gone around the whole place the first evening reading it aloud to anyone in earshot, drunk on it, calling it the finest weather-work he'd seen outside the buried city, and Cael had understood one word in five and agreed with all of them. The people who'd built it had meant travelers to live through the night. Whatever else the dead makers had done wrong in the world, a roadhouse that would not burn and would not crack was a thing you could respect with both hands.

He learned the doors best, because the doors would be his.

There were two of them, north and south, each a narrow slot a big man filled by standing in it. Good timber gone grey, dead-maker hinges that had never been oiled and never complained. The south door they barred and left barred. The north door was the door. That was Kessa's plan, and she'd walked every foot of it with them until each of them could have run it blind. If the finder wanted them, he had one approach and one door.

Cael warded the door on the second evening, and it came easy, and the ease of it sat in him strangely.

This far south, with farms in the next valley and a mill-town beyond, the hall inside him had gone short and warm. He laid the warding into the doorway slow and deep, layer on layer, banking it like a fire he meant to find alive in the morning, and when it was done the narrow slot of the north door hummed against his palm like a kettle two breaths from singing. It was good work, maybe the best he'd ever set.

"That'll stop a man?" Wick asked him, watching from the well-coping with her knees drawn up.

"It'll ask him to stop," Cael said. "Hard. A man leaning into it feels like the whole day's walk lands on him at once. Most men sit down." He flexed his hand, feeling the warmth bank itself. "It won't kill anybody. It's not for that."

"What if the man doesn't sit down?"

"Then he comes through tired," Cael said, "and the rest of us are on this side of it."

Wick looked at him for a while, and he could see the next question, the real one, standing behind her teeth. She didn't let it out. She'd been carrying it since the fire two nights back, the same as all of them: the vote had carried, him or us, and what that meant for whoever stood nearest when it stopped being words. Cael had been carrying it too. He was a big man; things landed on him first; he'd made his peace with that a long time ago, in the order of things he could make peace with.

He mended what there was to mend, that second evening. Tarn's shoulder he checked with both hands and found finished, the fibers lying quiet, work that would hold. He saw to a blister of Wick's, and then to a seam in his own boot with needle and waxed thread, nothing granted about it, and he did that in the doorway in the last light because his hands wanted plain work. The shield leaned against the jamb while he sewed. He'd scoured it that morning. He didn't usually scour it before trouble; it kept its own counsel, that shield, and he kept it fed with oil and let it be. But he'd sat that morning with it across his knees and worked the old strap-leather soft, and cleaned the rim back to bright metal, and not looked too closely at himself doing it.

It had failed the man who carried it before him. He'd never once blamed it. Shields don't fail. The shield had been there for the weight when it came. He set his palm flat on the boss, the old habit, and it was cool, and kept, like the walls.

"Tomorrow," Kessa said from the doorway. She'd been on the scarp-top at dusk and seen what she'd been waiting for: the last clean road, quiet, no carts and no drovers on it.

"Tomorrow," Cael agreed, and bit the thread, and put the needle away, and slept better than he had any right to, in the house the dead had built for travelers.

---

Corwen came at mid-morning, walking, with a score of men and no hurry in any of them.

Cael stood in the north door and watched them come up the old road, and his first feeling was pity, the one he'd been dreading, because he knew what it did to a man's arm later. They came on in loose order with cudgels and hooked staves and two crossbows, more than the work could need, and they were nobody, wage-men. He'd fed men like these at a hundred hearths — big-knuckled lads off the farms, debtors working a season for the House because the House held their paper, a grey-headed one who moved careful on a bad hip. Somebody's sons. Two of them were joking together, low, as men joke on the walk to work that might go wrong, and the joke died as the waypost got bigger in front of them.

Ahead of the score, in the good grey coat, walked the man himself, and about him Cael's dread found nothing to pity at all.

He'd heard the others on Corwen: Kessa's *certain,* Oscar's *apparatus,* the story the south told. Standing in the doorway with his ward humming at his back, Cael finally got his own look at the finder, by the body, and the body coming up the road was wrong in a way none of the stories had caught, like a man whose whole weight never commits to either foot. Corwen walked forty years old and moved older and stood like a hinge, and there was no fear anywhere in him, none, not the healthy animal fear every living body wears walking toward walls with armed people behind them. Cael had seen brave men. Brave men are afraid and walk anyway. This one wasn't afraid. Where the fear should have been there was a space, swept clean, like the walls, like something kept.

Corwen stopped forty paces out, past the reach of any bow but not by much, and lifted his hat to the doorway.

"The priest," he said, pleasant and carrying. "They put the warm one in the door. That's sound. I'd have done the same." His eyes went over the walls without hunger, a surveyor's pass. "This is a good room you've all chosen, and I want you to know I appreciate the shape of the thing. Truly. In a long career this is the first time the collection has set the table and waited for me, and I find it makes a pleasant change."

"You could turn around," Cael said. "That'd be a pleasant change too."

"It would," Corwen agreed, with what sounded like real consideration. "Here is my difficulty. Your wizard is on my paper, and my paper doesn't lapse, and I don't lose them. It isn't pride, understand. It's soundness. The country south of here works — the roads are safe, the writs are honored, the tithes feed the towns that pay them — because when the House writes a name, the name arrives. Every name that doesn't arrive costs more than itself; it teaches ten men the writ is weather, that they might be the eleventh who slips it. I've spent a life keeping that lesson untaught, and the country is quieter for it, and I'll not unlearn it here for five tired people in a dead man's roadhouse, however well they've chosen their walls." He resettled the hat. "The others still have my word. Stand out of the door, priest."

"No," Cael said. And because some stubborn hearth-keeping corner of him had to, he spoke plain to the score of them, over Corwen's head: "There's a fight in here you're being paid a day's wage to have. When it turns, and it will, any man who drops his stick and walks gets to keep walking. That's our word. Nobody follows you. Go home whole."

Some of them looked at each other, and that was worth something. The grey-headed one with the hip looked longest.

"There," Corwen said, mild as milk. "Everyone's terms are on the table. I do like a tidy start." And he set his cane into the road, and it began.

---

The first push was Kessa's, and it broke the way she'd built it to break.

Cael held the door and the door held. The rush came in two prongs — one at him, one at the barred south door with a ram-beam they'd cut on the walk — and both prongs paid. His ward took the leaders' momentum off them like a tide taking legs; men leaned into the doorway and sat down in it, weeping with a tiredness they had no name for, and Tarn's fists were waiting past the threshold for the ones who kept their feet, short work, mercifully short, men folding over their own middles and being hauled aside by their friends. The south prong got Oscar. He'd read the bar and the old frame for two days, and whatever he'd said to the dead-maker work in that wall, the wall had agreed to: the ram-beam met the door and the door did not so much as sound. The beam split on the third swing. Cael saw the crew that carried it stand looking at the pieces.

From the well-coping, held, aimed, Wick made the open ground expensive. She'd sat with Cael the night before and set her own terms, blunt as a hammer: *I won't cook anybody. Tell me where the line is and I'll hold the line.* The line was the strip of bare earth ten feet out from the walls, and she held it, heat without flame, a shimmer that made the air over the strip stand up and wobble, and the men who tried to cross to the blind corners met it and reeled back sunstruck, blistered-red across their cheekbones as if they'd worked a harvest week in a morning, and not one of them burned. Cael saw what it cost her to keep it that side of burning, the fire fighting her for its whole leash, her jaw set white and her eyes gone narrow with the load of it.

Three men dropped their sticks in the first pushes and walked. The grey-headed one was the first. Cael watched him go up the old road, not running, one hand pressed to his hip, and did not know he'd been holding that gladness clenched somewhere until it left him all at once, like heat.

None of it moved Corwen, because none of it was meant for him, and he wasn't in it.

He stood the whole time at forty paces with his hands folded on the cane, and he watched the door, only the door, not his men; he spent his men without looking at them. He watched the doorway, and the ward in it, and Cael standing in the ward, and it took Cael too long to understand what the finder was doing, because he'd thought Corwen was hunting a way through and Corwen was not. He was *counting.* Cael thought, cold, in the door: it won't break and he knows it. He's counting how many men it takes to bury it. And he's deciding he's got them.

"Kessa," Cael said, level, over his shoulder. "He's about to spend the rest."

Corwen lifted his cane.

It was not a working. It was a signal, and the whole body of them answered it at once — not the loose two-pronged testing of the first pushes, but all of them together, driven into the one narrow door in a single committed weight. Cael understood the shape of it. Corwen wasn't sending men through him. He was sending them to *drown* the ward. It took the first rank the same as it took everything, asked them to stop and they stopped, sat down weeping in the mud with a whole day's walk landing on them at once — and the rank behind climbed over the sitting men and were asked, and stopped, and the rank behind that climbed over those. Cael reached down the hall and renewed the ask, and reached and renewed it, warmth going out of him in gouts to keep it alive under all that meat, and the hall that had been so short and warm all morning began, under the load, to lengthen. There was a bottom to it. Near a town or far, a man could only ask so many bodies to lie down before the asking cost more than he had left. The ward did not break. It was buried — smothered under men handed a day's wage to put it out with their own falling — and the hum against Cael's back guttered, and the next rank came on across a threshold carpeted in the ones who'd stopped.

*He'll not break it, he'll bury it,* Oscar shouted from somewhere behind, raw, *and he's not here for the door — he's here for me. Keep me off him.* And then the wizard stopped warning them and started telling a dead thing what to do, in the certain voice he used when he'd quit asking a made thing and begun to instruct it.

The waypost answered him.

Cael felt it come up through his boots before he understood it. Two days Oscar had walked these walls reading them aloud to no one, and Cael had taken it for a scholar's fidgeting. It had been a man learning the house. The dead makers had raised this place to keep travelers alive, and low in the old stone they had set a keeping into the threshold, asleep, and long forgotten, and *made*, and Oscar found the word for it and woke it. A cold that was not Cael's warmth and was not fire laid itself across the north door, hard as a held shield, and the rank coming on over the fallen met that and stopped as flesh does not stop, and a man went down over a wrist bent the wrong way.

It wasn't Oscar's. Cael understood that much even with a stave coming at his head — the wizard hadn't made it, he'd only known it, and asked a dead people to remember what their door was for. It was old stone work, and it did not tire as Cael's warmth had tired, and it did not sit down. But it was thin, and holding a keeping that had never been his put a grey strain in Oscar's face across the room. It held one door. The waypost had two.

Corwen did not turn a working on it. Cael watched him look at the woken keeping without heat, the same as he looked at everything, and decline to spend himself on a wall he couldn't talk down — and simply send more men. He fed the wage-men into the dead-makers' ward the same as he'd fed them into Cael's, careless which wall did the breaking of them, and while the old door held its rank the ones who found the barred south door and the well-coping came at those instead.

The seconds the keeping bought were real, and Kessa spent every one: Tarn up into the slot at Cael's shoulder, the well-line reset, Wick's heat banked and aimed, every hand set for the moment the wards gave and it came down to the door and the men who filled it. Oscar couldn't save the door. But he'd made the man who closed doors for a trade spend a second wall's worth of men to close this one, and even worn to that grey strain he'd bought them the time.

Then the old keeping thinned as even stone gives when enough bodies are piled against it, and the rush came through over both smothered wards, and it became what Cael had known since the vote it would become, which was bodies in a narrow place. He filled the door because that was his and the shield did what the shield was for. Weight came in on him in waves with men behind it. He broke a stave with the rim and felt the blow he gave back land wrong on someone's collarbone, felt it give, took no joy and no time from it. Beside him in the slot Tarn worked with terrible economy, no wasted arm, no anger, catching wrists and finishing balances the men didn't know they'd lost — until a hooked stave came over the top of a lunge and laid his forearm open near to the bone. He changed hands without a sound and kept working. Cael saw the blood going black to the wrist and could not reach him, and hated the door, for the first time in his life, for being his to hold and no one else's. Somewhere left, Kessa's voice ran the room, three words at a time. The well, Wick's heat cracking loose at last in one licensed roar as a man came over the coping at her, flame this time, off his sleeve and gone, the man rolling on the floor more scared than burnt, her voice shrieking something at him that was pure gutter and no part of it apologetic.

They were holding. Even with the ward struck out, the room was theirs, the door was one man wide and the man in it was Cael, and the wage-men had no more want for this work than they'd carried up the road. It was turning. He could feel it turn, the weight coming easier, the waves thinner. Two more dropped their sticks in the doorway itself, close enough that he took their cudgels out of their hands and let them squeeze out past his shield, and heard them run.

Corwen came through his own men's ebb like a man boarding through surf, and he came for Oscar, and he did not hurry even now.

Cael saw it in pieces, between blows, as a man sees a thing across a working room. The grey coat shrugging off Kessa's thrown knife — the coat *shrugging,* the cloth moving wrong, worked cloth, another made thing wearing the shape of an ordinary one. Oscar, back to the well-house wall, hands up and already reading, already talking, buying his half-second the only way he'd ever bought it. And the finder's hand coming out of the coat with something small and bright between the fingers, thread or wire or a line of light, and Cael, forty, wedged in a doorway under two men's weight with his shield arm singing, understood the whole rest of it before it happened, understood it in his body the same as you know a falling cup will break.

The collar had been cut to Oscar's measure once already. The man had had the days since to cut it better.

"Cael." Kessa's voice, from the beam-rack, pinned behind three men and a hooked stave, seeing what he saw. It wasn't an order, because there wasn't one to give. It was just his name.

Corwen's hand opened, almost kindly, and the bright thing went onto Oscar's raised wrist and climbed his arm, fast, wrist to elbow to shoulder, and Oscar's voice stopped mid-word. There was no cry. It just stopped. The wizard came off the wall rigid, arched on a scaffold of cold light that had already reached his throat, his eyes still moving, still working at it and getting nowhere, because the finder was holding it on him, standing composed at arm's length with two fingers raised and still. Cael watched Oscar's lips shape one word with the last of his breath, and the word was nothing clever at all. The word was *out,* aimed at Wick, at all of them. Get out.

Cael put the two men off his shield and left the door.

He was slow. He'd always been slow. He crossed the room at the pace the room allowed, one man's grasp broken over his forearm on the way, and the whole time some back shelf of him was reaching down the hall as he'd reached ten thousand times, for warmth, for the granted thing, for anything of his sworn trade that answered this. And it answered. The hall was short, the hearth was near, the warmth came willing into his hands, all of it, and none of it was any use. Warmth wards. Warmth mends. This was neither. Oscar had five breaths left in him, maybe, and was being let take none of them, and the only thing in the room that answered was the oldest thing he had, older than any vow.

His thumb found the iron at his throat on the way, the old touch. For the first time in his life there was no comfort in it.

"Put him down," Cael said, at the finder's shoulder, one last time, because he had to. "He's a person."

"He's a name," Corwen said, patient, not turning, two fingers holding his place. "They're rarely persons, this deep in the paper. You'd be surprised." And then, in the same mild bookkeeping voice, to the man at his shoulder: "You keep doors, priest. Your paper says so. *Wards. Mends. Buries other men's dead with their boots on. Does not kill.* Forty years of sound paper is why you're still standing there, and why I haven't turned around."

The shield had failed a person once, and been carried anyway, and fed with oil, and kept. It came around flat and full, with all of Cael behind it, the whole slow weight that things landed on first, and it caught the finder below the ear where the coat's cleverness didn't reach, and Cael felt it through his arm, all of it: the rim going in, the small bones behind the ear giving first and then the big one under them, the jolt coming back up into his shoulder. He'd had his hands on enough broken men to know what that was. He could have named every bone.

Corwen went down the way a coat slides off a chair.

The cold scaffold vanished from Oscar's body between one instant and the next, unheld, let go, and the wizard hit the floor breathing in whoops with Wick already over him, already shouting his name into his face. The last standing wage-men looked at the grey coat crumpled on the old stone, and at the big man over it with the shield still raised, and dropped everything, and were let go, all of them, up the road into the noon.

The room went very quiet, the kept walls holding the quiet as they held the cool.

Corwen was not dead yet. Cael knelt, because kneeling by the broken was the oldest habit he owned and it did not check first whose breaking it was. The finder's eyes were open and going. There was no fear in them even now, no anger, nothing arriving at the end to be met or eased or forgiven; Cael had knelt by many dyings and there had always, always been *someone* there to hold the door for, and here there was only the eyes, going, looking at him as they'd looked at the door.

"Sound work," Corwen said, or most of it, the consonants failing. And then, not to Cael, not to anyone, the eyes gone past them all toward some far standing desk: "Unfetched. Note it —"

The word finished him, or he finished with it. Cael watched the empty place where the fear should have been become the ordinary empty place, the one he knew, the one every body came to. There was no comfort in it. He put two fingers to the finder's eyes and closed them, because his hands would not agree to leave a dead man staring, whatever the man had been.

Under the coat's collar, against the breastbone, something cracked with a small dry sound like a seal breaking, and a thin grey smoke that was not smoke went up from the body and moved off south along the floor, low, purposeful, unhurried even now — and Oscar, still on his back, still whooping air, got one arm up and read it going, and what Cael saw on the wizard's face as he read was not triumph, and not relief, and Cael's own body understood before any word came, the falling cup again:

It had somewhere to report to.

"It's a term," Oscar rasped, to the ceiling, to the room, to the five of them alive in it. "The paper. His whole errand had a term written on it, like the house did. *In the event the fetching fails.*" A breath, whooped, spent on this instead of on being alive, because he was who he was: "It's gone to tell the seat we killed its hand."

Cael knelt on the old kept stone with a dead man's eyes closed under his fingers, in a room he had helped make safe, in a fight they had won, everyone breathing, everyone his, no one lost, what they'd built the walls for. He looked at his hands. They were steady. The warmth sat banked and willing in them still, unspent, unasked. Tarn's arm, open to the bone. Oscar's throat, whatever that thing had left in it. Wick's hands, from a morning of holding the line. Tarn's arm first. There was nothing on himself to mend, not a mark.

Then he got up, big and slow, and went to see about the living.
