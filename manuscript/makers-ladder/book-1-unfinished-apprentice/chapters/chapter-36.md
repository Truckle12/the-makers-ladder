## Chapter Thirty-Six — One Tumbler Off

They came up out of a fold of the thin country at midmorning, and Oscar looked south, and knew the place before he had any right to.

It was not a thing he decided. Knowing arrived the way nausea arrives, from underneath, with no argument attached: the far country resolved out of the haze — a long green valley, a river bending west of a low hill, a pale suggestion of towers where the hill crested — and something in the made part of him, the part that had been another man's before it was cobbled into this one, said *home* in a voice that was not asking his opinion. His stomach turned over. His feet stopped without his telling them to. And then, a beat behind the body as always, the clerk in him caught up and started, uselessly, to take notes.

He knew that hill. He knew the *bend* of that river, the particular westering of it, and how the light would come off the water at evening from the high cold halls above it. He had never in this life been within a hundred miles of here. He had woken under ice five hundred miles north with a stranger's memories packed into him like silt, and one of them was a coast road under larches and a walled town gone longer than the maps went back, a picture cut so clean out of a book that he'd stopped being able to feel its edges. *Evermere,* which he remembered and had never seen, which belonged to a *when* he couldn't name and a man he wasn't.

This was not Evermere.

This was one tumbler off.

He had been telling himself, since the market at Marrow, that place-names rhyme; that *Everset* wearing the bones of *Evermere* was the sort of coincidence a tired mind makes into a portent. He had told himself so on the road for weeks, patiently, the way you keep pressing a bruise to prove it's only a bruise. Standing here with the valley opening in front of him, the argument died in his mouth. The hill was the hill. The river bent the wrong way — no; the *right* way, the remembered way, and that was the wrongness of it, that a place he'd never stood should be so exactly the shape of the drawer where he kept a home that wasn't his. Someone had built this. Someone had taken the picture in his silt and *made it,* one tumbler off true, and set it down at the bottom of every road he'd walked since the ice.

"There it is," Kessa said, coming up on his shoulder, low. She was reading the valley as she read everything, for the ground of it — the approaches, the river as an obstacle, the towers as a place men could stand and watch a road. She did not feel what he felt; he could see it in her, the plain professional attention, and he envied it so hard his throat ached. "Two days. Maybe less if the going holds." She glanced at him, and he watched her not-ask the thing she'd been not-asking since the heath, the snag she'd clocked in him a dozen times and filed in whatever column she kept for things that didn't add up. "You've gone that colour again."

"I know this place," Oscar said. It came out flatter than he meant. "I've never been here and I know it, and I've spent three weeks pretending I didn't, and I'd like it noted for the record that pretending was the smart play and I'm only stopping because we're out of country to pretend in."

Kessa took that as she took most of what he said, which was as data. "Know it how."

"Like a room in a house you grew up in." He made himself look at the towers. "Except I didn't grow up. I was — filled. And whoever filled me used *this* for the mould." The word *mould* landed in his own chest like a dropped stone. He heard how close it sat to another word, and did not say the other word, because the crew was strung out on the slope behind them and some of them were watching him and one of them was sixteen.

Behind him, quiet, he heard Wick make a small sound.

---

They didn't reach the valley that day. Kessa wouldn't be hurried onto open ground she hadn't read, and for once Oscar was grateful to be slowed, because it gave him somewhere to put his hands that evening that wasn't the horizon.

He took out the book.

He'd carried it since the good claim under Marrow, wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs, and he'd told the crew it was salvage worth keeping and told himself it was a resource and told neither of them the true thing, which was that he could not have set it down if he'd tried. It was old past reckoning and whole where it had no business being whole, the binding supple, the pages uncrumbled, kept as the dead world kept its best things — a working woven through the leaves that held off the centuries. A grimoire. Not a relic to be read once and shelved. A *workbook,* a craftsman's private notebook, figure after figure in a patient close hand, refinements and second thoughts and small marginal corrections where the writer had caught himself in an inelegance and done it better.

He'd been learning from it since Marrow. That was the plain shameful fact of his last month and more: every figure he'd set on the road that he hadn't woken knowing — the drive that threw Wick clear at the spring, the brace, the cleaner lance — he'd had off these pages, by firelight, an apprentice cribbing a master's book after the master's gone to bed. It had made him more than the half-remembered scraps he'd clawed back from the basin. It was, he'd thought when he first opened it, a door on paper. A way forward.

He had stopped thinking of it as a way forward somewhere around the third night, when he'd turned a page and known the hand.

He knew it now as he'd known the valley. The slope of the figures, the private flourishes, the choices a writer makes that are as particular as a walk or a laugh — familiar in his fingers, familiar in the part of him that reached for a working without being told how, because it was *his* hand. Or it was the hand he'd been *given*. He had never worked out which was the worse reading and had stopped trying to, most nights. Tonight, with the towers of the not-home two days south and the valley shaped like his stolen silt, the question would not stay in its drawer. He looked at a figure he'd copied a dozen times — a clever, close thing for holding a working steady while you edited it, elegant past his own instinct — and he understood, cold, that he was reading a dead man's notes toward becoming that dead man. That the book in his hands and the town on the horizon were the same instrument, aimed the same direction, at him. *Here is the shape of the house. Here is the craft of the master. Come and be fitted to both.*

"What's it say," Wick asked.

She'd folded down beside him as she did now, closer than she used to sit, pretending she wasn't watching his face. The heat came off her wrong, banked too low, the mark at her throat a shade too pale, and he'd been marking that since the heath and saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't frighten her with a fear he couldn't yet name, and a girl's fears weren't owed to him anyway.

"It says a great many things very cleverly," Oscar said, "by a person with better handwriting than sense." He turned the book so she could see the figure and know she couldn't read it, which was their oldest joke and still worked. "Mostly it says the man who wrote it never in his life did a thing the plain way when a complicated way would let him show off. I find him insufferable. I'm learning everything he knew."

Wick didn't smile. She was looking at the page with her too-sharp eyes, not at the figures (she couldn't read the figures) but at the hand, and her face did something he didn't like.

"It's like yours," she said. "The writing. It's exactly like yours. I've watched you make your little marks in the mud a hundred times." She said it slowly, working it, and he saw the moment she arrived where he'd been living for a month, saw it go into her and turn cold. "That's a dead man's book and it's in *your* writing."

"Yes," Oscar said. There was no version of a lie that was kind here, and he was done handing her the kind ones; she'd earned the true ones in a grey hollow with her hands over a monster's mouth. "The man who wrote this is the man I was copied off. His hand is my hand because I'm — a fair copy of him, down to the flourishes. Ash told us that much, at her table. The book only makes it particular." He closed it, and held it against his chest a moment, and knew as he did it that he was holding it exactly as Cael held the shield, and let himself, because some things you were allowed to hold like that. "The town down there is his town. Made over. I don't know yet what for. I have a guess and I don't like it, and I'm not going to say the guess out loud until I have to, because saying a thing gives it hinges."

Wick was quiet for a while. Across the fire Cael had gone still in the manner that meant he was listening without seeming to, and Tarn's eyes had come up off the dark and settled on Oscar, unhurried, weighing. Only Kessa kept working, sharpening a knife that didn't need it, and even she had slowed her stroke.

"Then we don't let it have you," Wick said finally, fierce and low, near the version of it she'd said in a freed town a lifetime north. "Whatever it made itself for. It's a house. Houses burn." And then, because she was who she was, and because the fear had got into her and had to come out sideways or not at all: "I'm good at houses."

"I'm aware," Oscar said, and the joke landed for both of them, badly, wetly, and was better than the quiet would have been.

---

He didn't sleep much, and near the bottom of the night he stopped trying and went and sat where Kessa was keeping the watch, because she was the only one who wouldn't make him talk and the only one he wanted to talk to, which was a contradiction he'd stopped examining.

"I have to go in," he said, after a while. Not asking. "You know that. Whatever it is. There's no line of country that doesn't end at those towers, and even if there were, I'd walk the one that does, and I've been trying to work out why since noon and I think I finally have it and you're not going to like it."

"Try me."

"Part of me wants to." He watched the far valley, black on black, the pale ghost of the towers just legible against the coming grey. "There's a thing down there I was *made for.* A shape they cut me to fit. And some clerk-shaped part of me has spent this whole road wanting to go and see the shape, wanting it the same as you want to press a bruise, not because I mean to lie down in it. Because I want to stand in front of the thing I was built to be and find out, once, for certain, whether I can say *no* to it. I can't know that from up here. Nobody could. So I'm going to go down and stand in it, and I'd rather do that on purpose, with all of you, than get walked into it with a leash on. That's the whole of my reasoning and it is not tactically sound."

Kessa was quiet, marking the dark. "No," she agreed. "It isn't." She turned Doss's knife over once in her lap. "But it's the first plan you've said out loud in weeks that didn't have you spending yourself first and telling the rest of us after. So." A pause, dry as she got. "We'll pick the ground. You do the reading. And you'll tell me what you find the second you find it, and not one working before you've said it aloud to a person who can stop you."

"I read the made things," Oscar said. "The town, the workings, whatever's holding that valley in the shape of my drawer — those are made, those I can get a hand on, and I'll tell you what I get. But I've been honest with you since the grey and I'll stay honest: my reading is going to be *an* input down there, not *the* plan. The place is bigger than one man's looking. Some of what waits in it, I won't be able to touch at all." He glanced at her. "You've been running this crew as five people who are the answer since before I understood that's what you were doing. Keep running it that way. Especially when I forget."

"I always run it that way," Kessa said. "You're just finally awake for it."

Off at the edge of the fire's dead reach, Cael sat with his big hands loose and his sworn warmth banked low and even, and Oscar felt it the way you feel a hearth in a cold room without looking at it — steady, close, its own. Cael had said little since the waystation and carried much, but the warmth in him ran true and near now, the shortened hall of a country full of roofs and people, and Oscar found an odd comfort in the plain fact of it: whatever the town below drew its strength from — and it would draw it from the deep, he'd bet the book on that, from the cold written under-thing that ran through Oscar's own substrate — Cael's fire came from somewhere else entirely, a different river, answering to a thing that kept to hearths and had never heard of Everset. It would not gutter down there. It was the one light on this whole crew that the place could not have made and could not unmake. Oscar marked it, and was to be very glad of it before the week was out.

And Wick, asleep and not, twenty feet off with her back to a stone and her fist against her sternum even in sleep — Wick was the other thing. He'd watched her since the heath and the watching had become dread by degrees: the closer they came to the valley, the lower her fire banked and the harder her jaw set against something in the ground that was pressing on the part of her she kept shut. Whatever ran under that town, whatever the deep did where it came up close to the surface, it came up closest here, and it was leaning on her door as it had leaned at the spring, patient, glad, sure of its welcome. She didn't say so. She'd stopped saying so somewhere south of the waystation. But twice tonight, half-asleep, she'd flinched at nothing, and both times her hand had gone to her throat, and Oscar had made himself keep watching the fire and not the girl, because there was nothing his looking could do for a door, and pretending otherwise was a comfort he was no longer willing to sell her.

The grey came up over the valley slow, and the towers took shape in it, and were exactly the shape he'd known they'd be. Two days. He held the dead man's book against his ribs and looked at the dead man's home wearing a stranger's stone, and did the new work in place of the old — not reading the thing he couldn't yet reach, but counting the four people he could, and the owl somewhere overhead that wouldn't come to his hand, and the one thing he meant to carry down into that valley that no one had built into him and no one could take out: the intention, cold and clerkish and entirely his own, to walk into the shape they'd cut for him and refuse to fit it.

He had been made to come home.

He was going to go home and say no.
