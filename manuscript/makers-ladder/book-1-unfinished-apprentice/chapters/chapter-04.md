## Chapter Four — What the Fire Showed

They found the room three streets down and a level deeper: a squat stone chamber that had been a counting-house once, by the look of the slotted shelves and the long dead ledgers fused to them in blocks of ice. It had one door. Kessa walked the whole of it twice before she let anyone sit, running her fingers along the seams of the walls, testing the single entrance, and only when she had satisfied some private arithmetic did she nod, once, and drop her pack.

"We rest here," she said. "Short. Cael's bleeding and the girl's spent and I won't take the deep road tired. Two hours. We keep the fire small and behind the door, and one of us watches it the whole time."

No one argued. That, Oscar was learning, was how the crew agreed on things now: not by anyone deciding, but by no one objecting, a kind of governance by exhaustion. He found it inefficient and said nothing, because the one time he'd offered an opinion on the balcony it had nearly got him shot.

Wick built the fire. She did it without using her gift, he noticed, gathering the splintered remains of a shelf, striking a flint with hands that still shook a little from the street, refusing even to look at the easier way, as though she did not trust herself with so much as a spark. Oscar marked that and filed it under things to mend later, if there was a later.

The fire caught. And here at last was light that was warm instead of silver, light that threw real shadows and put real color back into the world, and in it Oscar finally *looked* at the three people who had decided, for reasons of their own, to keep him alive.

Kessa first, because Kessa was the one most likely to end him. By firelight she was older than her movement had suggested: past forty, he'd guess, though the frontier aged people early and she might have been less, with a face all weather and economy, brown skin gone leathery at the cheekbones, pale eyes set deep and always moving. Her hair was cropped short and shot through with grey she hadn't bothered to hide. She was working-lean, no spare anything, and she sat with the crossbow across her knees the way other people sit with their hands folded. She was, he understood, entirely mortal: no flicker of the deep current about her, no granted light, nothing but the long hard discipline of a body that had kept itself alive in dangerous places by being better at noticing than the things that wanted to kill it. She had taken out a folding knife and a whetstone and was working one slow against the other while she watched the door, and Oscar noticed (because noticing was the one trade neither of them had lost) that the knife was wrong for her hand. The grip was worn pale and smooth to a broader hold than hers; she had to choke up on it to keep the edge true. It was not her knife. She sharpened it anyway, the way you keep a blade keen for an owner who is going to come back wanting it.

Cael had stripped back his sleeve and was seeing to the gash on his forearm, and the firelight made a study of him: a big man, broad and slab-muscled, with the kind of plain open face that looks built for laughing and had clearly not done much of it lately. Younger than Kessa, thirties, maybe, with a nose that had been broken at least twice by something that hadn't apologized. The iron token at his throat caught the light: a simple thing, a hearth-mark, the sign of one of the small kindly gods Oscar had known of in his day mostly as a footnote. And when Cael laid his free hand over the gash and murmured something too low to catch, a faint and gentle warmth gathered under his palm. Not the deep current at all, not the cold writing Oscar's whole life had been built on, but something else, something *given*, a borrowed grace that knit the torn edges of the wound a little closer and left Cael grey and tired for the spending of it. It was not his to command, only to ask for: it answered when he knelt and not when he reached, and it always took more out of the man than it put back into the hurt. Oscar had always been faintly snobbish about it and found, watching it close a wound by firelight, that the snobbery had less ground under it than he'd assumed.

And Wick. Wick sat closest to the flames with her knees pulled up, and the fire showed her for what she was: a half-grown girl, sixteen at the most, underfed-thin, with a sharp clever face and a great deal of dark hair escaping a braid that had given up hours ago. There was a mark at the side of her throat, just below the ear. He'd taken it for dirt, in the dark, and saw now that it was under the skin, a faint reddish branching like the ghost of a lightning-scar or the vein of a leaf, the kind of thing a person is born with and not given. He did not know what it was. He knew it was the reason the heat lived in her hands, and he knew she did not know what it was either, by the way she kept her collar pulled up over it. The wild-touched were rare, and rarely happy, and almost never taught, because the people who could have taught them were afraid of them. He had been one of the people who could have taught them. He had, on occasion, been one of the people who were afraid.

"You're staring," Wick said, without looking up.

"I'm cataloguing," Oscar said. "It's different. Staring is rude. Cataloguing is scholarship."

"It's still rude when the scholar does it." But there was no heat in it; she almost smiled, and pulled her collar higher, and he let the mark go unmentioned, which she noticed, and which earned him a small and grudging softening he had not been angling for and was obscurely pleased to have.

He needed water. There was a cracked basin near the dead shelves where melt had pooled and refrozen and thawed again at the fire's edge, and he went to it to drink, and that was how he saw himself.

The water was still and the firelight behind him made a dark mirror of it, and Oscar looked down expecting nothing in particular—expecting his own face, which he had worn for three hundred years and could have drawn blind—and instead went very quiet.

It was his face. The long bones of it, the pale skin of the old long-lived blood, the silver hair, the eyes that held a little of the deep current's cold light the way all his people's eyes did. All of it his, unmistakably, the face that had argued with kings and outlasted kingdoms.

And it was *new.*

There was no other word that fit, and he turned the wrong word over and over looking for a better one and did not find it. Three centuries leave marks even on the long-lived; not age, exactly, but *wear*, the small accumulated record of a life: the thin scar through one eyebrow from a duel he could not now recall the cause of, the weathering at the corners of the eyes from squinting at small script in bad light for two hundred years, the particular set a face takes on from being a specific person for a very long time. He knew that face. He had shaved that face. And the face in the water had none of it. The eyebrow was unbroken. The eyes were unweathered. The skin was smooth as a thing just finished, just lifted from the mold, just—

*Made.*

He did not let the thought finish. He had got very good, in the last several hours, at not letting certain thoughts finish; it was becoming his chief remaining skill. He drank, and did not look at the water again, and when he sat back down by the fire his face was arranged into its usual mild superiority and only his hands, had anyone been watching them, were not quite steady.

Someone had been watching them. Kessa's eyes came off the door for half a breath, found his hands, and went back to the door, and she said nothing, and somehow her saying nothing was the loudest thing in the room.

"You should sleep," Cael said. To all of them, but mostly, Oscar thought, to fill the quiet that had got too large. "I'll take the first watch."

"You're grey as ash," Kessa said. "I'll take it. You spent yourself on your arm."

"And you've not slept in two days."

"I've not slept in two days a great many times." She did not move from the door.

It was Oscar who said the thing out loud, because of course it was. He had been turning it over since the balcony: the gap in the formation, cut to the size of a fourth body; Kessa's road that "wanted four"; the way the three of them governed by exhaustion, no one willing to put a hand up and decide. He was clever enough to fit the pieces together, and, as ever, nowhere near wise enough to leave them fitted in the privacy of his own skull.

"You've lost someone," he said, into the middle of the small argument about the watch. "Recently. The one who used to make the calls." He said it the way he said most true things, as though being right about a thing were the same as being permitted to mention it. "You move like a cart with a wheel off. Three of you, and a hole shaped like a fourth, and not one of you will stand in it. Who were they?"

The argument stopped. Something went out of the room and something colder came into it, and Oscar understood, a half-second too late, as he understood most things that weren't written on a page, that he had walked his clever feet directly into a grave.

"You just *say* it." Kessa's voice had gone very flat. Her hand had found the crossbow again, not lifting it, only resting there. "Whatever's in your head. Straight out the mouth, no toll at the gate." She looked at him with something that was nearly hatred and nearly grief and could not seem to settle on which. "I've put a bolt in men for less and slept the night through."

"That was unkindly put," Oscar conceded, and meant it, which did not help. "I have a gift for the unkindly put. I'm given to understand it's among my warmer qualities."

For a moment he thought she might do the thing the balcony had only threatened. Then Cael let out a long breath and set his mended arm across his knee and looked into the fire, and when he spoke it was not really to Oscar at all.

"There were four of us," he said. He turned the worn hearth-mark at his neck without seeming to know he did it. "Kessa, the girl, me. And Doss—Doss made four. He found these places. Knew how to read the old wards, knew which doors would hold and which would drop on you, knew when a thing was worth the risk and when it wasn't. We followed him into a dozen dead towns and came out of every one because he said *no* more often than he said *yes.* He was the one who said no."

He stopped. Kessa had gone still at the door, not watching it now, watching nothing. And the slow scrape of the whetstone, which had not paused once since they sat down, paused. She laid the knife flat across her knee and left it there. She did not say anything. Whatever she had to say about Doss she had said a long time ago and was done saying; the rest of it she kept in her hands, and her hands had stopped.

"There was a vault," Cael went on. "Deep in the last place, deeper than we'd gone. Doss said leave it. Said the wards on it weren't broken, they were *sleeping*, and a sleeping ward is one nobody's tripped yet, and that meant whatever it kept was still in there. We were tired and we were poor and we'd come a long way for nothing, and the three of us—" his jaw worked "—the three of us put it to a vote. Like it was a thing you could vote on. Like the dark cares how many hands go up. We outnumbered him. He came because he wouldn't let us go in without him."

"You don't have to," Kessa said, low.

"They've asked." He let the words settle, and went on, steady and not unkind, telling it now because telling it was owed. "The ward wasn't sleeping for nothing. It woke the way this place woke, all at once, the cold things coming up out of the floor, and there was a moment—there's always a moment—where one person can hold the door so the others get clear, and Doss took it, because Doss always took it, and we got clear, and he—" Cael's voice did not break. It simply stopped, the way the dying voice in the tower had stopped, on a word it could not get to the front of. "He didn't."

The fire popped. No one filled the silence. Oscar, who had spent his life being the one who filled silences, had three or four things he could have said. He always had three or four things. And he looked at the iron token turning in Cael's fist, and the stilled knife on Kessa's knee, and he set every one of them down unsaid and let the quiet stand. It had cost him only the pleasure of being heard.

"So when you came up out of *your* deep house," Cael said, finally, lifting his eyes to Oscar, and there was no accusation in them, which landed harder than accusation would have, "and Kessa said *put it back or put it down,* and I said no. You should understand I wasn't being kind. I've voted to go through a sleeping door once already. I'm not doing it twice. I'd sooner carry a stranger I'm not sure of than make that call again and be wrong the same way." He almost smiled, and didn't. "That's not faith. That's just someone who's learned the one lesson he was ever going to learn."

Oscar looked at the three of them: the watchful woman who would not leave the door, the gentle ruined man who had run out of *no*, the wild girl who carried a mark she didn't understand and a guilt that wasn't hers to carry. And understood, with the sudden clarity of a thing he should have seen hours ago, that he was sitting in a dead man's place. They had been moving since they lost Doss in fits and lurches, voting on things no one should vote on because the one who used to *decide* was holding a door somewhere in the dark forever. And some part of him, the part that had commanded and been obeyed for longer than these three had been alive, was already leaning into the gap, already reaching for the empty place at the head of the crew the way an hour ago he had reached for the empty place on his shoulder.

He did not take it. Not yet. It was not his to take, and he had learned, watching Cael, what it cost to want a thing for the group and be wrong. But he felt the shape of it, the wheel-shaped gap, one more space cut exactly to his size that he had not asked to be cut, and he wondered, he let himself wonder this much, how many such spaces there were in the world, and why they all seemed to be waiting for him.

"Sleep," he said. It came out gentler than he meant it, and with more authority than he had any right to, and he watched all three of them hear the authority in it, and watched none of them object. "Kessa keeps the door. I'll wake whoever's next. I don't sleep much, and I sleep badly, and I'd as soon spend the bad hours being useful." A pause. "And Cael—I'm sorry about Doss. For whatever the sorrow of a stranger is worth, which I'll grant is not much."

"It's worth something," Cael said. "You'd be surprised what it's worth."

They slept. Wick first and hardest, curled small as a child, the mark at her throat faintly warm in the firelight. Cael after, grey and heavy. Kessa did not sleep at all, but she let her eyes rest on the fire instead of the door for a little while, which from her was nearly the same thing.

And Oscar sat his watch in the warm uneven light, in a dead city under the bones of the world, and did not reach for his shoulder, because he had decided he was done discovering the absence over and over like someone worrying a bruise to be sure it still hurt. He missed the weight. He missed the voice. He missed it the way you miss a thing that is part of the architecture of you, so that its loss is not a wound in you but a wound in the *shape* of you.

"You'd have something to say about all this," he told the fire, very low, where no one could hear. "Something unforgivable. I'd pretend to hate it."

The fire said nothing. The dark beyond the door said nothing. And somewhere far below them, deeper than the deep road, deeper than the heart of the dead city, something that was not quite a sound and not quite a thought turned over once in its long sleep, the way a name turns over behind your teeth, and was still again before Oscar could decide whether he had felt anything at all.
