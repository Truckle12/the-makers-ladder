## Chapter Eleven — A Day's Grace

The trouble with a day's grace, Oscar discovered, was that it was extremely difficult to spend a day being graceful when you had no money.

They had four coppers between the four of them, two of which were Wick's and of a denomination Oscar didn't recognize and suspected were no longer legal tender anywhere. Marrow ran on coin and on credit, and credit in Marrow ran through the Tithe, and so by the middle of the morning Oscar had assembled a thorough and depressing picture of exactly how the trap was built.

It was, he had to admit, beautifully built. He could appreciate a good working even when he was caught inside it.

You could not sell salvage in Marrow except through a factor, and every factor wore the red cord, which meant every factor was the Tithe, which meant the Tithe set the price you got for what you risked your life to drag up out of the dead places—and set it low. You could not buy supplies for the next run except on credit, and the credit was the Tithe's, at the Tithe's interest, which meant the moment you came up short on one run you owed against the next, and the next, forever. You could not work the ruin without a factor's license, and you could not get a license without being in good standing, and you could not get into good standing without working the ruin. Every door in the town opened onto the same room.

"They don't even have to be cruel," Oscar said, with the flat admiration of a man examining a rival's elegant proof. "That's the genius of it. They've made the arithmetic do the cruelty for them. Every salvager in this valley is one bad week from owing their life to a ledger, and not one of them can point to the moment it was done to them, because it was never done in a moment. It was done a copper at a time." He looked around the crowded market street, at the hard tired faces hauling barrows of old-world stone past factors' stalls hung with red cord. "It's the best-run prison I've ever stood in, and I've stood in some celebrated ones."

"Could you not," said Kessa, low, "admire it quite so loudly. In the prison."

She had a point. He was working on it. It was, like not-confessing, a discipline he was bad at.

The relic trade itself was the part that turned his stomach, once he understood it, and he understood it standing at a factor's stall watching a salvager—a gaunt woman with frost-scar up one arm—sell a season's haul for a price that wouldn't winter her family. Most of what came up out of the dead places was just stone and dead metal, worth its weight and no more. But every so often the deep digging turned up something that still *held*—a worked thing, an old-world figure still live in some object, a lamp that lit without flame or a lock that knew one hand or a small grey box that kept whatever you put in it from ever growing cold. Those were the prizes. Those were what the salvagers broke their backs and buried their dead for. And those, the factor explained to the gaunt woman in a bored voice, "go south, to the proper buyers," at a price the factor named and she could not argue.

*South. The proper buyers.* Oscar filed it away. Somebody, somewhere up the long chain of red cord, was paying well for old-world workings—paying well enough that an entire town could be made into a machine for digging them up—and he found he was very curious who, and what they wanted them for, and decided this was a curiosity to keep behind his teeth for once.

They got their roof through luck and through Cael, in that order.

The luck was a boarding house tucked up under the high curve of one of the great stone ribs, its timber frame nailed into the ancient masonry like a swallow's nest under an eave. The Cael was that he carried the gaunt salvager's haul to the factor's scale for her when her scarred arm failed halfway, and would not be talked out of it, and the woman—her name was Brida, and the boarding house was hers, salvaging being a thing she'd done until the arm—looked at the big stranger doing a kindness that would profit him nothing and decided, against every hard lesson Marrow had taught her, to trust four people who could not pay.

"You'll sleep in the loft and you'll be gone before the cord comes asking," she told them, in the low back room that smelled of tallow and old stone. "I can't be seen feeding strays. They don't like generosity here. Generosity's competition." She said it plainly, without self-pity, ladling thin stew into bowls that had seen better decades. "Eat. You've the look of people who've not, in a while."

"Why," Cael asked her—gently, the way he asked the hard things. "Why risk it. For nothing."

Brida was quiet a moment, working the stiffness in her scarred arm without seeming to know she did. "My man went down into the deep claim four winters back," she said. "The good one. The one the Tithe keeps for itself, that they let no crew work but their own, because it's the richest and the worst. He went because we owed, and the only way to clear what we owed was the good claim, and the only way onto the good claim was to take the Tithe's terms." She looked at them. "He didn't come up. And the debt didn't die with him. It's mine now. Everything I make in this house, half of it's owed against a hole in the ground that ate my husband." She picked up her ladle again. "So when I see somebody do a kindness for free in this town, I feed them. It's the only coin the cord can't take a cut of. Eat your stew."

The room was quiet. Wick had stopped fidgeting. Cael's thumb had found the little hearth-mark again. And Oscar, who had a gift for filling silences and a worse gift for filling them badly, opened his mouth—

—and, for once, closed it again. Because the thing he had been about to say, the clever bitter true thing about the elegance of a debt that outlives the body it killed, was not a thing this woman needed to hear said cleverly. He had watched the words form and had set them down unspoken, and it had cost him only the small vanity of being heard, and he was beginning to suspect that this—this exact small swallowing—was a thing a person was supposed to learn to do, and that he had reached three centuries, or a week, without ever quite learning it.

"Thank you," he said instead. Just that. It came out plainer than anything he'd said in days, and Brida nodded as if it were the right thing, which surprised him, because he so rarely said the right thing on purpose.

It was the lamp that undid the morning's small peace.

Brida had one—an old-world thing, a salvage piece she'd kept back from a haul years ago and never sold, a hand-sized grey lozenge that gave a steady cold light when you turned it in your palm, and lately had begun to flicker and fail. "It's dying," she said, turning it, the light stuttering across her tired face. "They do. Everything the old world made dies eventually, same as everything else. I only wish it'd lasted me out."

Oscar took it because he could not not take it. That was the truth of him, the truth under all the rest: a thing that held a working put itself into his hands the way a question puts itself to a scholar, and he turned it, and he *looked*—the small interior figure of it resolving for him the way the husks and the wards and the great dead city had resolved, the old grammar plain as print. And he saw at once what was wrong: not the working, which was sound, but a single hairline fracture in the figure's binding, a place where the centuries had worn one line of it thin. The kind of thing he could mend with a thought, now that he had thoughts to spare again.

He mended it. He barely had to reach for the work; a touch, a single small true figure laid over the fracture the way you splint a cracked bone, and the grey lozenge flared up clean and steady and *warm,* years of guttering undone at a stroke, bright enough to throw shadows, and Brida made a small sound and nearly dropped it.

"That's—" she started. "How did you—nobody can do that. The factors say it can't be done, that when they die they're dead, that's why you have to keep buying—" She stopped. Her tired eyes went sharp, and then, a half-second later, afraid. "You can *read* them. The old work. You can read it and you can mend it."

"It's not difficult," Oscar said, which was the wrong thing, and which he knew was the wrong thing the instant Kessa's head came up.

Because it *was* difficult. It was, he understood a beat too late, watching the fear arrive on Brida's face, very possibly the most valuable thing a person could do in a town built on dying old-world relics that everyone had to keep replacing through the Tithe—and the most dangerous thing to be known for, in a town where the Tithe took a cut of everything and did not tolerate anyone who could give the salvagers a reason not to keep buying.

"You can't let anyone see that," Brida said, very low, pressing the warm bright lamp into his hands as if to hide it. "You understand? Not anyone. There are people in this town who'd pay a great deal to own a man who can do that. And there are people who'd pay more to be sure no one else could."

"Bit late," said Wick, from the window.

She had drifted to the loft's one small gap that looked down on the street, the way Wick drifted toward any view, and she said it lightly, the way she said most things, and then she did not say anything else, and the quality of her not-saying brought every one of them off the bench.

Down in the street, where the morning crowd was thinning toward the dead heat of midday, the scar-mouthed tallyman from the gate—Sarn, a salvager had called them, with the particular hush people used for the cord—stood at the mouth of the lane that led up to Brida's, and was not bored anymore. They had three of the red-corded with them, and a folded paper in one hand, and they were looking up the lane at the boarding house with the patient certainty of someone who has finished doing their sums and arrived at a number they like.

"That's about me," Oscar said. He knew it the way he'd known the door in the tower, the way he'd known the frost-shedding thing in the cradle—an animal certainty under the clever centuries. "That's not the debt and it's not the lamp. That paper's about me." He stepped back from the window. "Brida. Is there another way down out of this house that doesn't end at that lane."

"Through the back, along the rib, there's a salvager's run—" She was already moving, fear making her quick. "But they'll have watched the runs, if they're cord—"

"They'll have watched the runs," Kessa agreed, crossbow already in her hands, reading the room and the angles and the trouble the way she read a slope. "Which means we don't take the runs. Cael, the back. Wick, you stay on Oscar and you do not light anything unless I say, you hear me, not one spark in a crowded town. Oscar—" her flat eyes found him "—whatever that paper says you're worth, I'd like to keep it theoretical. Move."

They moved. And as Oscar went down the back stair of a kind stranger's house with a mended lamp still warm in his coat and a folded paper with his name—or his shape, or his *kind*—on it climbing the lane behind him, he understood two things at once, and neither was comforting.

The first was that he was not, in this town, an anonymous stranger passing through. Somebody had wanted him specifically enough to put it on paper and pay the cord to read it.

The second was that he had no idea why. Not the faintest idea. He knew he was made; he had stood in a font and learned that much and spent every day since trying to unlearn it. He knew, now, that he was wanted. But the line between those two facts—the *why* of it, the *what it meant*—was a dark he could not see the bottom of, and he was running down a stranger's stairs away from people who knew something about him that he did not know about himself.

He found he liked that least of all. He had spent three centuries—

He had spent, he corrected himself, even now, even running, a long time being the one in the room who knew the most. He did not at all care for being the one who knew the least, especially when the subject was himself.

Behind them, up the lane, a fist hammered on Brida's door, and the tallyman's bored quiet voice said something Oscar didn't catch, and the day's grace, such as it had been, was spent.
