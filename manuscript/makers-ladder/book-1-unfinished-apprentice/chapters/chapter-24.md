## Chapter Twenty-Four — What Listens

The wild did not feel empty. That was the first thing, and the wrongest.

Oscar had expected empty. The managed south had taught him that *unwatched* meant *dead:* a field nobody tithed was a field nobody tended, and a road nobody policed was a road gone to weeds. He'd carried that south with him without noticing, and so he was unprepared for the truth of the country past the paving: that the House's absence wasn't an absence at all. Something else lived here. Something older had simply never left.

They'd been two days in it. Two days of forest that got taller and stranger as the last farms fell behind, of trees whose bark ran with a faint slow light when the sun went, of birds that watched them pass with an attention birds were not supposed to have, of a deep green quiet that was not silence but the opposite. A held breath. A thing aware. The deep current ran close to the surface out here. Oscar could feel it the way you feel a river through the soles of your feet on a bridge, that cold vast under-thing he'd only ever brushed in dead cities and worked relics, here running warm and living and everywhere, threaded up through root and leaf and the slow blood of old animals. In the south they wrote on it with figures and stamps and licenses. Out here it wrote itself, and had been writing itself a very long time, and did not appear to require anyone's permission.

They crossed it in a loose order that had sorted itself without anyone deciding it, and the sorting of it told Oscar something true about each of them.

Tarn led, and Tarn had changed. In Oressa he'd been a banked thing, all that borrowed power held down under a lid; out here, on ground he knew, he walked like a man who'd had a collar taken off. He read the country the way Oscar read a working, except he did it with his eyes and his boots and forty years, naming things as he went. *That's a marker, leave it. Black alder there means water inside a mile, and something drinks at it come dusk, so we don't bed down close.* At a cairn of moss-grown stones where two deer-paths crossed, he stopped the whole line and made them wait while he set a strip of dried meat on the topmost stone, unhurried, almost solemn.

"Edge-stone," he said, when he caught Oscar's look. "You leave something. Doesn't matter what—it's not for the stone. It's for the habit. So you keep remembering whose house you're walking through, and you don't start mistaking the quiet for *nobody home.*" He turned back to the path. "Loudest way to die out here is to forget you're a guest."

Oscar filed it away, and would remember it later with a great deal of feeling.

Behind Tarn came Kessa, and Kessa had come *unwound.* He hadn't understood how tight Oressa had wound her until he watched it leave her shoulders, mile by mile. The city had handed her nothing she could trust: too many people, too many eyes, no clean line to anything that mattered. The wild gave her back a language she'd been fluent in her whole life. She read the ground the way Tarn read it, the two of them trading findings in half-sentences (*cat, old, gone off west; three days since rain; something bedded here and didn't care for company*), and she found them water twice and a dry camp once and a way around a fen that would have eaten a day, and she did all of it without a grain of magic.

It was, Oscar thought, watching her crouch over a scuff in the moss and tell him what had made it and how long ago and which way it had gone, quietly humbling. Here was a mortal woman reading the world as fluently as he read the deep, needing no gift to do it, and never making it look like a miracle.

"You're good at this," he said, and meant it as more than it sounded.

Kessa gave him a flat look that wasn't unfriendly. "It's just paying attention." She stood, wiped her hands on her thighs. "You'd be better at it if you did less of the other thing. The other thing keeps opening holes the rest of us have to climb out of."

He didn't have an argument, which was becoming a habit around her, and not an unwelcome one.

Cael was the one the country didn't suit, and he bore it the way he bore everything: quietly, and by working harder. Every dusk he built the fire with more care than a fire needed, banking and feeding and tending it like a thing with a soul. His warmth still came when he called it, the slow gold mending that had kept Oscar on his feet since the bowl, but it came a little slower out here, reached a little further, like a voice answering from the far end of a very long hall.

"It's not gone," Cael said, when Oscar, unable not to, saw the small extra effort in him and asked. "It's just a long way to wherever it comes from, out here. The thing I'm sworn to keeps to roofs and roads and people. This—" he nodded at the dark trees, the bark-light, the held-breath quiet "—isn't its country. It's older than roofs." He fed the fire a careful branch. "Doesn't mean I stop tending the hearth. Means I tend it harder, because somebody has to, and because I would rather be the man who kept a fire lit in a place like this than the man who let one go out." He said it lightly. He meant it as scripture.

And Wick.

Wick walked in the middle, where a crew keeps the things it can't afford to lose, and the wild was *working* on her. Oscar could see it without trying, which was its own warning: the heat coming off her stronger out here, the mark at her throat gone darker, her jaw set with the particular effort he'd learned to read as her holding something down with both hands. She'd gone quiet in a way that wasn't her ordinary quiet. She kept looking off into the deep green where there was nothing to look at, then catching herself and dragging her eyes back to the path, and saying nothing to anyone.

He fell in beside her on the second day, careful, the way you approach a thing that might bolt or burn. "The wild's loud for you," he said. Not a question.

She didn't look at him. "It's not loud. It's—" she worked for a word and didn't find one she was willing to give him "—*close.* Everything out here is close to the part of me I keep shut. Like the whole country's standing on the other side of the door, being patient." Her hands had knotted in her sleeves. "In the city it was just me holding it. Out here it feels like the holding's the only thing I'm *for.*"

"I know that feeling better than you'd guess," Oscar said, and when she finally glanced at him, skeptical, he gave her a true thing back, because she'd handed him one. "Every hour out here I want to *look.* Throw it all the way open, read the deep where it runs this close, drink the whole shape of the world down at once. It's the strongest want I own and the worst idea I've ever had, and I've been swallowing it for three days straight." He managed a crooked smile. "Turns out the holding's the hard part for all of us. You've just been doing it longer. And alone. And younger. So I'm not going to tell you it's easy and you should buck up. It isn't easy, it's the hardest thing there is, and you do it every minute, and nobody's ever once clapped."

Wick's eyes had gone bright and furious in the way that meant she would sooner die than cry in front of him. "*Don't,*" she said, thick. But she walked a little nearer to him after that, and didn't drift toward the trees again the rest of the day, and Oscar counted it the only honest work he'd done all week.

But the wild's attention wasn't a thing he had to read to feel, and across those three days it pressed in. It was nothing you could put a bolt through. A doe that watched them from forty feet and didn't run, didn't so much as flick an ear, just *regarded* them until Kessa's hand drifted to her crossbow, and then turned and walked, not bolted, away. A night when the bark-light all around the hollow dimmed at once, like a hundred eyes going half-shut, and came back up only when Cael fed the fire. A sense that thickened by the hour: that they were not being hunted. Hunting would have been simpler; hunting was a thing Kessa knew how to answer. They were being *assessed.* Weighed. Read, the way Oscar read things, by something in no particular hurry and of no particular mercy.

"It knows we're here," Kessa said the second night, flat, not a question, cleaning a knife that didn't need cleaning.

"Known since the edge stones," Tarn said. "Everything out here knows. The only question was ever whether we'd be quiet enough, and small enough, that it decided we weren't worth getting up for." He looked into the dark past the fire, and for once the dryness had gone out of him. "We've been lucky. Stay small. Leave payment. Don't go loud." He said the last word to the flames, but Oscar felt it land squarely on himself. Tarn had a fetcher's instinct for which member of a party was most likely to do something costly, and he had correctly identified the wizard.

Oscar said nothing, because by then he was spending most of his waking strength on not doing the exact thing Tarn was warning him against, and losing the argument a little more every mile.

His own reserve had been creeping back the whole while: slowly, the scorched place still tender whenever he leaned on it, Cael's slow gold doing its patient work each night, the wild's strange green abundance filling the well a little past the fumes. And as it filled, the want he'd confessed to Wick stopped being a small thing he could swallow between steps and became a steady pressure behind his eyes. Worse every hour. Worst of all near water, where the deep came nearest the surface and all but spoke.

He should have kept swallowing it.

He lasted until the third evening, when they made camp by a spring.

It was a small thing, the spring. A stone basin in a hollow where the trees stood back as if out of courtesy, and water welled up into it from somewhere far down, clear and cold and faintly, constantly moving though no stream fed it. And the deep current came up *with* the water. Oscar felt it the moment they stopped: a place where the under-thing surfaced, where the cold vast Script of the world broke the skin of the living one, and you could, if you knew how to look, read the deep current itself: the raw written stuff that ran under everything made.

He knew how to look. He was the only person alive, perhaps, who'd been built specifically to. And he was so tired of being careful.

He let his looking open. Gently, the fingertip he'd learned in the reeds, then more, then more, he bent his attention down into the spring, into the place where the deep came up, and for one long shining moment he saw it: the current running clean and unwritten, the raw stuff before anyone made anything of it, beautiful past saying—

—and an old memory rose to meet it, one he'd carried since the dark under Marrow and never been able to date: cold water, cold stone, the smell of larches over a coast road, spires past them, high cold halls. *Evermere.* He'd named it to Cael that first week and watched the whole crew fail to know it, because there'd been no Larchwall in longer than the maps went back. The horror of Evermere was that he remembered it perfectly, every cold stone of it, and it was fixed to no *when* at all, a picture cut clean out of a book, beautiful and flat and not his. He had never walked that road. The one he'd been copied from had, and left its evenings in him like silt.

It wasn't the memory that set the ache turning. It was the other name, the one Brida had given them on the road, the one waiting at the bottom of every map. *Everset.* The same bones as *Evermere,* one tumbler off. Place-names rhymed; he'd told himself so a dozen times since the open country. But standing in a spring where the deep came up cold the way it must once have come up somewhere with a larch-walled road, the rhyme stopped sounding like coincidence and started sounding like a question he didn't want to say out loud: why should the seat of the thing hunting him wear the bones of the only home he had? He didn't know. He shut it in the cold deep drawer where he kept the questions he couldn't yet afford, and left it there.

He should never have opened the spring.

He understood it the way you understand a misstep on ice: after, and all at once. He was still half-lost in the clean cold beauty of the deep, the Evermere ache still turning in him, when the hollow changed. The wrong, waiting quiet he'd half-noticed for three days and told himself was nothing, the wild's long-held breath, drew tight around him. The trees seemed to lean a degree nearer. And the deep he'd thrown wide stopped being a thing he was looking *into* and became a thing that turned, far down, slow and enormous and aware—and looked *back.*

He had time for exactly one thought, far too late to be of any use: that he had just done the loudest possible thing in the one country where something was always listening; that whatever had let five strangers walk three quiet days through its house now knew precisely where they stood, and precisely what they had brought into its wood.
