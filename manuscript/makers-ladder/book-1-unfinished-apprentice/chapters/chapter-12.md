## Chapter Twelve — Kindling

The hardest thing in the world, Wick had decided a long time ago, was holding still while you burned.

Not real fire. She wished it were real fire; real fire you could put out. This was the other thing, the thing that lived under her skin and especially under the mark at her throat, the heat that came up when she was scared or angry or just *too much* of anything, and right now she was all three, running crouched through the dark under Marrow with the cord somewhere behind them and her whole body wanting, wanting, *wanting* to let go.

*No sparks,* Kessa had said. *Not one. Not in a town full of people and old dry timber.*

So Wick held it. She held it as you'd hold a breath underwater, with her jaw tight and her fists tighter and the heat banked down to a coal in her chest, and it hurt, the holding, it always hurt. They all looked at her like she was a problem that might go off. None of them got that she spent every waking minute *not* going off, and that it was the most exhausting thing she did, more exhausting than running, more exhausting than being hungry, which she also was.

Oscar got it, maybe. A little. He'd said the aiming was the hard part, but she thought he'd had it half-backwards, and he was the only one who'd ever made her feel like the holding was a thing she was strong for doing instead of a bomb they were all standing next to.

She'd have walked into the deep dark for him just for that. As it turned out, she more or less did.

They'd gone down into the bones to hide. That was what the salvagers called it, *the bones,* like the town sat in the skeleton of some dead giant, and they all said it that way, low and spooky. But it wasn't bones. Wick had worked that out her first hour in Marrow, just by looking. It was a *city.* An old one, an impossibly old one, so huge that the town up top had grown inside it the way moss grows in a cracked pot: a whole second city buried under the first, tunnels and shafts and rooms built for nothing human-sized, all of it stone, all of it just *city,* and not one inch of it ever alive enough to have a bone in it. Grown-ups liked the giant better. The giant was a story, and *very large dead town* wasn't a story, it was just sad, and you couldn't sell sad to a salvager. Either way the cord didn't like to follow down here, because even the Tithe couldn't watch every corner of a buried city the size of a country. Brida had told them a way in before they ran. Kessa was leading. Cael was at the back being a wall. And Oscar was in the middle reading the walls the way he always did, his lips moving a little, his eyes catching that faint cold shine they did in the dark, and Wick was supposed to be on him, right on him, that was her one job: *stay on Oscar, don't light anything*—

—and that was the exact moment she heard the cord coming up the cross-tunnel ahead. The way ahead. The way Kessa was about to lead them straight into.

There wasn't time to say it right. There was barely time to think it. If she shouted, the cord would hear. If she did nothing, they'd walk into a patrol in a dead-end dark with the kid's-one-job-was-to-not-make-noise, and Oscar would be the one they wanted, and the whole thing would go wrong, and it would be her fault for not—

She did the stupid brave thing. She was good at the stupid brave thing. It was practically the only kind of brave she had.

She peeled off sideways into a side-shaft, the one going the wrong way, the one going *toward* the cord, and she let exactly one spark go: one, small, the size of a coin, flicked off her fingers into a heap of dry salvage-tinder twenty feet down the side-shaft. It caught with a *whump* and a flare of orange light, and she heard the cord's bored voices turn sharp and excited and go *that* way, toward the fire, toward the noise, toward her, away from the cross-tunnel where her crew was.

It worked. She'd hold onto that later, when the rest of it had gone bad. They went for the bait. Kessa's path was clear.

And then Wick was alone in the bones of Marrow with a fire behind her and the cord between her and her people, and the only way left was down.

She went down.

She wasn't scared yet, not really, not for the first while. She was *proud,* which she knew even then was the kind of feeling that gets people killed, but she couldn't help it. She'd done it, she'd been useful, she'd been the one who saw it and acted, not the liability, not the bomb, the one who *saved* them. And the pride carried her down three turns and a long sloped shaft into the deep bones before it ran out and left her standing in a dark so complete it had a texture, breathing air that tasted of cold stone and older things, with no idea at all where she was.

What came in to take its place was the cold thing, the *alone* thing. She had never been properly alone before. Not like this, not in a dark with a texture to it and no bottom and no Kessa's-steps to set her feet in. She kept one hand flat on the wall and the other curled around the small banked coal of her own heat, the only warm thing left in the world, and she told herself she'd find a shaft, find air, find them. She told herself a lot of things. The dark ate every one.

She didn't have long to be frightened of it, because that was when the thing in the dark found her.

She heard it before she saw it, which was wrong, because she couldn't see anything: a dry scraping shuffle, many-legged, coming along the tunnel toward her, and a smell like a cellar that something had died in. She backed up. It came on. And she realized, with the heat already rising in her without her telling it to, that it was coming *straight* for her, unerring in the pitch dark, and she knew why: it couldn't see her either. It could feel her. It could feel the heat coming off her, the coal in her chest, the warm living furnace that she was and couldn't ever fully bank, and down here in the cold bones she must have lit up to a thing like that like a bonfire on a hill.

*No sparks,* said Kessa's voice in her head. *Not one.*

"Yeah, well," Wick said, out loud, to the dark, her voice cracking on it, "Kessa's not *here,*" and she let go.

She meant to do it the way Oscar taught her. One thread, one fist-sized place, aimed. She honestly meant to.

Then it came into the light of her own kindling, and the aiming went clean out of her hands.

It was a spider. Not the word she'd have said out loud—out loud she'd have had a worse word—but that was the body her eyes found in the orange light: eight legs, jointed and bone-pale and long as her arms, folding and reaching and feeling at the floor, carrying a swollen sac of a body low and heavy behind a smooth blind head. No eyes anywhere on it that she could find. Just that pale head, and underneath it a busy wet working of mouthparts that opened and opened. It had no color on it at all; nothing down here did. And it came straight at the warmest thing in the dark, which was her, fast and certain and patient all at once. Not a monster, just a *beast,* an honest blind animal that had spent its whole life in the bones eating whatever wandered down, that wanted to eat her and felt nothing at all about it either way.

She reached for the heat the way she had in the ruined street the day she nearly killed everyone—reached for *all* of it—

—and there was more there than there had ever been.

She would never be able to explain this part, after, even to herself. The heat she knew was a coal in her chest, a thing with a bottom to it, a thing she could fill her hands with and then empty. But cornered and alone and more afraid than she had ever been in her life, she reached down *past* the coal. And there was no bottom there. Past the coal there was more, and past that more there was *more,* a depth opening under her like a trapdoor onto a furnace with no floor: something vast, and old, and *alive,* something that had always been down there behind the mark at her throat, on the far side of a door she'd never found the back of. And when she reached into it, it reached *back.* It noticed her. She felt the notice happen, an attention swinging toward her up out of the deep the way a great slow head turns in the dark, ancient and unhurried and *glad,* glad clean through, that she had finally, finally opened the door.

The power it poured into her was not the coal. It came up through her in a roaring flood that was hers and not hers at the same time, and the spider did not so much burn as simply *end,* a single white clap of heat that lit the tunnel like the inside of a forge and left a scorch and a smell and nothing else—

—and she couldn't stop.

That was the thing that turned the pride to terror and would stay with her long after the burn was gone: she reached to shut the door, to bank it, to *stop,* the way she always could, the one thing about herself she had ever been sure of—and it would not shut. The thing on the far side had her in its attention now and it was *pouring* through, eager, endless, glad, and she felt her edges going thin and bright, felt the heat begin to burn her *outward,* felt the hungry gladness come climbing up through the open door to take the rest of her, and not to kill her. She understood that much in the half-second she had for understanding anything. It wanted to *keep* her. To draw her down through the door and make her part of whatever it was, until there was no Wick left at all, only the door, and the thing that came through it.

She slammed the door with everything she had. With her whole self. With the part of her that wanted to live more than it wanted to be powerful, which she hadn't been totally sure existed until right then.

It shut.

She came back to herself on her knees in the dark, shaking, the mark gone cold and quiet at her throat, the tunnel black again and stinking of ash, her own breath sawing loud in the silence. Whatever she'd touched was gone, back down below the floor of her, sleeping or waiting or whatever it did. She was alive. She was herself. She'd almost not been, and nobody would ever, ever know how close it had been, and she made a decision right there on her knees in the dark, without looking at it too hard: she was not going to tell them. Not Kessa, who'd look at her like the bomb again. Not Cael, who'd pray over her. Not even Oscar.

*Especially* not Oscar. Because Oscar would understand it, and she couldn't stand, just then, to be understood.

She didn't have to find her way back. They found her. She heard them before she saw the bobbing light of Brida's mended lamp, heard Cael's big careful voice calling her name with an edge of real fear in it that made her eyes sting, heard Kessa's flat *there she is* that somehow had more relief in it than a shout would have. And then there was light, and there were her people, and Cael was crushing her into a hug that smelled of sweat and iron and safety, and she let him, and she did not cry, and she was very proud of not crying.

"That was reckless," Kessa said, but her voice wasn't hard. "And it worked, and it saved our skins, and if you ever do it again I'll tie you to me with a rope. Both can be true." Which, from Kessa, was practically a song.

And then there was Oscar, crouching down to her level the way grown-ups never did, looking at her with those cold-shine eyes, and she braced for him to *know,* to see the ash and the shaking and put it together, because he saw everything—

—but he didn't look at her like she was a problem. He looked at the smear of ash where the thing had been, and then back at her, and what was on his face wasn't suspicion. It was something that looked, impossibly, like *respect.*

"You held it together," he said quietly. "Down here, alone, with something coming at you. You held it together and you walked away." He didn't ask how. She loved him a little, in that moment, for not asking how. "That's the whole job, Wick. That's the entire job. Everything else is just decoration."

And then he stood, and turned, and read them a way out.

The part that stuck wasn't the thing in the dark, or even the door. It was the way he laid his hand against the old dead wall of the deepest bones of Marrow and the cold shine in his eyes brightened and he simply *knew,* the way nobody could know, where the tunnels went and which ones would hold and which way was air. The walls talked to him. The dead world's bones, the thing that had nearly eaten her alive from the inside, the place that had opened a door in her she hadn't known was there. He walked through it like it was his front hall, like it was *his,* unafraid of any of it, reading a way home for all of them out of a language no one living was supposed to be able to read.

He was magnificent. He was the most magnificent thing she had ever seen.

She wanted, with a want that was bigger than the heat and steadier than the fear, to be like that someday. To stand in the dark with all of it coming at her and not be afraid, because she *understood* it. She wanted him to teach her until she did. She'd been calling herself his apprentice in her head for days now, secretly, the way you try on a coat in a shop you can't afford. And watching him read them out of the bones, she stopped trying it on. She just decided it was true.

There was one thing. One small thing she noticed and didn't know where to put, so she put it away with everything else she wasn't saying. It was the way the dead place *let* him. The walls, it was a silly thought and she knew it was silly, the walls seemed almost *glad* to tell him the way. Like the dead city knew him. Like it had been waiting a long time for him to come home.

So she followed her teacher up toward the light, and she kept her own door shut, and she told no one—not Kessa, not Cael, not even him—what had reached back through it in the dark, or how glad it had been.
