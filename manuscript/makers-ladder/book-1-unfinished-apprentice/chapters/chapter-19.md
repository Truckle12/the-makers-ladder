## Chapter Nineteen — The Open Country

The road taught them what the south was before anyone said a word about it.

It happened by degrees, the same as the cold had let go—first a second road joining theirs from the east, then a third, the three of them braiding together into something wider and harder-packed than any track in the north, a proper relic-road laid on the makers' old bones and kept up, Oscar realized, *kept up,* by people with the wealth to keep things. Then traffic. A teamster with a wagon. A family on foot with a handcart. Two riders who passed them at a trot and didn't so much as glance, which in the north would have been an insult or a threat and here was apparently just manners. By midday the empty white silence Oscar had woken into months ago was simply gone, replaced by the ordinary churn of a country with somewhere to be, and he found the noise of it stranger than any monster he'd met in the dark.

The wonder came at a crossroads stall, and it gutted him a little, which he hadn't expected.

It was a tinker's tray, set up where the roads met, the sort of thing that sold buttons and needles and luck-charms to travelers. And laid out among the buttons, in a chipped wooden bowl, casual as eggs, were lamps.

Worked lamps. Old-world ones—he could see it from ten paces, the cold seam-light of a real figure folded into glass and metal, the genuine thing, not a candle in a fancy housing. There were six of them in the bowl. The tinker was selling them three for a copper-bit, and using a seventh himself, unlit in the daylight, hung off his cart for evening, as you'd hang any lamp.

Oscar stood and looked at the bowl and thought about Brida.

He'd nearly died reading the working in her lamp. Up north, that single mended lamp had been a thing worth a winter's salvage, worth a bounty-hunter's notice, worth carrying into a town that would kill him for it: a small precious miracle pulled out of a dead world by people who bled for every scrap of it. And here it sat, six to a bowl, three for a copper, beside the buttons, because down here the dead world wasn't a graveyard you robbed. It was a *supplier.* The north dug the relics up out of the ice with frozen hands and dead crews, and shipped them south on the House's seal, and down here they came out the other end of the long bright pipe as merchandise. As lamps in a bowl. As the cheap end of the trade.

"You buying, friend?" the tinker said, friendly. "Good lamps. Honest light. Won't gutter, won't smoke. Reader-stamped, all of them—" he tapped a little tin tag wired to one "—House assay, so you know they're sound."

"No," Oscar said. "Thank you. Just looking."

He moved on, and didn't explain the joke to anyone, because it wasn't funny, and because Wick had gone quiet at his shoulder looking at the same bowl, and he thought she'd understood it too, in her own way: that everything they'd suffered for in the north arrived down here as something you'd haggle over and forget.

"I almost died in a pit for things that end up in a bowl like that," Wick said, low, to no one in particular. She wasn't asking for comfort; she was just setting it down, the way you set down a stone you've carried too far. "Three for a copper."

"I know," Oscar said.

"It's not *fair,*" she said, and then heard herself, sixteen and saying a child's word about the shape of the whole world, and shut her mouth and walked on with her jaw set, and Oscar thought it was one of the truer things anyone had said since they'd come down out of the ice.

---

The town was called Hollet's Cross, and it was the first real town of the south, and it ran on the House the way a mill runs on water.

You couldn't not see it. Oscar had thought, back in Marrow, that "a southern house" meant a family, a faction, a rich name among rich names. He understood within an hour of walking into Hollet's Cross that he'd had the scale wrong, as he kept getting the scale wrong about everything. Here, *the House* was not a family. It was the air. Its mark was over the door of the assay office and the broker's and the lending-house and the bonded warehouse where the relic-wagons offloaded under guard; its mark was on the weights the grain-sellers used and the licenses pinned to the readers' stalls and the very coin that changed hands, because the House minted it. People didn't say its name. They didn't need to. They said *the House,* the way you'd say *the sky,* and they said it with a particular tone: not love, not hate, but the flat respect you give a thing too large to have feelings about, a thing that simply *was,* that had always been and always would be, that you lived inside like a fish inside water, never thinking to be grateful for it.

And on the House's mark, worked into the seal beside its own device, small enough you'd miss it if you didn't already know it in your bones—a loop. A knot tied in a circle. The same mark burned into the haul out of the good claim, and stamped on the crates that went south, and, under the sacking on Oscar's own back, scored into the steel of his sword.

He found he had stopped in the middle of the street, looking at a coin in a fruit-seller's palm, at the loop on it, and he made himself walk on before anyone noticed a hooded northerner staring at money.

The loop was the House's, then. Or the House was the loop's. He didn't know which, and the not-knowing had teeth. *That's my mark on their money. That's their money on my sword.* He filed it, cold, beside the haunted hand in the grimoire and the writ that had been waiting longer than he'd been alive, in the growing drawer of things that all pointed the same direction and none of them anywhere good.

There was a worse discovery waiting, and it was about him.

Reading was a trade here. A licensed one. He'd thought his gift was a wild private thing, a quirk of whatever he was. And in Hollet's Cross there were *stalls* for it, men and women in House-stamped aprons who sat behind little counters and read relics for the brokers, assayed them, told a buyer whether a worked thing was sound or spent or dangerous, for a fee, under license, with the House's seal on their certificate. They were nothing like him. He drifted close enough to one to watch, and saw that what they did was real but shallow—they could tell *that* a thing was worked, and roughly how strongly, and whether it would bite; they read the label, not the page. None of them, he was fairly sure, could have read a living tree as workmanship, or unstitched a figure down to its strokes, or done a tenth of what came to him as easily as breathing. But that wasn't the point.

The point was that reading belonged to the House. Here, you read with a license or you didn't read at all, and an unlicensed reader was a thief or a smuggler or worse, and the worst thing of all to be, in a town where the House was the air—Oscar understood it with a slow cold certainty—was a reader the House hadn't made and didn't own. His gift, which had been his value in the north, was a noose down here. The single most useful thing he could do was the single most exposing thing he could do. He thought of Brida again: *the kinder it gets, the closer it is.* He hadn't understood that she meant the whole country.

"Keep the sword wrapped," he said quietly to Kessa, "and keep me from reading anything where anyone can see. I'm a brand that lights itself, down here."

Kessa, who had been pricing dried fish and listening to the market the way she listened to everything, only nodded. "Already worked that out. You've got a tell when you look at a thing—you go still wrong." She paid the fish-seller. "We get supplies, we get out, we sleep off the road. And we find out about your polite friend before we lose another day to him being ahead of us." Her flat eyes came up. "That part I've already started."

---

She'd started it the way she did everything, by being unremarkable in the right places, and by the time the four of them found a quiet corner of a quieter taphouse on the edge of town she'd assembled most of it, and what she had was worse than not knowing.

"He's a finder," she said.

"A finder," Oscar repeated.

"It's a trade." She said it the way she'd say a track was a wolf's and not a dog's—certain, unhappy. "Licensed, like everything here. The House loses things. Relics that walk off a wagon. A debtor who runs his bond. A piece of property that goes where it shouldn't." A beat, very slight. "And it pays people to bring them back. *Finders.* There's a whole guild of them. There's a *board* in the broker's with standing finds posted on it, like a butcher posting cuts. They're respectable. People tip their hats." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table, which for Kessa was a storm of feeling. "I read the board, Oscar. It's pages. The House is always finding something. It's the most ordinary thing in the world down here, hiring a man to go and quietly bring a thing home that doesn't want to come."

Oscar sat with that, and felt the floor of the thing drop another level beneath him, as it kept doing, every time he thought he'd reached the bottom of how large this was.

Because there it was, the thing he hadn't wanted said out loud, dressed up as nothing. *The most ordinary thing in the world.* He was a find. Not a special one, not a personal vendetta, not Sarn's clever once-in-a-career score. He was a line on a board he'd never seen, one of who-knew-how-many things the House was patiently, professionally, respectably bringing home this season, and there was an entire *guild* of polite men who did this for a living, who tipped their hats and asked after your children and brought you back, and the only thing that made his line on the board different from a stray relic or a runaway debtor was the size of the number beside it. He didn't say any of that out loud. He looked at Wick, dozing against the wall with her hand in her pocket, and was glad she wasn't listening.

"This one has a name," Kessa went on, lower. "The one ahead of us. They knew him at the finders' board—he's not local, he came up from the deep south special, which the man I asked thought was *interesting,* a finder of his standing coming all that way for one find." Her jaw worked. "Corwen. That's the name they gave. They say—" and here she stopped, and Oscar saw her decide to give it to him straight, because Kessa did not believe in softening a thing you'd have to face anyway "—they say he doesn't lose them. Ever. Not once, in a long career. They say he's the one the House sends when it actually wants the thing back, not just wants to look like it's trying. And they say he's the most civil man you'll ever meet, which the fellow telling me thought was a recommendation, and which made the hair stand up on my arms, because I've met a lot of men, and the civil ones are the ones who've stopped seeing you as a person at all."

"Brida said that," Wick mumbled, not asleep after all. "He'll come kind."

"He'll come *certain,*" Kessa said. "That's worse. A man who's certain doesn't hurry." And that, Oscar thought, was the cleanest and most frightening thing anyone had said about Corwen yet, and Kessa had got there in an afternoon, from a butcher's board and a tipped hat.

---

They almost left it there. They had what they'd come for—a name, a trade, a shape to the dread. But Cael, who had been quiet all through the town in a way Oscar had taken for his usual peace, spoke up as they gathered their packs, and it turned out his quiet had been something else.

"I want to know if he left word," Cael said.

Oscar looked at him.

"For you." Cael's broad mercy-priest's face was troubled, the green country sitting wrong on him in a way Oscar was only now seeing. "I've been listening too, just not to the trade of it. To the *kind* of him. A man that civil, that certain, who knows we're behind him—because he must know, Oscar; he's been asking at every door, and an asked-at door remembers two faces, his and the next one through—a man like that doesn't just leave a trail. He leaves a *note.* It's courtesy. And his whole horror is the courtesy." He set his jaw. "I'd rather we read it knowing what it is than be handed it cold somewhere worse."

He was right, and Oscar hated that he was right, and they went to the taphouse where the southbound travelers stopped and Cael asked the keep, plainly, in his warm steady voice, whether a southern gentleman, a finder, had left any word for a party coming down out of the north.

The keep's face did something complicated, and he reached under the bar.

"Wondered when you'd come," he said, not unkindly. "He said someone might. Silver-haired man, he said, or his people. Said to hand it over with no charge and no trouble." He laid it on the bar: a single folded square of good paper, sealed with a drop of plain wax, no device pressed in it—a private man's seal, not the House's, which was somehow worse. "Bought the house a round when he left it. Civil sort."

Oscar took it. It weighed nothing. He broke the wax aware of the three of them at his shoulders, and read it once to himself, and then, because they had earned it and because secrets were a luxury a crew couldn't afford, he read it aloud, low, in the corner, in a voice he kept very level.

It said:

*To the gentleman coming south, and the friends who travel with him—*

*Forgive the presumption of a note. I find it spares everyone the indignity of a chase, and I have never cared for chases; they damage the goods and frighten the bystanders, and you have frightened quite enough of those already, up north. I'm told it was loud. That isn't necessary with me. I'd like you to understand that at the outset.*

*You owe a debt you did not choose and cannot pay, and I have been engaged to settle it, and I will. That is simply the case, the way winter is the case. I'd ask you not to make it ugly on the way. The people with you are not on my paper; I bear them no ill, and if they part from you they may go home unbothered, and I'd gently urge them to. The girl especially. She has a long road of her own ahead, by the look of her, and none of it lies through me.*

*As for you—I'll be at the end of the road south, where I have always been, doing my work, in no hurry at all. You'll come to me. They always do. There is nowhere else the road goes. I'll have wine.*

*Yours in the meantime, and with no hard feeling on my part whatsoever,*
*Corwen*

The corner was very quiet.

Oscar folded the note back along its creases, slowly, because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. Every line of it had landed exactly where it was aimed. The contempt dressed as courtesy. The casual *the goods.* The reach into the crew to split them—*they may go home unbothered*—the surgeon's neatness of it, and worst, the line about Wick, *the girl especially,* naming the long strange road under her skin that even she didn't understand, and doing it gently, to show Oscar how much he could see. And the certainty under all of it, smooth and seamless as the House's own coin: *you'll come to me. There is nowhere else the road goes.*

And there was the thread that pulled the whole thing loose, sitting in the corner with the note going cold in his hand. *He's never seen her.* Six days ahead the entire way. Corwen had never been in a room with any of them. So what he knew about Wick hadn't come from his own eyes; it had come from whoever sent him. Oscar thought of the licensed readers in the market, hands flat on their relics, reading label-deep, and understood that was only the small end of the trade. Somewhere was the large end—a reach that needed no hand, no room, no face in front of it. The House had looked at them once, he'd have wagered, one long cold look from very far off, and seen everything it cared to, and handed the picture to its hound like a scent—then gone back to running a country. And that was the part that turned his stomach worse than the threat itself: not that they could see him from the sky, but that, having looked, they'd found him not worth the bother of a second glance.

"He's not chasing us," Oscar said, for the second time in two days, and found the thought had grown teeth since the Throat. "He's not even *moving.* He's sitting at the bottom of the only road there is—at Everset, I'd wager; the seat Brida named, the House's own city—pouring wine, certain as winter, because the country itself does his walking for him. The road goes south. The answers are south. The House is south. And he's south, waiting, because he knows the writ leaves us nowhere else to go." He looked up at his crew. "He's right that there's nowhere else the road goes. He's wrong about why we're walking it."

"Which is?" Kessa said.

"He thinks we're prey being driven down a chute." Oscar tucked the note inside his coat, against the cold spot under his ribs, where he was apparently going to keep collecting the things that frightened him. "Let him keep thinking it. A man certain you're prey doesn't watch carefully for a hunter coming the other way." He stood, and pulled his hood up, and felt the loop on the coin and the loop on his sword and the loop on the House's seal all pulling the same direction—south, toward the wine and the answers and the patient civil man at the end of the only road there was. "We're not walking south because he's herding us there. We're walking south because that's where the truth is. He just told us, very politely, that he'll be standing right next to it."

He led them out of Hollet's Cross into the long bright wealthy country, where the dead world came to be sold and the House was the air and a man named Corwen sat somewhere ahead pouring two cups, and Oscar, for once in his short strange life, walked toward the thing hunting him on purpose—because Brida had been right about that, too. There was no running it off.

The only way out was straight through.
