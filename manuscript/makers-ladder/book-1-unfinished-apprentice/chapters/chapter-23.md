## Chapter Twenty-Three — Where the Roads Won't Go

The drain gate let them out under the fish market exactly where Tarn promised, into the gray nothing-hour before dawn, onto a mud track that ran along the river toward the southern road and the last House post standing between them and open country.

The post was lit.

It should not have been. It was a customs shed, no more: a single lamp-bright box where a bored clerk stamped the carts going south with grain and stamped them going north with everything grain bought, and at this hour, Tarn had said, it would be one sleeping man and a brazier. Instead there were three lamps burning, and figures moving against them, and Oscar felt it before he saw anything worth seeing, a cold needle laid against the back of his skull—a reader. Sitting in that shed in the dark before dawn like a spider feeling its web. Waiting.

Waiting for the particular flavor of a man who could read without a license.

"Down," Kessa breathed, and they went down into the reeds along the bank, five exhausted people folding into wet cover, and for a moment the only sound was the river and Oscar's own pulse and Wick's breath going too fast beside him until Cael laid a hand on her shoulder and it slowed.

Oscar lay in the mud and counted it up, and did not like the answer.

The post was watched because someone had told the House where to watch. Not the road generally. *This* road, *this* night, the southern gate of Oressa with a reader posted to it like bait left for one specific animal. And there was only one person who'd known, an hour ago, that the silver-haired freak who'd gutted Borr on the sand meant to leave the city before dawn by the river road.

"Hauk," Tarn said, very low, reading Oscar's face. There was no surprise in it, only a tired, bitter certainty. "He couldn't keep me and he couldn't kill you in front of his own bowl, so he sold the only thing he had left. You." A breath. "Told you I was a waste of your evening. Didn't think the bill would come this fast."

"It's not your bill," Oscar said.

"It's got my name on it." Tarn's jaw worked. "Hauk to a fixer. Fixer to a House factor. Word goes up the line, comes back down: *reader on the south gate, loose one running.* They didn't have to want you badly. A boss whispers and the House just—" he turned a thick hand over, the gesture he'd used in the cell, the one that meant *owing* "—shuts a gate. Doesn't even look."

That was the part that put the cold all the way through Oscar, worse than the reader in the shed. Not malice. *Convenience.* The machine hadn't been roused. It had barely been nudged.

He could fix this, normally. On a full reserve he could have read the shed down to the working the reader had strung across the dark, found the slack place in the net, and walked the crew through the gap like smoke. But he wasn't on a full reserve. He was on the dregs and the fumes of the dregs, and the place inside him where the figures came from still felt scorched and tender from the bowl, the frayed edges of it twinging every time his pulse climbed.

He tested it anyway, because he couldn't not. He reached for the lightest read he knew—

—and the scorched place *shrieked*, a white wire of pain up the inside of his arm, and he had to bite down on the reeds to keep from making a sound that would have ended all five of them.

"Don't," Cael whispered, who'd seen it cross his face. "Whatever you just tried. *Don't.* You burned too close last night. You go back to that well now, you'll tear something that doesn't grow back. I've mended men who frayed. I couldn't put them back the way they came." His hand found Oscar's shoulder, steady and warm. "Not tonight. Tonight you let the rest of us be the clever ones."

It was, Oscar thought, possibly the most galling sentence anyone had ever said to him, chiefly because it was correct.

---

So he learned, in the reeds, with a reader thirty yards away and dawn coming, the thing necessity had been trying to teach him for three days: that he had only ever known how to read with his whole hand.

He'd always gone in full-bore: throw the looking wide open, drink the whole shape of a thing, spend whatever it spent. It was, he understood now, lying scorched in the mud, a child's grip. A fist where a fingertip would do.

He could not afford a fist. So he tried a fingertip.

He let the looking *barely* open. A hair, a thread, the least touch he could manage, like laying one finger on a still pond to feel which way it would ripple if you ever pushed. It cost almost nothing. The scorched place didn't shriek; it only ached, dully, the way a bruise aches when you press it on purpose. And through that single thread he felt, not the buckles on the reader's coat, not the whole shape of the shed, just one made thing. The reader's net: the working they'd strung across the dark to catch a gift being used, laid out like a snare on a game trail.

It was strung across the road. North, up toward the city, toward the gate the criers were watching, toward where a fleeing man would sensibly come *from.*

"The net's on the road," Oscar breathed. "Strung for a man coming down it from the city. Nothing over the water." He almost laughed, and didn't, because laughing hurt.

Kessa was already up on her elbows, sighting along the bank. "If they're watching the road, we don't take the road."

Tarn was nodding slowly, the wild-ways man waking up behind his eyes. "The river bends west of the post and the reeds run with it. Stay low, stay wet, and the bank hides us till we're past their lamplight. After that—" he glanced south, where the dark was just beginning to gray "—after that it gets interesting."

---

It got interesting four hundred yards on, when the patrol came.

They'd cleared the post: five shapes sliding through cold reeds with the river loud enough to cover the suck of mud, the reader's net combing the road behind them and never reaching the water. They'd just come up onto firmer ground where a cart-track crossed the river by a low wooden bridge, when Kessa flung up a fist and everyone froze.

Hooves. Unhurried. Coming down the cart-track from the south, toward the bridge, toward them.

Not a gate this time. A horse patrol: House men by the cut of them, two riders walking their mounts along the southern approaches, not hunting so much as *being present.* There was nowhere to fold. The reeds had given out at the firm ground. There was the bridge, and the open track, and a single huddle of cart-sheds twenty feet off where the night's carters had left their loads, and the riders would be on all of it in the time it took to think.

"Sheds," Tarn said, and didn't wait to be agreed with.

They got into the gap between two loaded carts as the first rider's lamp swung across the track. Pressed flat. Wick was shaking. Not fear, Oscar realized, with a fresh drop of dread; she had her fist jammed against her own sternum and her teeth set, and there was a heat coming off her in the cold that had nothing to do with exertion, the banked fire of her stirred up by terror and proximity and the long bad night. If she lit, here, now, they were finished. Cael saw it the same instant. He didn't speak. He took her clenched fist in both of his broad hands and held it, the way you'd cup a candle against a draft, and bent his head to hers and breathed, slow, deliberate, in and out, until she caught the rhythm and matched it and the heat banked back down.

The riders stopped on the bridge.

Close. Close enough that Oscar could hear the saddle creak as one of them leaned, the small wet sound of a horse at the river. Close enough that one good look into the shadow between the carts would end it. And he couldn't read them at all; there was nothing worked on two tired patrolmen for his gift to catch. Men were just men; his looking slid off them like rain off glass. But he had eyes, and the riders carried a lamp.

He watched instead: the swing of the lamp as they turned, the tilt of a head, the lazy track of a man's gaze in the dark. Two of them, one settled on the river, one drifting along the sheds, looking because looking was the job and not because he expected to find anything—

—and the lazy one was drifting *toward* them, slow as a clock hand, and in maybe four seconds it would arrive at the gap.

"He's drifting onto us," Oscar breathed, barely a thread of sound. "The far cart—the loose tarp—his eye's already half on it."

Kessa already had a stone in her fist. She didn't look at it; she'd palmed it somewhere in the last ten seconds without seeming to, and she put it where Oscar told her the man was half-listening: a low underhand toss into the dark beyond the far cart, where it knocked, rolled, and settled against the flapping tarp like the night had simply shifted its weight.

The lazy attention snapped toward the sound. Away from the gap.

"Rat," one rider said, bored, to the other.

"Whole town's rats," the other agreed, and clicked his tongue, and the hooves moved on, north, over the bridge and away, unhurried as they'd come, and took their lamplight with them.

Nobody moved for a long count. Then Tarn let out a breath through his nose that might, in a man less economical, have been a laugh. He looked at Kessa with something close to professional respect. "Remind me to never wager you at stones."

Kessa was already wiping her palm on her thigh, unbothered, working out the new shape of things aloud. "You watch them," she said to Oscar. "Where they're looking, when they turn. You tell me, I do the rest. That's the arrangement till you've got a pulse again."

"A generous arrangement," Oscar said, "in which I am the lookout and everyone else does the surviving. I'm told it builds character." He let Cael haul him upright. "For the record, last night I beat a man the size of a barn. Tonight I am being out-thrown by a tracker and lectured by a priest."

Kessa was already moving.

---

They didn't try a third road.

They didn't have to; by the gray of full dawn, hunkered in a hedge a mile south of the city with Oressa's smoke at their backs, Kessa put questions to Tarn, and the answers were worse than one watched gate. He'd counted the patrol's gear, the post's lamps, the simple fact of a reader spent on a customs shed in the dark, and he read it as Oscar would a working: the shape of the whole thing from its pieces.

"It shut too fast," he said, first, like a man putting the strangest figure at the top of the bill. "A grudge buys a word up the line. It doesn't put a reader in a shed by dawn. They were already listening on this stretch. Before Hauk."

Oscar thought of a notch in the hills gone silent, and a name written on a road in letters a blind man could read, and Kessa's flat voice saying *you think your polite friend won't hear about it,* and said nothing at all.

"One gate," Kessa said. "Or all of them."

"All of them." He scratched his jaw. "You don't post a reader and walk horse on the south approaches for one loose man you half want. That's a cordon. Every paved road south: a watcher, a reader where they can spare one. Men being present."

"For how long?" Oscar said.

"Till the loose thing dies trying a gate. Or sits still long enough to collect."

"Then we don't take a road," Kessa said. She'd said it once already tonight. This time it landed differently, because this time there was nothing after it.

"No," Tarn agreed. "We don't take a road." And he turned and looked south and west, away from every line on every map, to where the cultivated country gave out in a gray-green smudge of hills and the hills gave out in something darker behind them, and Oscar felt the hair go up on his arms before the man even said it. "We go where the roads won't."

---

Oscar had been raised, if *raised* was the word for memories poured into him secondhand, on the south as a made and managed place. He had not, until Tarn pointed, properly understood that the managed place had an *edge.* That it stopped.

"Past the last farms the paving quits," Tarn said. "Nothing out there the House can tax, so it doesn't spend a copper on it."

"What is out there?" Kessa said.

"The old country. Forest, fen, the bones of places older than the House. Runs south for days." He said it flat. "Salvage crews cross it for relics. Fetchers for what goes in a bowl." He said *fetchers* like a man says a job he's done. "That was half my life. Three and four days deep and back."

"Back," Oscar said.

"Mostly. Some come back *changed.* Some send word they're not coming." He paused. "No cordon out there. No reader for fifty miles. No road either. No inn, no law, nothing between you and the sky."

"You make it sound restful," Oscar said.

"It would be," Tarn said, "except for whose it is."

And there it was: the thing that had put the hair up on Oscar's arms before a word of it was spoken. Cael had gone still. Even Kessa had gone still.

"Whose," Oscar said.

"Not the House's." The dry had gone out of Tarn's voice. "House licenses every working in the south. Paper, stamp, tithe, or you're a criminal for breathing wrong. You know that. You've been one since you came down out of the north." He almost smiled. "It never licensed the wild. Never took it. What's out there was running before anyone thought to stamp it."

"That's not a whose," Kessa said.

"It's got a keeper." He nodded south, at the dark behind the hills. "The deep forest. The part fetchers won't go in without leave. That's *spoken for.* Something lives in it. Answers to no House and no god."

"Then how do the fetchers cross it?" Oscar said.

"You ask. That's the rule. The one rule." A pause. "The ones who don't ask are the ones who send word they're not coming back."

It should have been the worst news of a night made entirely of bad news. And it nearly was. But Oscar, scorched and swaying and propped half-upright on a hedge with a reader's needle still cold in his memory, felt something underneath the dread that he recognized with a kind of horrified amusement as *interest.*

For the first time since the bridges, the thought of being read by another set of eyes didn't feel like a noose. It felt like the possibility of meeting someone who spoke the language.

He glanced at Wick, and wished he hadn't, because she'd gone somewhere behind her eyes. She was looking south at the dark behind the hills the way a thirsty person looks at water they've been told not to drink: the hand in her pocket, the heat banked but humming, something in her leaning toward the wild and the free thing in it before the rest of her caught up and pulled it back. She felt him notice and shut it away, fast, the lid going down. Oscar filed it under the long list of things he was afraid for her about and said nothing, because a girl's fears weren't owed to him either.

"So we ask," he said instead. "We go off the map, into the one country the House can't follow us into, and we find the keeper, and we ask leave to pass." He pushed himself off the hedge, found his feet, found they mostly held. "It's the only road left that isn't theirs. I'd rather owe a toll to something old and honest than walk back through a gate they're holding open for me."

"You don't know it's honest," Tarn said.

"No," Oscar agreed. "But it's not the House. Right now that's the whole of what I'm shopping for."

Kessa was already shouldering her pack and turning south and west, toward the gray-green edge of everything she could navigate and the dark beyond it that she couldn't. Cael got Oscar's arm over his shoulders again without being asked. Wick fell in close, the lid down tight, not looking at the hills and very obviously not looking at them. And Tarn stood a moment with his back to the city that had owned him for forty years, the bowl, the bosses, the debt that had grown in him while he fed it. Then he turned away from all of it for good and took the front, because of the five of them he was the only one who'd ever walked where they were going.

Behind them, Oressa woke up to find its south gate had caught nothing in the night. Somewhere a clerk would file it. Somewhere a word would go up a line and come down it, unhurried, incurious: *the loose one didn't run the cordon. Watch the roads. It'll keep.*

Ahead of them the paving thinned, and crumbled, and stopped—and the five of them stepped off the edge of the managed world into the country that belonged to something else.
