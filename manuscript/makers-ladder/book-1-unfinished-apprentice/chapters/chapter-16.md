## Chapter Sixteen — The Squeeze

They came up out of the good claim like kings and walked into a town holding its breath.

Oscar felt it the moment they surfaced: a wrongness in the air of Marrow, a quiet where there shouldn't have been one. It was past midday; the salvage market should have been a roar of haggling and barrows and the clang of the scale-houses. Instead the lanes were half-empty, and the people who were out moved fast and looked at nothing, and the red cord stood in clusters at the corners where before there'd been one bored tollman with a ledger. A town learned to go quiet around trouble the way the good claim had gone quiet around the hush.

"They don't know yet," Kessa murmured, reading the street the way she read everything. "If they'd found the claim cracked, there'd be ten times this cord, and it'd be hunting, not standing. This is something else. This is them sitting on the lid of something." Her eyes moved. "We've got a little time. Not much."

"Then we spend it well." Oscar shifted the weight of the pack on his shoulders, a fortune in old-world workings, and under it, wrapped close, the grimoire he hadn't been able to stop thinking about, and the branded board with the seal. "We go to Brida. We get her clear of the debt with the haul, we get the truth in front of the right ears, and we move before the lid comes off." He glanced at Wick. "All of us. Slowly. Looking like nothing."

Wick wasn't looking at the cord, or the lanes, or him. She'd been somewhere else since the pit, quieter than he'd ever known her, her hand drifting now and then to her pocket where the worthless grey token sat, her eyes going to middle distances that weren't in the room. She came back when he said her name, but slow, like surfacing.

"Yeah," she said. "Nothing. I'm good at nothing."

She wasn't, and they both knew it, and Oscar let it go because there wasn't time and because he didn't have words yet for what he'd felt at the edge of that pit. He filed it with the rest.

Brida's boarding house was dark in the middle of the day, the shutters drawn: the first wrong thing. The second was the cord on the corner watching it, two of them, not bothering to hide it. The third was Brida herself, when she cracked the door at Cael's careful knock. Gray-faced, her bad arm held tight to her side, her eyes going past them to the watchers on the corner before she pulled them in.

"You shouldn't have come back," she said, but she was already bolting the door behind them, already moving to the back room, the safe one. "You shouldn't be in this town at all. They've been here. Twice. Asking after the silver-haired one and his crew." She rounded on Oscar, and there was fear in her, but under it something harder. "What did you do? The whole town can feel it. The cord's wound tight as I've ever seen them, and they won't say why, and that means it's big, and you walk back in here grinning under a pack fit to break your back. What did you *do?*"

"We took the good claim," Oscar said.

Brida went still.

"We went down the back way," he said, gently, because the next part was the part that mattered. "We killed the thing that guards it. We walked out with the season's haul." He swung the pack down, careful, and worked the branded board free, and laid it on her scarred table where the lamplight could find the seal. "And we found this. On every crate." He let her look at it. "There was a monster down there, Brida. A real one. It near killed the four of us. The Tithe told this valley it couldn't be killed, that the dying was just the price of the deep. It could. We killed it in a day. In a hundred years they never tried, because a thing that ate their crews for them and asked no wages was worth more than every soul it took. Your husband didn't die because the deep can't be beaten. He died because it paid."

He watched it land on her.

"You're saying," Brida said slowly, "it was never the claim. It was them. All of it. The debt, the terms, the—" her voice cracked and she mastered it "—the going down at all. Them."

"Them," Oscar agreed. "And here's the thing about a lie that's holding up an entire town: you don't have to fight it. You only have to show people it's a lie, all at once, where they can all see. The thing that killed your husband is dead at the bottom of a pit, the thing they swore could never die. The proof's in that pack. And there's a southern seal on the Tithe's own crates that says they've been selling this valley's blood to strangers in the south for years." He let that sit. "We can take them apart, Brida. Not with knives. With the truth, said loud enough, in front of enough people that the cord can't make them all forget."

For a moment hope and terror fought it out on her tired face. Then the door came apart.

Not their door. The front, the bolted one, kicked in off its hinges with a single boot, and the dark little boarding house filled with red cord and the bright cold midday behind them, and at the center of it, stepping over the wreck of the door with a ledger still in one hand because of course it was, came Sarn.

The tallyman looked, if anything, mildly disappointed. "You made me come into the dark to find you," they said, to Oscar, in that same bored accountant's voice, the scar pulling one corner of their mouth. "After all the trouble I went to giving you somewhere bright to walk into. That's poor manners." Their eyes flicked to the pack, the table, the branded board lying in the lamplight, and something moved behind them: the sums, running. "Ah. So it *was* you, in the claim. We weren't sure. The crew that went down this morning are still screaming about it, frankly. A dead hush. The season's haul, gone." The bored eyes came back to Oscar, and now there was something almost like respect in them, which sat colder than contempt. "Do you have the faintest idea what you've done? Not to me. To this town. You've made me make an *example.*" And then, because some part of Sarn plainly believed it: "You think the Tithe is a boot on a neck. The Tithe is the only reason grain comes up this valley at all. Break the order and Marrow doesn't get free, friend. It starves, slowly, politely, with no one left to bill for it. Somebody has to be the arithmetic. I've yet to hear one of you propose who."

"Let them go," Oscar said. "All of them. The crew, the woman. This is between the Tithe and me. I'm the one your writ wants. You've matched the loop, you know what I'm worth alive. So take me and let the rest walk."

"*Oscar*—" Kessa started, low and furious.

"No," said Sarn, almost kindly, and that was the thing that turned Oscar's blood to water—the *almost kindly.* "You misunderstand the arithmetic. I don't want you handed to me in a back room by a crew of killers I'd lose half my people taking. I want you to walk out into the square tomorrow at noon, in front of this whole town, and give yourself up to the cord like a debt being paid, so that every soul in Marrow watches the marked man kneel, and learns again, in their bones, what happens to anyone who thinks the deep belongs to them." They smiled, the scar twisting. "And to make sure you come, I'll be holding the boarding-house woman against your good behavior. She fed strays. There's a price for that. You can pay it, or she can." The ledger snapped shut. "Noon. The square. Come alone, come kneeling, and she lives. Bring your killers, or don't come, and the town watches her pay your bill instead." Sarn turned for the ruined door. "Either way, I get my example. You only get to choose whose face it wears."

They took Brida. She didn't scream. She looked back once, at Oscar, and there was no blame in it. And then the cord were gone, and the door was a wreck of light on the floor, and the four of them stood in the wrecked dark of a kind woman's house with a fortune they couldn't spend and a deadline they couldn't meet.

For a while nobody said anything.

"I'm going to walk into that square tomorrow," Oscar said.

"Like hell," said Wick, surfacing all the way at last, fury doing what fear couldn't.

"Listen to me—"

"No. *You* listen." She was shaking, and it wasn't the door this time, it was just her, sixteen and furious and right. "You always do this. You go quiet and you go clever and you decide you're the one that gets spent. Like you're the cheap one. You'd kneel in that square and let them box you up and cart you south and call it the fewest dead, and it's not, it's you, you're the dead one, you just never count you." Her voice broke and she shoved through it. "We got you down off that mountain. You dragged me out of that hole *yesterday.* You don't get to kneel. You don't get to. Kessa said it, you don't let the dark have your people, you were standing right there when she said it."

"That's the crew we said we'd be," Cael agreed, very quietly, from where he stood with the warm shield still on his arm. "I'd not like to go back on it the day after." He looked at Oscar with that steady terrible gentleness. "You stood at that font and you didn't go in. Don't go into this. There's another way."

And Oscar—who had been so ready, who had felt the made thing's old certainty that he was the most spendable coin in any purse—felt something in him that wasn't despair. He looked at the branded seal on Brida's table. He looked at the pack full of proof. He looked at the deadline, noon in the square, the whole town summoned to watch a man kneel.

The whole town. Summoned. To one place. To watch.

"Sarn's made a mistake," he said slowly, and the others heard the change in his voice and went still. "A clever mistake, which is the most dangerous kind, because clever people trust them. They want a stage. They want every soul in Marrow in that square at noon, watching, learning their lesson." He started, despite everything, to smile: the real one, the dangerous one. "They've gone to a great deal of trouble to gather us an audience."

Kessa's head came up. "You're not going to kneel."

"I'm going to walk into that square at noon," Oscar said. "All of us are. With the haul, and the seal, and every word of the truth, and we are going to turn their example inside out in front of the entire town. Sarn wants Marrow to watch the marked man learn his place." The smile sharpened. "Let's let Marrow watch instead while four strangers prove the Tithe has been lying to them their whole lives, and dare the cord to silence the truth with the whole valley looking on."

"That," said Cael, after a moment, with the air of a man committing a sin he intended to enjoy, "is a hard thing very much worth doing."

"It's insane," Kessa said. "We'll be outnumbered fifty to one in the open, with Brida in their hands and a price on his head." A beat. The ghost of that dangerous hope again. "What do you need from me?"

Wick said nothing. But she'd stopped shaking, and her hand had come out of her pocket, and the look on her face was no longer somewhere far away. It was right here, fixed on Oscar, fierce and frightened and entirely present.

Outside, the cord watched the broken door, and the town held its breath, and somewhere across Marrow a square was being readied for an example.

It was going to get one. Just not the one anybody had ordered.
