## Chapter Fourteen — The Descent

The good claim had a reputation, and the way down to it earned every word.

Kessa took them by the back route, the one the Tithe didn't watch because the Tithe didn't know it was there: a fault in the old city's bones that Oscar read open ahead of them turning by turning, hand to the cold stone, the dead grammar unfolding the safe way down through a place that had a great many unsafe ones. They had what they'd decided: take the deep haul, take the proof, prove the thing could be done. Kessa had said it plainest, before they started.

"People go down to the good claim," she'd said. "Fewer come up. The ones that don't, mostly, are still down there. So watch where you step. You'll be stepping on them."

She had not been speaking poetically.

The first body was a quarter-mile down, folded into a side-niche as if it had crawled there to be out of the way of its own dying. Salvager's leathers, gone stiff. A pick still in one hand. Wick made a small sound and then made herself stop. Cael stood over the dead stranger a moment with his head bowed and his lips moving, the small private mercy he did for everything that had stopped living, and then they went on.

There were more after that. The good claim had been eating crews for a long time.

It was the third body that changed the shape of the day.

This one had died sitting, back to the wall, in a wide gallery where the old workings in the walls had begun, faintly, to wake. A salvager in better gear than the others, and propped against their knees, half-fallen, was a shield. Not a salvager's buckler. A real shield, broad and old, the metal of it dark and strange and worked all over with a fine raised tracery that Oscar felt pull at his attention from twenty feet away.

He crouched and looked at it, and the cold legible shine came up behind his eyes, and he read it, and went still.

"This isn't dead work," he said. "It's sleeping. There's a whole figure in it, intact. A good one, better than good, better than anything I could write." He ran a finger just above the tracery without touching it, following the shape of the working the way you'd follow a line of beautiful handwriting. "It's a ward. A real one. You set it, you feed it, and it raises a wall of warding around the anchor: stops a blow, stops a fall of stone, stops most things that come at you with force behind them." He sat back on his heels. "It's been asleep for an age because there's been no one to wake it. The charge it held ran out a hundred lifetimes ago. It just needs power, and a hand to give it." He looked up—not at himself. At Cael. "And it wants a protector. I can read that much in how it's made. It was written for someone whose whole purpose was standing between."

He lifted it free of the dead salvager's stiff hands, which took some doing, and held it out. "This one's yours."

Cael took it the way a man takes something he's afraid to want. It was heavy; he was not. He turned it, and looked at the dark old tracery, and then, because Oscar told him how, low and careful, he closed his eyes and did the thing his faith had taught him, reached for the warm granted strength that came to him when he asked it to protect, and he *poured it in.*

The shield woke.

The tracery filled. Not with the cold blue-white shine that the old work always gave under Oscar's hand, but with a deep and living *warmth,* a glow the color of banked coals, of lamplight under a door, of a hearth at the end of a hard cold road. It spread up the dark metal and hung there, steady and golden, and the air around Cael went suddenly close and safe, and Wick actually stepped toward it the way you step toward a fire on a freezing night.

"Huh," said Oscar, with the particular delight of a man watching his understanding of the world get larger. "It ran cold for me. It runs warm for you. Same figure: the relic's only the shape of the thing; the power that fills it is yours, and yours comes with the temperature of what you are." He was almost laughing, here in a tomb, over a dead stranger's shield. "I have spent a very long time thinking I understood how this all worked. I love being wrong about it. Genuinely. It's the best thing that happens to me."

Cael wasn't listening to the lecture. He was looking at the warm shield, and then down at the salvager he'd taken it from, and the joy went out of the moment for him in a way it took Oscar a beat too long to follow.

"It didn't save them," Cael said quietly.

Oscar watched Cael understand that, and watched him decide to carry the thing anyway: a man who would shoulder a shield *because* it had failed the last hand that held it, as if to argue with the fact.

Kessa, meanwhile, had been doing what Kessa did, which was robbing the dead with brisk unsentimental respect, and she came up from a fourth body, red cord at the wrist, a Tithe man, gone the same way as all the rest, holding a pair of boots.

"Tithe crew," she said. "Their own. The good ones get the good gear, and these are old-world made." She turned them over; the soles were of some dark supple stuff, seamless, strange. "Quiet-soles. You walk in these and the ground doesn't hear you. The Tithe works the claim in silence, remember: wraps their boots in rag. These poor fools didn't need rag." She was already pulling off her own worn boots. "Didn't help him in the end either. But it might help us get past the thing that got him." She laced them, stood, took two steps—and made no sound at all, none, a hunter's tread gone utterly silent on old stone. Something that was almost satisfaction crossed her face. "Well. That's unfair. I like it."

"You're robbing his grave," Wick observed.

"He's robbed half this valley from up top," Kessa said. "I'm taking his boots. Move."

They moved, better armed than they'd come, and quieter, and more afraid.

The spiders came at them in the dark below the galleries, and the spiders were running.

Oscar understood that almost at once, which was the only reason the first rush didn't end someone. They came not as hunters but as a flood: a dozen of the pale bone-legged things, the kin of the one that had nearly taken Wick, pouring up out of the deeper dark in a panic, and only turning on the warm living crew because the crew was in the way and warm and there. They hunted by heat. Four bodies and a girl who burned hotter than all of them, in the cold, lit them up like a bonfire, and a fleeing thing that runs into food does not keep running.

"Down!" Kessa had a bolt away before the word finished. "Cael—the shield, now, give us a wall—Wick, *you do not light up,* you'll call every one of them and worse besides—"

Cael drove the shield's edge down into the grit and poured himself into it, and the hearth-dome bloomed: a half-sphere of warm golden warding, ten feet across, and the first spiders hit it and could not come through, their bone-pale legs scrabbling at a wall of force they couldn't see and couldn't pass. It held. It *held,* and for one breath the crew had a fortress in the dark.

But the dome didn't move, and it didn't tire the spiders, and there were a dozen. The shield bought time. It didn't spend the threat.

"We can't wait it out," he said. "Cael holds the dome. The rest of us go out and end this, fast, before he's empty. Kessa, left. I'll take right. Wick—" he caught the look on her face, the terror and the awful straining want "—Wick, you've got the cleanest aim of any of us, so you make it count. Small. Aimed. One at a time. Nothing past what you can hold—" he held her eyes "—nothing more than that. Like the street."

She heard him. He watched her hear him. And then she reached for what she could hold and *only* that, no further, not a spark past it, and she put a tight lash of fire through one spider's body, clean, and it dropped, and she didn't spill, and she didn't break, and her hands stayed her own.

It was, all told, a short and ugly and well-fought thing. Oscar took the right with the lance and the Measure, the lance throwing them off their feet, the blade finishing what the lance started, and found, with a grim private satisfaction, that the figures came when he called them now, every time, the way they were supposed to. Kessa flowed along the left in her stolen silent boots, a bolt and then a blade, never where the spiders expected the warmth to be. Wick burned them down two and three at a time, aimed and aimed and aimed. And Cael knelt in the center of it with the hearth blazing golden around them all, jaw clenched, pouring out his strength, the still warm point the whole fight turned around.

When it was done, the gallery was littered with pale dead, and Cael let the dome fall and sat down hard, grey-faced and emptied, and Wick went and sat against him without a word, and they breathed.

"You held us," Oscar told him.

"It held us," Cael said, one hand flat on the dark warm metal, and didn't argue further.

They did not talk much after that. The spiders had been running from something, and they were going the way the spiders had come.

The deeper they went, the more the old workings woke, the walls veined now with live seams that pulsed faint and slow like something sleeping, the richest the buried world had to offer. And the quieter it got. Oscar found himself setting each foot down with care, found his breath going shallow, found that the silence here was not the absence of sound but a held thing, a pressure, the quiet of a room where something is listening. He raised a hand. They stopped. He pointed at his own feet, then at his own ear, and the crew understood: *from here, we are not heard.* Kessa, silent-shod, took the lead. The rest of them learned to walk like ghosts.

And then the bones opened up into the good claim, and they saw what kept it.

The chamber was vast: a cathedral of the old world, walls dripping with live workings, a fortune in light, the haul Brida's husband had died for stacked and glowing at the far end. And between, on the floor of it, among the scattered bones of everyone who had ever come this far, the thing the Tithe crews called the hush lay waiting, and it was no beast.

It was a monstrosity.

It was vast and low and pale, the grey-white of a thing that had never needed color, bigger than a house, and it had no eyes anywhere upon it because it had long since had no use for them. There was only, where a face should have been, two great trembling fans of membrane spread wide to drink the dark for sound. Its hide was not hide. It was armor, and the armor was *made of the dead:* picks and shovels and salvager's iron, ribs and skulls and the long bones of crews beyond counting, all of it swallowed and fused and grown into the thing's grey flesh over an age of feeding, a carapace of everyone it had ever eaten, worn like plate. And its whole vast front, where the worst of any animal's hunger lives, was a *maw*—a slow grinding cavern of a mouth, ringed in plates that turned against each other like millstones, wide enough to take a person whole and never hurry about it.

It made no sound. It had spent ten thousand years learning to make no noise, so that the only noise in its dark would ever be the prey.

The haul was thirty feet past it. There was no way to the haul that did not go through it. And it had not yet heard them, because they had not yet, by some grace, made a sound it could love.

Oscar looked at the membrane fans, and the seam beneath them, and the millstone maw, and the carapace of the swallowed dead, and the long silent patient bulk of the thing between his crew and everything they'd come for, and he understood that the plan he'd half-formed up above was about to meet the place where plans go to be proven wrong.

He raised one hand, slow, and the crew gathered close, and in the breathing dark at the edge of the good claim, they made ready to wake the hush.
