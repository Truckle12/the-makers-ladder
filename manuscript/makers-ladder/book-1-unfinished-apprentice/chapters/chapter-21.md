## Chapter Twenty-One — The Cull

The country stopped pretending to be on anyone's side three days south of the camp where Wick had handed Oscar the first piece of her fear.

They'd been climbing a long bare ridge since morning, the green country falling away behind them and a harder, older land rising ahead—stone bones breaking through the turf, the makers' work everywhere now, not buried under ice the way it had been in the north but lying open under the southern sun, weathered and silent and vast. And at the top of the ridge the road did a thing roads do when they reach a place people are afraid of: it split.

The right fork was new-cut and well-kept and ran down toward a gate. A real gate—a House toll-house, squat and clean, with a banner and a brazier and a short queue of wagons, and beyond it the road carried on smooth and safe and southward through a cutting in the hills. The left fork was old. It was the maker-road itself, the original, dead straight and dead level, running into a deep notch in the ridge where the stone bones clustered thick, and across its mouth someone had long ago set a leaning post with a board nailed to it, and the board said, in faded paint, in three languages, the same short word.

Kessa read it. "*Cull.*"

The toll-keeper had come out to watch them choose, a bored man with a ledger, and he called up the slope, friendly enough. "Toll road's two bits a head, and your papers. Quick and clean, in by dark. Or you take the free road, but it's free for a reason. Folk call it the Cull. Old maker-place in the notch, still lit, still mean—it'll let some through and keep some, and it don't take coin or papers, just whoever's slow." He shrugged, the shrug of a man who'd watched the choice made a thousand times. "Papered folk take the toll. Everybody else takes their chances with the old gods' leavings. Your road, friends."

*Papered folk take the toll.* Oscar looked down at the clean House gate and its short polite queue, at the brazier and the banner and the ledger waiting to write down every face that passed, and understood the country in one cold glance of his own. The safe road was the House's road. It cost coin, which they had, and papers, which they did not—and even if they'd forged papers, it cost a slow walk past a brazier and a clerk and a board where, for all Oscar knew, a description was pinned of a silver-haired man with a young face. The toll road was a net with manners. And the only alternative the country offered the un-papered, the marked, the poor, the running, was a notch in the hills the locals had named for what it did to the slow.

Nobody was chasing them. They didn't need to. The road forked, and one tine was watched and the other killed, and the country quietly funneled everyone it didn't want toward the teeth. Corwen had told them as much in his note and Oscar hadn't fully heard it until now: *there is nowhere else the road goes.* He'd meant it about the whole south. Here it was, made small and literal, painted on a leaning board.

"We can't take the toll," Kessa said. Flat. She'd done the same arithmetic. "Brazier, clerk, a look at every face. That's exactly the kind of slow, careful look we can't sit still for."

"No," Oscar agreed.

Wick was staring at the board with the word on it. "So we take the one called *Cull.*"

"We take the one called Cull," Oscar said, "and we don't be slow."

---

The notch swallowed the daylight by degrees, and the working woke when they were well inside it, and Oscar felt it before it fired, because feeling the shape of made things before they bit was the one talent that had never once let him down.

It came up out of the stone all around them as a *pressure*—the deep current gathered and braced and aimed, an old, old figure folded into the walls of the notch and still running after centuries the way a heart runs, without thought, without mercy, without the faintest idea that the makers who'd set it here were dust and the war it guarded against was over. He'd half-expected a trap, a deadfall, a pit. This was worse and stranger and, the instant he read it, horribly familiar.

"Down," he said, and then it fired, and he knew the working the way you know your own voice played back wrong.

It was the lance.

His lance—the first figure he'd ever made, the one that had failed him in a buried tower and worked at last in Marrow, the bolt of written force that flew true and struck hard. This was that, and it was that the way a war-galley is a rowboat: the same figure, the same clean hateful logic, scored vast and deep and permanent into the bones of the world by hands that had built it to kill for a thousand years without tiring. A bolt of cold force lashed down the notch from a seam in the high stone, faster than a thrown spear, and blew a fist-sized crater in the road where Cael had been standing a half-second before he threw himself flat.

"It's a battery," Oscar said, flat on the cold stone, his heart going like a hammer, his reader's eye already racing over the working because that was the only thing that was going to get them out. "Defensive. It fires at movement—down the length of the notch—" another bolt, from a different seam, and a third, the air cracking with them, the stone smoking where they struck "—in a *sweep,* there's a rhythm, it's old and it's stupid and it can't think, it just *sweeps*—"

He read it the way he'd read the tree and the hush and the lamp, only faster than he'd ever read anything, because the cost of a slow read here was one of them in a crater. And it gave itself up to him, because it was old and simple and built before subtlety: the battery fired in a sweeping sequence, seam to seam down the notch, a wave of bolts that crossed the road in a pattern as fixed as a millwheel—lethal where the wave was, and for a breath behind it, dead empty. There were gaps. There was a rhythm. A man who could read the rhythm could walk a crew through the empty water between the waves.

"On me," he said. "When I say, and only when I say, and *fast.* It can't see. It can't think. It only sweeps. We go behind the wave." He found the count of it, the awful patient millwheel beat of a thing that had been killing the slow in this notch since before his oldest borrowed memory. "Now—*move*—"

They moved. He took them down the notch in lurches, a dozen frantic strides and then flat against the stone while the wave of force cracked past overhead close enough to feel the cold of it, then up and on, his whole mind given over to the count, to the millwheel, to the gaps—Kessa silent and fast in her stolen boots, Wick low and quick and holding her fire banked even now, even here, Cael bringing up the rear with his bulk that was never quite fast enough.

And on the last gap, the one that should have carried them out into the light, the rhythm changed.

It wasn't malice. That was the part Oscar would keep. The thing had no malice; it had a flaw, a worn place in a working a thousand years old, and on that one beat a seam that should have fired late fired early instead, out of sequence, a single bolt screaming down the notch into the gap that was supposed to be safe—into the gap where Cael was, a half-step behind, too slow, his slow kind body that had spent its whole life a half-step toward other people's danger now a half-step short of his own.

There was no time to think and Oscar didn't. He had failed this figure twice on the open road and gone cold and gasping for the failure, and there was no part of his mind that had time to remember that, which was, he understood later, the only reason it worked. He flung out his hand and he *wrote*—the guard, the brace, the snap of force between a man and the blow he couldn't dodge—and where two days ago the figure had come apart in his fingers, now, with Cael's death a finger's breadth and a heartbeat away, it held.

It came into being in the air in front of Cael as a hard cold plane, edge-on to the bolt, no bigger than a door, there for less than an instant—and the maker-lance struck it and *skidded,* a screaming line of force turned a hand's width wide, close enough that it scorched Cael's sleeve and blew a crater in the wall beside his head instead of through it. Then the plane was gone, and the wave had passed, and the gap was empty and safe the way it should have been, and the cost arrived all at once.

It took Oscar at the knees. The brace had cost more than anything he'd made—more than the binding in the square, more than he'd known he had—and his reserve went out of him like water out of a cracked jug, cold and total, and he was suddenly on the stone without remembering having fallen, the notch tilting, his hands numb, his whole self scoured down to a thin grey thread. But he was awake. And down the notch, in the returned light at the far mouth of the Cull, Cael was alive, on his feet, staring at the scorched stone where his head had been and then at Oscar with an expression Oscar had never once seen on him.

They got out of the notch the rest of the way at a stagger, Kessa under one of Oscar's arms and Wick under the other, the battery sweeping its empty patient death behind them at things that were no longer there.

---

They sat in the grass on the far side a long time while Oscar's hands came back.

"You stood between," Cael said, finally. He said it quietly, looking at his scorched sleeve, at the place on his arm where the cloth had burned. He was a man who had spent years and a dead friend's worth of guilt learning to be the one who put his body in the gap, the one who held the door, and Oscar saw him struggling with the strange new shape of it from the other side. "That's—I'm the one who does that. That's the only thing I'm for. And you—" He stopped. Started again. "Thank you. The last person who held a door for me was Doss. You know how that ended for him."

"You'd have done it for any of us," Oscar said, his voice thin. "I just got there first, the once."

"You got there first," Cael repeated softly. He looked at Oscar a long moment—scoured grey, hands not yet steady, and *breathing*—and something eased in him, the way it had eased in the square, another inch of the old weight setting down. Because that was the sum he had never once been able to make come right, the one he'd built his whole penance around since the door that took Doss: that holding it was a thing you did exactly once, and did not walk back through. And here was a man who'd done it, and walked back. "Well," he said, rough. "There's a thing I didn't think I'd see. I'll have to get used to that one too."

Wick was looking at Oscar with frank professional interest, one student grading another. "That was the new one. The wall you've been trying for, that keeps falling apart on you."

"It was."

"It didn't fall apart."

"No," Oscar said, and let himself have it, the fierce small thing he'd filed away on the road for when there was a *later*—because this was the later, sitting scoured-out in the grass with Cael alive beside him and the brace finally, *finally* landed when it had to. It had cost him everything he had and it had held a maker's killing-bolt a hand's width off a good man's skull. He was getting better. It might not be enough. It was getting better, and a man could stand on that. "No. It didn't fall apart."

He looked back at the notch—at the Cull, at the old indifferent battery still sweeping its patient death over an empty road, the makers' machine that had no malice and no mercy and no idea the world had moved on, killing the slow because it had been built to and had never been told to stop. The same hands that built it had built him. His deadliest trick was a splinter of their war. And up in the sky, somewhere, was a power that looked at frightened children once and found them not worth a second glance, that funneled the un-papered toward the teeth and called it a toll road, that ran a whole country the way this battery ran the notch—without malice, without mercy, just accounting.

The makers were dust and their machine still killed. He wondered, cold under the warm sun, what that meant for the people who'd inherited their loop and their reading and their patient indifferent way of sorting the world into things worth keeping and things worth culling.

But he didn't stand to leave.

When his hands had come back enough to trust, he got up and turned the wrong way—back toward the notch, toward the Cull, toward the patient sweep of killing he could still hear cracking over the empty road—and Kessa read his face before he'd said a word.

"No," she said, flat.

"It'll kill the next ones."

"It'll kill the next ones whether or not you spend yourself into the ground over it tonight. You can barely stand." She stepped in front of him, not touching, just there. "And unmaking a maker-work the size of that, on the main road south, where every licensed reader between here and the sea would feel a working that old come apart—that's not quiet, Oscar. That's the loudest thing you could possibly do. You'd be writing your own name on this road in letters a blind man could read. *An unlicensed reader strong enough to unstitch the Cull came through here.* You think your polite friend won't hear about it?"

It was all true. That was the trouble with Kessa; she was always all true.

"The next person who can't afford the toll is a person," Cael said quietly, from the grass. "That's the whole of it, for me. I won't pretend it isn't." He didn't push. He just set it down beside Kessa's truth, the way he set his body in gaps, and let the two of them sit there together.

Oscar looked at the notch. He thought about the makers' hand that had built the killing-engine and the makers' hand that had built him—the same slope, the same patient cleverness, the same trick of setting a thing down and walking off to be clever elsewhere and never once coming back to ask what it cost. He thought about a battery that had culled the slow and the poor and the un-papered for a thousand years because it had been told to and never told to stop, and about a power up in the sky that sorted the world by the same arithmetic, and about how the one inheritance these hands had left him was the gift to read how a thing was made—

—and to take it apart.

"I know it's loud," he said. "I'm going to do it anyway. Not because it's smart. Because I can read exactly how these hands built this thing to last forever and never wonder who it killed, and I am so *tired* of being the kind of thing they made." He looked at Kessa. "So mark the road with my name. Let him read it. Let all of them read it—*here came a made thing that unmakes what you build.* I'd rather they knew that than knew nothing about me at all."

Kessa held his eye a moment longer. Then she stepped aside—not agreement, exactly, but the particular grace of someone who's said her piece and won't make you fight her for the thing you've decided to bleed a little over. "Be fast," she said. "And don't fall into it."

He went to the mouth of the notch, to the edge of the sweep where the bolts didn't quite reach, and he looked.

Not the way he'd looked crossing it—fast and frightened, reading just enough of the rhythm to live. This time he opened the looking all the way, deeper than he had ever taken it, down past the bolts and the worn flawed beat and into the working itself: the thousand-year logic of the thing, read the way you'd read a sentence in a language you were only beginning to dream in. It cost. It cost in a way that scared him, his scoured reserve thinning toward something that felt less like tiredness and more like *coming apart,* the grey thread he'd been reduced to fraying finer the longer he held the read. A season ago he could not have done this. A season ago he could read a lamp. Now he read a war-engine down to its bones—and somewhere in the depths of it he found the thing he was hunting: the keystone, the single anchoring figure the makers had tied first and hung everything else upon, the knot that held the whole battery in the world.

He set his grip to it, and pulled.

For one terrible instant the working *fought*—not with malice, it had none, but the way a structure fights, the whole vast weight of a thousand years of aimed force bearing down on the one stroke he was unpicking. The battery fired wild as it felt its center go, bolts screaming off the walls in every direction at once. Oscar held the unstitching, and did not flinch, and drew the knot the rest of the way loose.

The Cull came undone. It didn't explode; it *stopped,* the way a held breath stops. The deep current that had been gathered and aimed in those stones since before there was a south to walk to simply let go—unwound, dispersed, sank back into the cold river it had been drawn from—and the notch went silent. Truly silent, for the first time in a thousand years. The sweep was gone. The road through was only a road.

The last of his reserve went out with the working, and he would have fallen if Cael hadn't already been there—Cael, who was always there, who got to the gap first now that he'd learned it could be done—and the big man took his weight without a word.

"It's dead," Wick said, almost reverent, come up beside them to stare down the quiet notch. "You killed it."

"I unmade it," Oscar said, which felt like a different and more important verb, though he was too far gone to say why. "Anyone can walk it now. Toll or none. Papers or none." Down in the notch the light fell ordinary and clean on stone that had been a killing-field an hour before, and he thought, dimly, through the grey, that it was the first thing he'd done since the buried city that had nothing to do with running and everything to do with the kind of thing he wanted to be. "First road I've ever opened instead of run down. I think I needed to do one of those."

They camped at the mouth of the silent Cull that night, because Oscar could not have walked another step, and Kessa took the first watch facing south down the dead notch and said nothing more about names written on roads. She'd spoken her piece. She'd let him do the thing anyway. That was the crew of it.

In the morning he could stand, and they went on.

And when they came up over the next rise, the land opened, and far off down the long bright valley—white walls and bridges and a hundred thin threads of smoke, bigger than Marrow, bigger than anything Wick had ever seen or Oscar could remember—stood the first true city of the south.
