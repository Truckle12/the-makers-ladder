## Chapter Seventeen — The Example

By noon the square was full, because the Tithe had made sure of it.

They'd sent the cord through Marrow at first light, lane by lane, and the message had been simple: be in the square at noon, all of you, or be marked absent, and *marked absent* was a sentence. So they came: salvagers and scale-clerks and children and the old, herded into the open square between the great stone ribs, hundreds of them, with the quiet of people who have learned that noise gets noticed.

At the head of the square, on the steps of the largest scale-house, the Tithe had built its stage. Brida stood on it between two of the cord, her bad arm tight to her side, her chin up. And before her, ledger in hand, stood Sarn.

Oscar walked into the square at noon, and he did not come alone, and he did not come kneeling.

He came down the central lane with Kessa on one side and Cael on the other and Wick a half-step behind, with the pack of the season's haul on his back and the branded seal in his hand, and he watched the calculation stutter behind Sarn's scarred face, a sum that had not accounted for this. The cord shifted, hands going to weapons. The crowd drew a single breath. Brida's eyes found Oscar's across the square, and he saw her understand that he had not come to die for her, and he watched something in her stand up straight.

"That's far enough," Sarn said. Their voice carried. A square has good acoustics, which Oscar was counting on. "This was meant to be done with dignity. You were meant to come alone. Now I'll have to do it the ugly way, in front of the children."

"No," Oscar said, and he didn't say it to Sarn. He turned, and he pitched his voice to the stone ribs and the hundreds of frightened people beneath them, and he spoke to Marrow. "I came alone, the way you asked. Look—" he spread his empty hand "—no army. Four of us. The same four who, two days ago, walked down into the good claim, the deep one, the one the Tithe keeps for itself, the one that's been eating this valley's people since before most of you were born."

A ripple went through the crowd. The good claim. The name alone was a wound in this town.

"Quiet him," Sarn said to the cord, unhurried.

"Your husbands went down there," Oscar said, faster now, louder, to the crowd. "Your daughters. They went because they owed, and the only way to clear what they owed was the good claim, and they did not come back, and the Tithe told you why. They told you the deep is simply deadly. That the good claim takes its toll the way winter takes the old. A sad thing, a natural thing, nobody's fault, the price of living on the bones of the old world." He let the contempt show now. "And they told you that only the Tithe is brave enough to broker that bargain on your behalf. That's the story. You've all heard it. You've all *paid* it."

Two of the cord were pushing through the crowd toward him now. He had maybe a minute.

"It's a lie." He said it flat and certain and it cracked across the square. "Not the danger—the danger is real. There was a monster down there, an old and terrible thing, and it nearly killed the four of us. But it was a *thing.* It had a body. It could bleed. It could *die.*" He turned in place, meeting eyes, the whole square. "And we killed it. Two days ago. In a single afternoon. The thing the Tithe swore could never be beaten is dead at the bottom of a pit, and the proof is that their own crew went down for the season's haul yesterday morning and found it gone—ask them, they're in this square, I can see the cord that came up screaming—"

"*Quiet him,*" Sarn snapped, and the bored mask was gone.

"—and ask yourselves," Oscar roared over the cord shoving toward him, "why a monster the Tithe could have killed any time in a hundred years was allowed to go on eating your families *generation after generation!*" He had them now; he could feel it, the awful held breath of a crowd at the edge of understanding. "Because a monster guarding the richest claim in the valley was the best thing that ever happened to them! It kept the claim theirs. It kept you afraid. It kept you in debt, sending your own down into a grave the Tithe never tried to close, because your fear was worth more to them than every soul that thing swallowed. They didn't fight the monster. *They fed it. They fed it you.*"

The square erupted—not into action, not yet, but into sound, the dam of a hundred swallowed griefs breaking at once, and the cord faltered in the press of bodies that suddenly would not politely move aside.

"And here is who they fed you *for.*" Oscar held the branded board high, over his head, turning so the carved seal caught the noon light. "This is burned into every crate of the haul we pulled out of the good claim. A southern house's mark. The relics you die to dig up don't make Marrow rich. They go *south,* to buyers you'll never see, on the Tithe's own seal. You have been bleeding into the deep for a hundred years to make strangers in the south wealthy, and the red cord is nothing but their hired hand, and they have *lied to you about all of it!*"

For one heartbeat the whole square hung silent.

Then Sarn made the mistake that ended them.

"Kill the woman," Sarn said to the two on the stage. "Now. Where they can see. Remind them what the cord *is.*"

The held breath of the crowd became a roar, and the roar became a surge, and Marrow stopped being a crowd and became a tide.

After that it happened very fast, and the crew's whole job was to keep the spark alive long enough for the fire to catch.

The cord on the stage went for Brida. Kessa was already moving. She'd been drifting that way the whole time in her silent stolen boots, unnoticed at the stage's edge, and she came up the steps low and fast and put her shoulder into the first one and her knife into the gap in the second's guard, and then she had Brida by the good arm and was hauling her off the back of the stage, swearing steadily, alive.

The cord in the crowd drew steel and the crowd drew back from the steel, and for one ugly moment the old fear tried to reassert itself. Cael's thumb went to the iron at his throat, once, the old touch. And that was when he drove the hearthward into the cobbles at the front of the press and poured himself into it, and the dome of warm gold bloomed up between the cord and the people, a wall of warding ten feet wide, and the blades that came down on it skidded off light and the front rank of terrified salvagers found themselves suddenly, impossibly, *protected.* Cael stood inside his own warmth with his arms spread and his voice carrying like a bell over the square: "*They can't touch you! Look! Look at them! They can't touch you—STAND UP!*"

And Marrow stood up.

It was not clean and it was not bloodless and Oscar would carry parts of it a long time. But there were hundreds of them and dozens of cord. Once the salvagers understood, once the dockworkers and the scale-clerks and the widows of the good claim understood that the cord bled, that the cord could be *grabbed,* that the people beside them were grabbing, the red cord drowned in the tide they'd spent a generation standing on.

Oscar fought to keep the center, and found the grimoire had been worth carrying. He'd had two days and one quiet night with it, and he'd learned exactly one new figure well enough to trust: a binding, a thing that seized and held rather than struck. When three cord came at him together he scored it fast and clean and the air around their feet simply *gripped,* stone flowing up over boots like cold tar, holding them fast and harmless while the crowd took their blades. It cost him; new figures always cost more than old ones, and his reserve went thin and cold. But it held. It *worked.* He filed the small fierce joy of it away for later, when there was a later.

And Wick. Wick was a wonder. She put herself at Cael's flank and she did not flood and she did not reach for anything she shouldn't. She fought the whole battle on the controlled part of herself, aimed and precise, a lash of fire to disarm, a wall of heat to drive the cord back from a knot of children, never near the thing that waited under her.

Sarn did not die in the square.

Oscar found them at the edge of it, at the mouth of a side-lane, in the last minutes when the thing was clearly lost, Brida safe in Cael's warded light. Sarn was not fighting. Sarn was *leaving,* unhurried even now, ledger tucked away, and they stopped when they saw Oscar, and the two of them stood for a moment in the noise of a town tearing free.

"You've won a town," Sarn said. No bored mask now; just a cold clear assessment, almost respectful. "Congratulations. It's a small thing, and it cost more than you know, and you have no idea what you've actually done." They studied him. The silver hair, the unlined face, the loop on the sword. "I matched you to a standing writ from a southern house. Do you understand what that means? I was going to make a quiet sum off you. But the house that issued that writ does not lose its property to a riot in a salvage town and shrug. They'll want to know who. And I am going to go and tell them. Exactly who, exactly where, exactly what you can do." The scar twisted. "You didn't just break my cartel, marked man. You introduced yourself to the people who own it. They've been looking for you for a very long time. Now they know which hole to look in."

"Then I'll be gone from it," Oscar said, and kept his voice level, though something cold had opened under his ribs.

"Will you," Sarn said. "Run south, then, where the answers are and the house is. Or run north, into the ice, where there's nothing." A shrug, almost kind. "Either way you're running toward them or away from everything. That's the writ. That's what it does. I almost feel for you." They stepped back into the dark of the lane. "I'd wish you luck, but you've cost me a fortune. I'll settle for watching what finds you."

And then Sarn was gone, into the under-town, the way the cord knew and the crew didn't, and Oscar let them go, because chasing one tallyman through the bones while a town burned its chains was a fool's errand, and because some cold honest part of him knew Sarn was right.

When he came back into the square, it was over, and it was beginning.

The Tithe's hold on Marrow was broken, not tidily, not forever, but in the way that mattered: in front of everyone, all at once, past unforgetting. The cord were down or fled. The stage was a wreck. And the people of Marrow stood in the wreckage of their own fear looking faintly amazed at their own hands.

Brida found him. Her bad arm was worse for the day and her face was wet and she gripped his sleeve with her good hand, hard.

"My husband," she said. "You said it out loud. In front of everyone. That it was them. That it was never—never the weather." Her voice cracked and held. "Four winters I've carried it like it was a thing that just *happened.* And you stood in that square and gave it a name." She wasn't quite crying. "I don't know what you are. The cord said things. I don't care. You gave this town its rage back. Thank you."

"Thank Cael," Oscar said, because it was true and because he couldn't carry her gratitude just then, not with Sarn's voice still cold in his ears. "He's the one who stood between."

Cael, hearing it, looked up from where he knelt among the freed and the hurt, the warm shield quiet on his arm, and for the first time since Oscar had known him there was no guilt in his face at all, just a bone-deep, weary peace. *The one who stands between.*

They gathered on the scale-house steps as the sun went long, the four of them, with a freed town moving around them and a fortune in old-world workings to put into the hands of the people it had been stolen from. Wick sat close against Oscar's side, worn to nothing, her hand in her pocket on the worthless grey token, her eyes far away again now that the fighting was done. Kessa stood watch out of habit, though there was nothing left to watch for. And Oscar looked south, down the long valley, the way the relics went and the writ came from and Sarn had promised the reckoning would follow.

"He's right, isn't he," Kessa said quietly, beside him. Not a question. She'd heard enough.

"Yes," Oscar said. "We won a town and woke a giant. The people who want me know where I am now, and what I can do, and they don't lose things gracefully." He was quiet a moment, and far off at the edge of his attention, fainter than a heartbeat and nearer than it had been in days, something tugged: a bond stretched thin across a great distance, an absence shaped like a familiar that knew him, pulling, almost in reach. He breathed past it. Not yet. Soon, but not yet. "So we don't wait for them to come north. We go south. We find the house behind the seal, and the answer to the writ, and whatever it is I am that's worth a fortune to bring home unbroken." He looked at his crew: the tracker, the protector, the wild girl, all of them watching him in the long gold light. "It's going to be dangerous, and it isn't your fight, and I'd understand—"

"Finish that sentence," Kessa said, "and I'll push you off these steps."

"We're a crew," Wick said, from his side, not opening her eyes.

Cael only smiled, and turned the warm shield in his hands, and said nothing.

So they sat on the steps of a freed town while the light went down the valley to the south, where the answers were, and the danger, and the thing Oscar had spent his whole short borrowed life refusing to learn about himself. And for the first time since he'd woken in a stone basin at the bottom of the world, he found he was ready to go and find out.
