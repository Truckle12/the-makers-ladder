## Chapter Twenty — Banked

Wick had a trick now, and the trick was this: she kept a coal lit behind her ribs all day long, every day, and never once let it become a fire.

That was the whole of it. That was the thing the pit had nearly killed her to learn and the square had let her prove. You didn't put the fire out—you couldn't, not hers, not the kind that lived in the blood and not the kind you struck off a flint—and you didn't feed it either. You kept it banked. A coal, steady, warm, hers, small enough to hold in one hand if a hand could hold a thing like that, and you carried it down the road mile after mile and you did not let it grow, no matter how it wanted to, and the wanting was the part nobody warned you about. The coal always wanted to be a fire. That was what a coal was *for.* The trick was being stronger than what you were for.

She was getting good at it. She let herself think that, walking at the back of the crew with the spring sun on her neck and the green country opening up ahead, because it was true and because nobody else could know it so somebody had to. *I'm getting good at it.* Three months ago the coal had run her. Down in the pit it had nearly taken her all the way—she'd reached past it, reached for the deep, the cold roaring underneath that was so much bigger than a coal, and she'd have drowned in it if the crew hadn't hauled her back by the collar of her own self. But in the square, with the cord coming and children screaming and every reason in the world to reach deep and burn it all down—she'd held. Coal, not fire. Aimed, not flooded. A lash to take a sword-hand, a wall of heat to back the cord off the little ones, and never, not once, the deep.

She'd held the whole battle on the part of herself she could carry. And she hadn't told anyone how close it had been anyway, how the deep had been right there the entire time, patient, *interested,* the way it always was now since the pit, like a thing that had gotten a good look at her and decided to wait.

Because that was the other half of the trick, the half she didn't have a clean name for. It wasn't just that the fire wanted to be bigger. It was that something on the far side of the door her mark opened *wanted her to let it.*

She put her hand in her pocket and held the token instead.

---

The token was hers and she didn't know why.

She'd found it in the good claim haul, in the crate the others were calling treasure—the grimoire and the seal and the rest of it—and it wasn't treasure. It was a flat grey disc the size of a big coin, heavy for its size, dull as a stone off a riverbed, and when Oscar had read the haul piece by piece with that still-wrong look on his face he'd picked it up and gone quiet and then said it was *inert.* Dead. Spent or never-living, he couldn't tell. Nothing in it to read. A blank.

And she'd taken it anyway, before she'd thought about it, the way you'd close your hand around something falling. *Mine.* No reason. It didn't warm to her or sing to her or do any of the things the stories said a girl's secret treasure was supposed to do. It just sat in her pocket being heavy and grey and hers, and every time her fingers found it she felt, faintly, the way you feel a word you can't remember—that it was a question, and the question had her name on it, and she wasn't going to get the answer by wanting it.

She liked it because it was quiet. Everything else about her was loud now—the coal, the deep, the door, the thing waiting—and the token just sat there asking its one patient question and never once tried to take her over. It was restful, having something in her life that wasn't trying to be more than it was.

"You're rubbing that rock again," Kessa said, not turning around. Kessa never turned around and always knew.

"It's not a rock."

"It's a rock that's not a rock, then." A pause; the closest Kessa came to gentleness was leaving a silence shaped like *I noticed, and I won't ask.* "You sleep?"

"Some."

She hadn't. She didn't, much, anymore. But Kessa let it lie, because Kessa carried her own dead in a knife she never put down and understood, better than anyone, that some things a person held alone on purpose.

---

The crew looked different from the back.

That was a thing Wick had figured out and kept to herself, that you saw people truest from behind, when they didn't know they were being watched and weren't performing whatever it was they performed. From the back she could see how Cael walked half a step toward trouble without meaning to, always, his body deciding things his slow kind face hadn't caught up to yet—a man built to stand between, even his feet knew it. She could see how Kessa's eyes never stopped, left to right, ridgeline to roadside, counting exits in a country at peace because she'd lived too long in country that wasn't. And she could see Oscar.

She watched Oscar a lot. She figured she was allowed; he was supposed to be teaching her, after all, though what he was teaching her wasn't what either of them pretended it was. He thought he was teaching her control—*what you can hold, the safe part, not one spark past that*—and he was, and it helped. But that wasn't the real lesson. The real lesson was just watching him walk down a road that wanted to kill him with his back straight and his terrible jokes ready and his whole strange ancient self refusing, every single day, to be the thing the world had built him to be. He didn't know she was learning that one. She didn't tell him. It would've embarrassed them both.

He didn't know a lot of things, lately. That was the part that sat wrong in her, worse than the coal.

He didn't know how close the pit had been. He thought she'd reached too far and gotten lucky and learned her lesson, and that was true as far as it went, but he didn't know about the *door,* or the thing on the far side of it that had noticed her down there in the dark and hadn't stopped noticing since. He didn't know that when he praised her for holding in the square—*you were magnificent, Wick, you didn't reach for any of it*—she'd had to look away, because she hadn't told him that holding had stopped being about whether she *could* and started being about whether she *would,* about a wanting that wasn't all hers anymore. He trusted her. He looked at her like she was a person who'd solved her problem, growing nicely, his apprentice coming along.

She let him. She told herself it was kindness. Some nights, at the back of the crew with the token in her fist, she knew it was just fear—that if she said it out loud, *something wants me and I think part of me wants it back,* he'd look at her the new way, the careful way, the way you look at a thing that might go off. And she would rather carry it alone forever than be looked at like that by Oscar.

So she held the coal, and she held the secret, and she held the token, and she walked.

She was good at holding. It was the only thing she was sure she was good at. She just wished, sometimes, that being good at a thing didn't mean doing it completely by yourself.

---

It almost got away from her on the fourth day out of Hollet's Cross, and that was what decided it.

Nothing *happened,* which was the worst part. They were fording a cold quick stream at the bottom of a gully, stones slick underfoot, when Wick's boot went out from under her and the water closed over her to the waist, shocking and freezing—and for one half-second the coal behind her ribs leapt toward the cold the way it always wanted to, eager, *helpful,* offering to flash the whole stream to steam and her along with it if she'd only let it off its leash. She didn't. She caught it the way she'd taught herself, clamped it to an ember, and hauled out dripping and swearing and ordinary, and Cael laughed and gave her a hand up and nobody saw a thing.

But it had been so *fast.* Faster than a month ago. The reaching for the coal was supposed to be the slow part, the part she ran—and lately it wasn't slow. Lately the coal met her halfway, leaning toward every spark of cold and fear and temper like a dog that had learned the sound of its own leash coming off. She was holding it as hard as she ever had. It just took more to hold the same amount, a little more every day, and she didn't know why, and there was no one to ask, because asking meant telling, and telling meant—

She walked the rest of that day wet and quiet and frightened in a way she couldn't point at, frightened of nothing in the world but herself, and by the time they made camp she was so tired of being the only one who knew it that she'd nearly stopped being able to bear it.

When Oscar came to take the second watch and folded himself down beside her by the banked fire, reaching, the way he did, for some dry joke about the cold to put them both at ease—she didn't let him get to it.

"It's getting harder," Wick said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted and she let it. "The holding. Everybody thinks I fixed it after the pit—I held in the square, didn't reach for anything I shouldn't, and you all looked at me like I'd *solved* something, and I let you, because it was nice, being looked at like that." She made herself look at him, this once, instead of away. "But it's not fixed. It's just *held.* And it takes more to hold every day, and tonight it nearly got off its leash in a stupid little stream over nothing, and I don't understand why it keeps getting heavier, and I'm scared of the day it's heavier than I can lift." The last of it came out in a rush, because a rush was the only way it was coming at all. "And I'm telling you because I'm tired of being the only one who knows when I'm scared. So. There. Now you know a thing about me too."

And that was true, all of it—and it was also the smallest true thing she could have offered. She didn't give him the rest: not the door, not the thing on the far side of it that had wanted her since the pit and never once stopped, not the worst part of all, the part she could barely shape into words—that some nights the holding was hard not because the fire pulled, but because a piece of *her* had grown tired of pulling back. She handed him the weight and kept the worst of it buried under her ribs with everything else. One thing at a time, or none. It was already more than she had ever passed to another living person, and she hated that even now, even telling the truth, she was only ever telling part of it.

Oscar went very still beside her—not the careful stillness she'd dreaded, the *about to handle the dangerous girl* stillness. A different one. The stillness of a man taking a thing seriously, weighing whether to make it smaller than it was just to ease her.

He didn't. That was the thing about Oscar; whatever else he was, he didn't, not to her.

"Then we work on it," he said simply. "Not because you're broken—because it's getting stronger, and so should you, and those aren't the same thing. Fixing is for something that's wrong with you. This is *training,* for something that's true about you." He looked at her, no careful in his face at all, just the plain awful respect of one person taking another seriously. "And you stop carrying the scared part by yourself. That one's not a rule about magic. That's just the rule. You told me you're frightened—good. Now it's two of us frightened, which is the worst arithmetic in the world for whatever's doing the frightening." The corner of his mouth went. "I'm told I'm insufferably calm about things that ought to terrify me. Practically the only thing I'm good for. Stay close enough and some of it might rub off."

It surprised a small, tired laugh out of her, which was, she understood, exactly what he'd been reaching for the whole time.

She didn't say anything more. She couldn't, just then. But the coal behind her ribs had gone quiet and warm and *small*—small in the good way, the restful way, the way the token sat small and patient in her pocket—and there in the dark beside the only person she'd ever handed a piece of the weight to, she understood at last that holding a thing alone had never once made it lighter.

It had only ever made her sure she was the only one strong enough to carry it.
