## Chapter Nine — The Thaw

They came up out of the world into the world.

The bright passage Oscar had torn held just long enough: a throat of forced light up through the dying shaft, the city folding in on itself below them as they climbed, the roar of it falling away by degrees until there was only the scrape of their own hands and feet and breath. And then the passage gave onto a crack in a glacier, and the crack gave onto the open, and the four of them spilled out onto a field of snow under an honest sky, the first sky in days.

Oscar lay on his back in the snow and looked up and could not, for a moment, do anything else.

It was night, or near it, the long blue dusk of the high country, and the sky was *enormous.* He had forgotten. Days under the bones of the earth with a roof of solid rock for a heaven, and he had let himself forget that the real one had no ceiling at all, that it simply went up and kept going into a dark scattered with more stars than the dead city had held dead. Cold air moved across his face. It smelled of nothing: of snow, of distance, of a world that did not know his name and had never been written into a single long sentence by anyone. He breathed it the way a drowning person breathes air. His hands, when he lifted them against the stars, held their shape and did not fray, and the loop in the web of the right one was only a scar now, quiet, the way a scar should be.

"That," said Wick, flat on her back beside him, staring up at the same impossible sky, "is a lot of sky."

"It is the customary amount," Oscar said. "You've simply been somewhere with less of it."

But he said it gently, and he did not look away from the stars, and Wick laughed, a real laugh, cracked and tired and young, and for a moment the four of them lay in the snow at the edge of a glacier that had swallowed a city, breathing, alive, and no one said anything at all, and it was the best silence Oscar had stood inside in three hundred years.

He caught himself on the number.

It was a small thing, and no one heard it but the inside of his own skull, but it caught him all the same, the way a stone in a shoe catches you on the third step and not the first. *Three hundred years.* He had said it to himself a thousand times in a thousand rooms, the warm weight of it, the proof of him: *I have done this before, I have seen everything, I am three centuries deep and you are not.* And lying in the snow with a new heart beating in a new chest and the loop of a long, long line scarred quiet into his hand, he found the number had gone strange in his mouth. It was true. He *remembered* three hundred years; he could walk through them; the spires, the galleries, the slow accumulation of a self. But he remembered them the way you remember a road you have never walked, and he was beginning to understand, lying there, not letting the thought finish, then letting it finish, because he was tired of the cowardice of not finishing things, that the three hundred years were not, strictly, *his.* They belonged to the one whose face he wore. He was, what. A day old. A day, and an inheritance of centuries he had not earned and was no longer certain he was allowed to count.

"Three centuries," he said aloud, experimentally, into the cold. And then: "Or near enough. Give or take. The figure may want revising."

"What?" said Wick.

"Nothing. A piece of scholarship. Don't trouble yourself."

Kessa had not lain down. Of course she had not. She stood a little apart at the lip of the crevasse they'd climbed from, crossbow loose in her hands, watching the dark fissure as though something might yet come up out of it. After a while she said, not turning around: "It's not dead. That thing."

"No."

"You cut it loose, you said. Blind. Lost." She did turn then, and her weathered face in the starlight was doing the count it was always doing. "But not dead."

"It can't be killed. I'd stake my—" He stopped. "I'm fairly sure it can't be killed. It treats its own self as a draft; you can't end a thing that simply removes the part of it you've ended. What I did was take away the book it was reading. It's loose in the world now, blind to the line it followed, and that will hold it a while." He sat up in the snow. "It won't hold forever. A lost thread can be found. I should know. I spent—a long time—finding threads other people had lost."

Cael had gone to one knee in the snow, and his lips were moving, the iron token in his fist, and Oscar realized he was praying, not for himself, by the shape of it, but *for the city,* for the dead they had left finishing themselves in the dark below, a hill-and-hearth god's small mercy spoken over a grave too large to comprehend. When he was done he stayed kneeling a moment, then rose, heavily, and looked at the three of them in the snow.

"We can't stay up here," he said. "We'll freeze before dawn, and the nearest hold's a week's walk if the passes are open, and I don't fancy explaining any of this to anyone who asks." He almost smiled. "So. There's the question no one's said yet, and I'll say it, because someone should, and Doss isn't here to."

The name landed quiet in the cold. No one flinched from it.

"You came up out of a dead-house," Cael said, to Oscar, plainly, without the wariness that had been in it on the balcony a lifetime ago. "Through a door that knew your hand. You could have stepped into that font and walked out a king. You tore yourself half apart hauling three strangers into the open instead." He let that sit. "I don't know what you are. I don't think you do either. I know what you did tonight." He put out his broad scarred hand, the one that had held Oscar together while he came apart. "You're short a crew and we're short a fourth. Come down off the mountain with us. Sort out the rest after."

Oscar looked at the hand.

He had been offered a great many things across three centuries: thrones and apprenticeships and the gratitude of kings, and he had taken most of them as his due, because they were. No one had ever offered him a place in a formation because of a thing he'd done in the last hour rather than a thing he'd been for an age. It was, he thought, a much smaller offer than a throne. It was also, he found, the first one he had ever entirely wanted. And he noticed that he was about to say yes to it without knowing the first true thing about himself: not what he was, not whose memories he carried, not whether the face he wore had any claim at all on the centuries stacked behind it. He found he did not want to wait for the answers. He suspected they would be worse than the question, and that the choosing would come out the same regardless.

He took the hand. Cael hauled him up out of the snow, and did not let go at once, and the two of them stood a moment in the starlight with the dead city under their feet, and then Cael clapped him on the shoulder, the empty shoulder, the one that ached, and let go.

"Marked," Kessa said, already turning, already reading the slope, already the eyes of the crew whether anyone had voted on it or not. "Single file, in my steps, and nobody touches anything that glows. We've had enough of things that glow."

They went down off the glacier into the long blue dark, four of them, into a world Oscar did not recognize and would have to learn from the snow up, and he walked where he was put, in the steps of a mortal woman who would never trust him and had decided to keep him anyway, behind a wild girl who hummed under her breath now instead of crackling, beside a kneeling kind of man who had run out of *no* and offered him a hand instead.

That night they made a fire in the lee of a ridge, and they slept in turns, and Oscar took the last watch because he did not sleep much and slept badly, and somewhere in the cold hours before dawn, for the first time since he had come up out of the dark, he slept.

And he dreamed.

He dreamed he was somewhere warm and high and full of light, a gallery he knew the way you know your own hands, shelves to the ceiling and a window full of summer, and there was a weight on his shoulder, talons in the wool, a brush of feathers at his ear, and a dry voice saying something cutting and fond that he strained to hear and could not, quite. And he was not afraid, in the dream. He was *happy,* with a whole and easy happiness he had not felt awake. He turned to the window. There was a face reflected faintly in the summer glass, his own face, and it was lined at the eyes from squinting at small script in bad light, and scarred through one brow from a duel, and *old,* worn and old and earned, the face the water by the fire had refused to show him—

—and then he woke, in the snow, in the cold, a day-old thing with a new smooth unmarked face and an empty shoulder, and the dream went out of him like warmth through a cracked door, and left behind it the slow cold certainty, settling in him with the dawn, that the life had been real, and warm, and happy, and entirely, perfectly, not his.

He did not wake the others. He sat the rest of his watch, and watched the light come up grey over a world he would have to start learning today, and somewhere out past the edge of everything a small warm weight he could not yet reach stirred again in its sleep, closer than the night before, and was still.

"You'd hate all this," Oscar told the cold, very low, where no one living could hear. "The mountain. The company. Me, like this." A long breath, fogging. "Come and hate it to my face, you wretched bird. I'll be here. I find I'm going to be here a while."

The fire burned down. The dawn came on. And below them, sealed again under the bones of the world, the dead city kept its long silence, and kept its loop-marked doors, and kept—though Oscar, climbing down away from it, had not yet thought to count them—its cradles. Most, not all, were empty. And this was only one dead-house, under one cold mountain; the loop was an old line and a long one, and somewhere under other mountains other doors waited for other hands, and other fonts turned over slow and patient and kind, ready to make a master of whatever climbed up to be set. Not one of those sleepers had a crew. Not one of them had yet been given a single reason to refuse.
