## Chapter Forty-One — What She Gives

"Ten," Wick said, and nobody answered her, because there was nothing left to answer with.

Ten wasn't a number. She'd been cheating about that since the waystation. Six had been a number. Seven had been a number, and nine had been sort of a number, a thing you could say out loud and then keep walking with your teeth together. Ten was only the word she'd picked, back when picking words was a game, for *there is no wall.*

There was no wall.

All her life the far side had been on the other side of something. She'd never seen the something and couldn't say what it was made of, but it had been *there,* the whole time, since before she could talk: a thickness, a floor under her, a lid on the pot. You could lean on it a lot. You could put your hands over a monster's mouth in a grey hollow and lean on it with everything you had.

It was gone. Not thin. Gone. She stood at the rim of a great bright ring of stone with her boots on nothing at all, and the enormous patient thing that had wanted her since before she had a word for anything was not far off, and was not behind anything, and was not waiting.

It was standing in the room.

---

The city was coming apart in front of her and she watched it do it, because her legs had stopped taking instructions.

It didn't look like ruin. That was the part she wanted to tell somebody and couldn't. When a house burns you *know*; there's noise and heat and the roof goes in and it's the most honest thing in the world. A bridge with lamps on it stopped being a bridge, and there was no bridge, and no gap where a bridge had been, just a piece of the world that had never held one and wouldn't hear a word against it. Then a garden. Then a street with people in it — she saw them, she was going to see them for the rest of her life, one man with a bucket standing in the road looking up — and then a street with nothing in it, and then no street.

And then the light in the column went up another notch, and it reached out and struck out one of its own clerks.

She saw that happen and something in her chest went cold and interested.

The grey figure had been standing about on the pavement with a broken stick in its hand, waiting for somebody to tell it a thing to do. The column found it. And the clerk came apart at the edges and went inward, tidy and quick, exactly the way it had done to a thin old man in a bad coat at a gate that morning. And then it did another. And then it found the empty robe lying on the stone where the Setter had folded down, the fourth of Oscar, three hundred years of standing at a basin, and struck that out too, and there had been a robe and then there was not.

*It's eating its own,* she thought. *Nothing belongs. It's got nothing left to hold it to anything and now nothing anywhere belongs, including the parts of it that are it.*

And behind her ribs, in the place where she kept the door, something sat up and *leaned in.*

She knew that lean. She'd felt it at the gate when the man came apart, and she'd said it out loud to Cael with his hand on her shoulder and her wanting to be sick: *it liked that.* There was nothing to lean through anymore, so this time the leaning came all the way into her and stood behind her eyes, and her body understood the whole of it a good four seconds before any of it reached the thinking part.

Two dogs, she'd said, in a stand of trees a night ago. Two dogs noticing each other through a fence.

There wasn't a fence now.

And the dog on her side of it wanted *everything.* Not her. She'd got that wrong for sixteen years and was only working it out now, at the worst possible moment, with a city going out street by street: she'd thought it was hungry for *Wick,* the way a wolf is hungry for a lamb, because when you're eight and something is looking at you the only thing you can imagine it wanting is you. It didn't want her. It wanted *everything,* and she'd only ever been the handle on the door.

And the other dog, the one standing in a ring of stone throwing the world away one bridge at a time —

That one wanted everything too.

Only it didn't want to *have* it. It wanted it *gone.*

"Oh," Wick said.

---

She turned around, and they were all looking at her, and that nearly finished her before she'd started.

They were so *small.* That was what got her. Four people and a bird on a plain of white stone the size of a village green with the whole sky bending down into a basin behind them, and Cael on his knees in the middle with his palms flat and the gold coming up out of him low and steady, and Tarn standing over him with his fists hanging, and Kessa out in front of the three of them, soot to the wrists, Doss's knife low in her fist, already moving, already coming for her.

And Oscar. With an owl on his shoulder. White in the face and thinned out to nothing and holding onto a plinth to be upright at all, and his eyes going over her, hunting for the made part, the seam, the bit he could get a hand on.

He wasn't going to find one. He never had. His looking had never once got a thing off her and never would, and he'd told her so himself, on a bad night before a worse morning, and she had always sort of liked that about it. Right now it was the worst fact in the world.

Up the slope behind them a whole terrace went.

She looked at Cael instead, because she couldn't look at that.

She could feel his fire from twenty feet. She could feel *fires;* it was the only thing she'd ever been able to do without being taught. And it hadn't dimmed — he'd said so and he'd been telling the truth, not a flicker in it, the same clean gold it had been in a cold room a hundred miles north.

The fire was fine. *He* wasn't.

She could feel how much of him was left, and it wasn't much. An inch in the bottom of a cup. When the cup went, the warm would go out, and the city would come straight back in and start taking words out of the middle of their heads, and it had already had *door* off Tarn and that was never coming back.

That was her clock, and nobody had given it to her. She just had it, the same as she had the heat and the mark and everything else nobody had ever taught her.

"Kessa," she said. "Stop. Stop *there.* I need to say a thing and I'm going to say it badly."

Kessa stopped, six feet off. She did not put the knife away.

"Say it fast."

"The thing behind my door wants everything." Wick's hands had started shaking and she let them. "That thing in the water wants everything gone. They're both — they're the same *size.*" She heard how stupid it sounded and kept going because there wasn't time to find better words and she'd never had better words in her life. "It's a fight and it isn't ours. We're not even in it. Everything we've got is a stick and it's a *tide,* Oscar said so, I heard him say so. He said there's nothing in the world that answers this."

"I did say that," Oscar said. His voice came out wrong.

"So don't use the world."

---

Nobody spoke. Out past the plain's edge a colonnade she'd walked under an hour ago stopped having been built.

"No," Oscar said.

"Oscar — "

"*No.*" He came off the plinth and got two steps and had to stop, and the owl shifted on his shoulder and screamed, which is a thing barn owls do and which nobody who has not heard one at close range in the dark has any business having an opinion about. "No. Absolutely not. I saw where you were going the moment you left the fire and I have spent the whole walk over here failing to find the sentence that stops you, so I'll use the ugly one. You are *sixteen.* I have spent this whole road being careful with you and it is the only thing I have done since the ice that I'm not ashamed of and I am not going to spend you now. Not for a city. Not for me. I would rather it took the lot of us in order."

"That's not the deal though," Wick said.

"I'm aware of what the deal — "

"It's not going to *stop*, Oscar." She was shouting and hadn't decided to. "You said it. It goes over the city and then the valley and then it keeps going, forever, politely. That's what you said, you said *politely*, I've got that word stuck in me now and I'm going to have it forever." Her voice cracked straight through the middle. "There's no version where it takes us and stops. There's only which of us it takes *first.* And I'm the only one of us it can't have, because I'm the only one with a — "

She got stuck on the word. Not the way Tarn had. She just didn't want to say it out loud in front of all of them, because it was hers.

"With a door," she said.

And then she watched him do the thing.

His eyes went somewhere else. It took about a second and a half. She'd watched him do it in a market and in a pit and over a book, and she'd always thought it was the cleverest thing about him, and had wanted, in a way she'd have died before saying, to be able to do it herself one day. His eyes went off and came back with a number in them.

She saw him get the number. She saw him check it. She saw him hate it.

"Say it out loud," Kessa said, not looking away from Wick. "You promised me you'd say things out loud."

"She's right," Oscar said.

It came out of him in pieces, and the four heartbeats after it were the quietest thing that happened on that plain all night.

---

What he did next was the worst kindness anybody ever did her and she never got to tell him she knew that.

He didn't argue any more. He didn't make a speech. He stopped being the man who'd been shouting at her and turned into the other one, the flat careful one who took notes, and he came the rest of the way to the rim and stood beside her and looked into the basin with her, and said, in a voice with all the shake pressed out of it:

"Then don't do it here."

"What?"

"Here is the *rim.*" He pointed, and his hand was steady, which she thought about afterward, wherever afterward was, and could not forgive and could not stop being grateful for. "If you open — if you do it standing here you'll get a spill. You'll take a piece off it. It'll heal around the hole and keep going and you'll have gone for a piece." His arm moved out over the water. "There. That's the seat-stone. Everything hangs off that. Every thread that runs out into every lamp in that city starts on that stone and there is *nothing* in this engine that doesn't go through it. If you're doing this, you stand *there,* and you get the whole of it at once, and it doesn't get to heal."

Wick looked at the flat pale stone at the middle of the ring with the whole standing column of the world's cold light coming up through it.

"That's really far," she said. It came out very young.

"It's eleven paces."

"It's really far, Oscar."

"I know." And then his composure came off him all at once, in one piece, like a lid: "I know it is. I'm sorry. I'm standing here telling a girl where to put her feet so that it works properly and I am going to be doing that for the rest of my life, every night, at about three in the morning, which is the hour you and I both do our worst thinking. Wick — "

"Don't." She had her fists jammed against her sternum and the heat was coming up off her in a way that made the air over the stone stand up and bend. "Don't. Please. If you're nice to me for one more sentence I'm going to sit down and cry and then the man with the bucket dies for nothing."

Oscar shut his mouth. It was, she thought, probably the hardest thing he'd ever done.

---

Kessa got her left hand round her arm. The knife stayed in her right, down and forgotten, which Wick had never once seen happen.

She'd known it was coming and had no plan for it, because you don't plan for Kessa, you just find out afterward what she'd already decided. The grip closed above her elbow and it was not gentle and it was not going to open.

"No," Kessa said.

"Kessa."

"No." The flat voice. The one that read ground and never lied. "You don't get to. I've had four of you since the ice and I've got the count and I am not writing it down at *three.* You want to know what I do? I keep people out of the dark. That's the whole of the job. That's the only thing I'm any good at and I've done it badly twice and Doss is *dead*, so no, Wick. No. Find another way. Somebody find another way, I don't care who, I'll take any way at all — "

"You don't let the dark have your people," Wick said.

Kessa's whole face went.

"That's not — "

"It's got all of you." Wick was crying now and it made her furious, it always did, she'd never once managed to be angry without leaking. "Kessa, look at it. It's *got* all of you. Right now. It's just doing it slowly and in order and being polite about it, and I am the only one standing here that it can't finish, and if you hold onto my arm for one more minute it's going to have four of you *and* me, and then who's got the count?"

Kessa did not let go.

Kessa was never, in her whole life, going to let go, and Wick knew that, and knowing it was the reason she did the thing she did next, and she was never afterward able to work out whether she had done it on purpose.

She stopped holding. For a second. Less than a second.

The coal came up off her like the door of an oven coming open, and Kessa's hand came away with a sound Wick would rather have been struck out of the world than remember, and the tracker went back two steps with her left palm turned up and the shape of a girl's arm burned across it, and looked at Wick with her mouth open.

"I don't know if I meant that," Wick said. "I'm not going to lie to you. I don't know if I let go or if I *made* it and I'm never going to know now."

"Wick — "

"I'm sorry. Kessa, I'm so — "

"Come here," Kessa said.

And she did. She went into her, hard, and Kessa's arms came round her with one hand held out stiff and useless to the side, and it lasted about three heartbeats, and it was the only one of those Wick had got since she was eight years old.

"You are mine," Kessa said into her hair, absolutely level, "and I am going to come and get you."

"Okay."

"I don't care where it is. Ground talks. Everything's somewhere and every somewhere has ground and I have never once been lost." Her voice did not move at all, which was how Wick knew. "You hear me? You're not gone. You're *taken.* That's a different word and it's the one I'm using."

"Okay," Wick said again, because it was all she had.

---

She went back into the middle of them, because Cael couldn't come to her.

He tried. She saw him get one boot under him and she saw what it cost, and she got there first and put her hand flat on his big shoulder and pushed him back down onto his knees, which she would never have dared do on any other night of her life.

"Don't. Keep it lit. They need it and I don't."

He looked up at her. His face was grey and there was sweat running off his jaw and he was still, somehow, warm to be near, the same as a room is warm.

And he opened his mouth to say a thing, and nothing came out.

She'd heard him do it for Doss, and for a woman in a burned village whose name he hadn't even known. He always had the words. He was the only person she'd ever met who *always had the words* — a short plain thing, three lines of it, always the same shape, and the first time she'd heard it she'd thought it was a spell and had been disappointed to learn it wasn't.

He didn't have one for this. He sat there in his own gold with his mouth open and his eyes full and no words in him anywhere.

"It's all right," Wick said. "You don't have to have one. Honest. There isn't one for this, and if you make one up now I'll know, and then I'll have to go through with all *that* in my head as well."

"Wick — "

"Also don't." She stepped back out of the reach of him, out of the warm, and it was like stepping out of a house in winter. "Don't put it on me. Not now. I can feel you wanting to." Her chin was going. She set it. "You keep making me feel like a person, Cael, it's the whole thing you do, it's why everybody follows you around. Don't do it now. I need to be the hole in the wall for about a minute and I can't be a person and a hole at the same time."

Cael made a noise she had never heard a man make.

Then he put his palms flat on the stone and put his head down and kept the fire lit, because she'd asked him to, and that was what he had.

---

Tarn was last, and Tarn was easy, and she'd known he would be.

He didn't grab her and he didn't argue. He stood over Cael with his ordinary weathered face and his hands loose and the pit in his eyes, forty years of it, a man who had counted every single thing that was ever owed to him and never once taken the drop that wasn't.

"It's a good trade," he said.

"Tarn," Kessa said, dangerously.

"It's a *good trade.*" He didn't raise his voice. He never did. "She knows it. I know it. Everybody here knows it and only one of us is willing to say the number out loud, and she's stood in this crew longer than I have and she's earned a true answer, so: aye. It's good. It's the best trade I've seen anybody offered in my life and I've been offered some filthy ones." His jaw worked. "And I hate it. Write that down as well."

"Thanks," Wick said, and meant it, and he was the only one she said thanks to.

He nodded once. Then he looked past her at the basin, and at the light standing up out of it, and something went across his face and stopped.

"Go on then," he said. "Open the — "

And he stopped.

She watched him go after it. She watched a man reach into the middle of his own head for a word that had been there since he was two years old and find a clean flat nothing where it had been, and she saw him understand, again, freshly, that it was not coming back.

"The thing in the wall," Tarn said. "The thing you go through."

"Door," Wick said. "It's called a door, Tarn."

"Door," he said. He tried it a couple of times, quietly, testing it, the way you press a tooth. It didn't stick. It was never going to stick.

"That's all right," she said. "I'll keep saying it."

And then Cael went out.

Not the fire. *Cael.* He folded down sideways off his knees with no more noise than a coat coming off a hook, and Tarn was there before Wick had finished turning, one hand in the back of the big man's collar and one under his arm, taking the whole weight without a word, because holding a beaten man up off a floor was Tarn's actual trade and always had been.

The gold went with him. It didn't gutter. It stopped, the way a room stops being a room when the fire's out, and the cold written thing that had been at all of them since the drain came straight back in through the gap.

"Six," Kessa said. And then, with the bottom out of her voice: "That's wrong. How many are we. I had it. I've had it all night — "

"Five," Oscar said. "Kessa, five." And then he stopped dead with his mouth open, hunting, and Wick watched the city reach into him and lift something out, and watched him decide not to say what.

Nobody had to tell her the goodbyes were finished.

---

Eleven paces.

She went into the ring at a run, because she had stood still long enough to lose her nerve twice already. The water in it wasn't water. It came up round her boots and it wasn't wet and it wasn't cold and it went through her leathers and her skin and her shins as if none of them had ever been the point. Light. Just standing light, going up. She'd been afraid of it for two days from a long way off and up close it wasn't even *interested* in her. That was the thing about the whole town; it had never once been interested in any of them. It just went over you.

She got three paces before the floor of the ring stopped being reliable.

That was the runaway doing what it did. With the hand off it and nobody left to say *this far,* it had started in on its own housing, and a patch of the basin three feet across simply had never been laid, and the standing light poured down through the gap into a nothing she did not look at twice. Then another, off to her left. Then the edge of the first one went wider.

"*Stop,*" Kessa said from the rim, and Wick stopped with one boot up, because that was the one voice she had never in her life argued with.

Then, level and fast, the voice Kessa used when the ground lied: "Left foot. Small. There's a seam. Now two quick, straight at the stone — two, and *hold.*"

"Kessa, it's coming apart faster than you can — "

"I read ground for a living," Kessa said. "This is ground. Oscar."

"Working," Oscar said, in the voice of a man lifting something off his own chest.

She felt it arrive. She never could *see* what he did — that was the whole joke of being his apprentice — but the pale stone under her boot went suddenly and absolutely certain, hard in a way nothing had been for four paces, and it went on being certain in a strip about two feet wide, out ahead of her, all the way to the slab in the middle.

"That's the brace." He had gone down on both knees at the rim without noticing he'd done it. "It's the last figure I've got standing and it's the same one that thing took off me with a flick of its hand, so it will not hold, and I want you to *run.*"

She ran, and she counted the paces out loud while she ran, because Kessa had spent this whole road teaching her that the thing you say out loud is the thing that's real, and because she was terrified, and because counting was the only prayer anybody in this crew had ever taught her.

Four paces behind her the brace let go with a small dry sound, and the strip went out of true and fell in.

The seat-stone was a flat pale slab at the middle of the ring, worn smooth, with a hollow in it about the size of a chair, and standing on it was like standing on the lid of something. She could feel every thread. Thousands. Going out and out under the light and up the slope and into all those beautiful lamps and clean cracks and rooftop gardens, and all of them coming *home* to the stone under her boots.

Oscar hadn't been wrong. He never was, about made things. It was just that being right had never been the least use to him when it mattered.

She got her hand out of her pocket and had to use the other one to open it. She'd been holding the token since the gate, hours of it, and her fingers had set round it like a cramp, and she had to bend them back one at a time.

It sat in her palm, the small grey disc she'd picked up in a dead claim under Marrow because it was quiet, and it was still quiet, and now she knew why. Everything in this valley was screaming. The token wasn't screaming because it had nothing to scream with. It read as nothing. Oscar's looking got nothing off it. And the thing standing in the room with her, the thing that wanted *everything* —

It couldn't want that. There was nothing there to want.

She looked at it for a second longer than she had.

"Right," she said, out loud, to nobody. "You can't hold two things."

That was the truest sentence of her whole life and she didn't have time to notice.

She crouched down and set it in the hollow of the seat-stone, careful, so it wouldn't roll. Then she stood up and looked back across eleven paces of standing light at four people and a bird.

"Don't lose that," she said.

---

Then she let the door open, and stopped being anybody for a while.

It came in the way weather comes into a house when a window goes: all at once, all of it, no asking. There was no floor. There was no lid on the pot. Everything she had ever held with both hands and her teeth went out from under her, and the enormous glad patient thing that had been on the far side of the world since before she could talk came *through* her, using her, through the hole she'd spent sixteen years being.

And it was so *happy.*

That was the part she never told anybody, later, wherever later was. It wasn't cruel. It had never been cruel. It was the gladdest thing she had ever been near and being near it was like being near a mouth.

*Hold it,* she thought. *Hold it, hold it, don't let it slip.*

Because that was the trick, and she was the only person alive who knew it. Any idiot could let go of a door. Letting go was what it had been asking her to do since she was eight years old, sweetly, patiently, every night — *rest, set it down, you've held it long enough* — and if she let go now it would come through her and eat the plain and the four small people on it and the bridge and the bucket and the whole world in one glad swallow, because that was what it was *for.*

You didn't let go. You held it *open,* and you held it *pointed.*

Holding aimed outward. She'd learned that in a grey hollow with her hands over a monster's mouth and hadn't understood it then, not really; she'd just been too stubborn to lose the one thing she was good at. She understood it now. She stood on the lid of the world with her arms out and the door in her wide as a barn and pointed it down at the stone under her boots — *there, that, all of it, the threads and the column and the ring and the whole polite murdering machine, take THAT, not them, THAT* — and she held the aim with both hands and her teeth and the bottom of her stubborn awful heart.

She was so hot she couldn't see.

And the coal started going out through the hole, and she understood the price about a half second after it was too late to matter: a door has to be held by *something.* The something was her. All of it. Everything in her that was warm, spending out into it, because you could not keep a thing like that pointed with anything else.

Fine, she thought. That's *fine.* I've got loads.

---

The two of them met over the seat-stone.

She would never be able to describe it. She tried, later. There wasn't language in her for it and there wasn't language in her afterward either, and the closest she ever got was: it was like two dogs, except the dogs were the size of everything, and the bone was the world.

The column bent.

It had stood there all night in a perfect silent line, higher than the towers, being more power than anybody had any business having, and it *bent,* like a rope going slack, and then it began to go down into her door. Not into her. Past her. Through the hole. All that cold careful written light, three hundred years of striking out what didn't belong, a machine built to finish things — going into a mouth that had wanted everything since before there was anything to want, and being *wanted.*

It didn't go quietly. It had one reflex and it used it.

Somewhere in the middle of being drunk, the engine turned on the thing standing at its seat, to read it and decide whether it belonged and strike it out. She felt it come. It was the same pressure that had gone over Tarn and lifted a word out of the middle of him, and it came down on her with the whole weight of the valley behind it.

And it found nothing to read.

There wasn't a girl on that stone. There was a hole with the far side coming through it, and a hole isn't written, and there's no clause in the world for it and no board and no stylus and no mark. The great attention went over her and through her and past her and came back blank. Then again. Then a third time, patiently, exactly as it had gone over every crack in every street in that beautiful murdering city for three hundred years, and got nothing, and had no next step, because nobody had ever written it one.

*I told him,* she thought, from a long way down. *I can't be a person and a hole at the same time. I told Cael and he thought I was being brave.*

After that it had nothing left to try. It was an engine for reading a thing and deciding whether the thing belonged, and there was nothing here to read, nothing written, nothing made, no grammar, no clause, no seam — Oscar had told her his looking would never get anything off the far side, not ever, and now she knew why, and so did the font. It poured. It went out of true and went slack and ran, the same as the grey had run when she'd left it nothing to be hungry with.

Out past the plain's edge, a street stopped stopping.

Wick saw it. She was mostly gone by then, going out through her own hands, colder than she had ever been in her whole overheated life, and she saw the erasure catch mid-stroke and hang there and lose interest, and the beautiful pale lamps up the slope did not go out, and somewhere a long way off a bucket hit the ground and a man started running, and that was the last thing she ever saw of the world that she got to keep.

The threads under her boots went slack.

The light in the ring guttered down through her fingers and drained away into the dark behind her ribs, and the great glad thing, full, satisfied, absolutely uninterested in the four small warm shapes at the rim, turned to go back where it had come from.

And it took the door with it, because a door with nothing left to hold it can't stay open and can't shut.

And the door was her.

---

She had a second. Maybe two. She'd have liked more and she'd have used them badly.

She turned her head and looked back across the ring and got them, all four, one look, and she did the thing Kessa did, because Kessa had given her that and it was hers to keep:

She counted them.

Oscar with an owl on his shoulder and his hand out, and he was saying something, over and over, and she couldn't hear it through the roaring but she could see his mouth and it was her name. Just her name. *Wick, Wick, Wick.* Which wasn't even a name; it was a thing in a lamp, a bit of string you burn, somebody in a village had called her it once to be unkind and she'd kept it out of spite. He said it like it was a name. He'd been saying it like that the whole time she'd known him and she had never once noticed, and now there was no time left to tell him.

Cael on his knees with his hands on the stone, keeping the fire lit for the others because she'd asked him to.

Tarn with his fists down, watching, because a man who counts things does not look away from the reckoning.

Kessa at the rim with her burned hand out and her mouth shaping a word.

Four. Breathing. Hers.

"Wick!" Kessa was screaming. "*Number!* Say the number! You promised me the number, out loud, first, not after — "

And Wick, going, with the last warm thing in her going out of her and the far side folding up around her like a hand closing, found that she was laughing, because it was the stupidest question in the world and it was the only one that was ever going to be asked of her, and she gave her the true answer, out loud, first, exactly the way she'd promised.

"*Zero,*" Wick said.

There was nothing on the other side of the wall.

There was no wall.

"Marked," said Kessa.

And the ring went dark, and the plain of Everset was quiet, and where a girl had been standing there was a flat pale stone with a hollow in it about the size of a chair, and in the hollow, alone, unread and unwanted and going on being nothing at all, a small grey rock.
