## Chapter One — Surfacing

Oscar came to in a stone basin the temperature of a grave, and his first conclusion—reached with the serene confidence of a man who had been right about nearly everything for three hundred years—was that an error had been made somewhere, by someone, and that an apology would shortly be owed.

It was not a bath. He lay in a shallow cradle the length of a man, carved end to end with figures so fine they read less like writing than like frost that had decided, against its nature, to mean something. The cold had gone all the way into him—past the skin, into the place where a man keeps the private conviction that he is warm. Above, a ceiling climbed into dark he could not find the top of, and across the underside of it a thin silver script crawled like light moving beneath the surface of a frozen pond.

A working, then. There had been a working—an important one, the kind that mattered, the kind he did not explain to people who would only get it wrong. He had been mid-incantation, on a matter of—

The thought stopped. Not faded; *stopped*, the way a sentence stops when someone has torn the last word off the page.

He reached after it. It sat just behind his teeth and would not come forward, the way your own name will hide when a stranger asks for it. He had been somewhere. The spires of home, maybe, or the long cold galleries where the real work lived. There had been a reason—a brilliant, pressing reason—and a danger, and then a clean edge, and nothing past it.

He had spent his life prying at the seams between one plane and the next, and he knew what such travel did to time and memory both, which was nothing kind. He had overreached. The details would surface once he had leisure to be clever about them. Until then he would proceed, which was the chief advantage of a lie told to oneself: it kept a man moving, while the truth only sat there being correct and watching him die.

He sat up. And looked at his hands.

They were his hands—long-fingered, the hands of a man who had never lifted anything heavier than an idea when there was an alternative. But they were wrong, and he had no word for it, and so declined to look squarely. Too smooth. Unlined. *New*, the way a coin is new before the world has had its turn. He turned them in the silver light, and for one bad moment had the sense of studying an excellent drawing of his hands rather than the hands themselves.

"Cold," he told the dark. "The cold makes liars of the extremities."

The dark did not answer.

He reached up to his shoulder for Quill.

He did it without deciding to. Three hundred years had worn the gesture smooth: the hand lifting, the half-turn of the head, the expectation—older than several kingdoms—of a familiar weight already settling there. Talons finding their grip in the wool of his collar. The brush of feathers at his ear. Some small, vicious, perfectly timed observation about the state of him, in that dry rasp, because Quill had attended his every triumph and his considerably more numerous disasters and had never once let either pass without comment.

His hand closed on his own bare shoulder.

No weight. No talons. No rasp. Only the cold, and the silver light, and the sound of his own breathing in a room built to swallow sound. He held the shoulder a moment longer than there was any reason to, fingers pressed to the place where the weight should have been, as if he might find it by touch where he could not by sight.

"Off sulking somewhere," he said. The words came out level. He made sure of it. "We'll discuss it."

He took his hand back, and stood, faster than he meant to—because the alternative to standing was to keep sitting there with his fingers on an empty shoulder.

The chamber assembled around him as he rose, vast and old in the way of things made before their makers had names anyone now living would know. Cradles in rows, scores of them, most empty, each carved with the frost-fine script. The walls went up and kept going. The air tasted of stone, and beneath the stone of something flatter and colder, a mineral nothing he had no name for yet.

A sword lay across the lip of his cradle, set there the way you leave a thing where a sleeper will find it on waking.

It was his. He knew it before he touched it—a thin blade the pale grey of moonlight on still water, the hilt worn to the shape of a hand that was the shape of his. He had carried it longer than most of the cities he'd outlived. He took it up, and its weight settled something the hands had knocked loose, and as he turned it in the light a single figure caught there, struck into the steel just below the guard: a small closed loop, like a knot drawn without ends, a circle that had agreed to come back to where it began.

He had no memory of putting it there. A man forgets the small marks on an old blade, he told himself. He did not let himself dwell on the same loop, faint as a healed scar, sitting in the web of skin between his own thumb and forefinger, on the new and wrongly smooth hand.

The voice came out of the walls.

Not an echo off them. The script *itself* brightened to speak, silver figures flaring across the stone in time with the words, so that the room said the thing and the sound was only how it reached him. It was not a living voice. It had been made to be calm, and it was calm, the way a frozen lake is calm.

*"The master has fallen. The line endures. Rise, and be set."*

Oscar had been addressed in his time by gods, by archmages, by an entity that was, strictly speaking, a theorem with ambitions. He knew the voice of a thing accustomed to obedience, and had never enjoyed it.

"The master," he repeated. "Which master. I'll have a name. I'm particular about whose death I'm told of before I've had so much as a cup of anything warm."

*"The master has fallen,"* the voice said again—identically, with the patience of a mechanism that has said a thing ten thousand times and will say it ten thousand more. *"Ascend. Be set. Inherit what is yours."*

"*Inherit.*" A small cold notion had begun assembling itself at the edge of his mind, and he stepped on it, because he didn't care for the shape it was taking. "There's been a clerical error. I am no one's heir. People inherit from *me*."

The voice did not argue. What it did instead was begin to fail.

The script overhead stuttered. To his left a figure the size of a doorway guttered out and stayed out. Far above, a sound started that he felt in his teeth before his ears caught it—a long descending groan of stone bearing more than stone had agreed to, the noise a mountain makes when it begins to reconsider being a mountain. Dust came sifting down. An empty cradle split in half with a crack like a snapped bone.

The room was coming down. That, at least, was a problem with a known shape.

He raised his free hand, reached for the cold place in the air where the work waited, and scored the lance.

It should be said what that meant to him, because it was the first thing his hands had ever learned and the truest thing he knew how to do. Magic, the way Oscar made it, was a matter of reaching past the warm everyday surface of things and down into the deep cold current that ran beneath—the place where the world kept the rules it could be argued out of—and writing, with will and motion, a figure the current would agree to carry. The lance was the simplest argument there was: *here, force, in a line.* He had made it ten thousand times. He could no more fail at it than he could fail to know his own name.

He reached down for the current—

—and there was nothing there to reach.

Not a barrier. Not resistance, which he would have understood, which he could have shoved against. The deep place where the power had lived his whole long life was simply *thin*, scraped to a film, a held breath where there had always been an ocean. The figure took shape in his fingers out of habit and then sagged, starving, the force he'd called for bleeding away before it could amount to anything, and the light of it went out in his palm like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

For one full breath Oscar did not think anything at all.

Then the fear came up through him, cold and total, from somewhere far below the centuries—the plain animal terror of reaching for the one thing you have always been able to do and finding the hand close on nothing. His breath loud in his ears. His heart going wrong. *It's gone. The well is gone. I am standing in a falling room and I cannot—*

He shut the thought off. He had to. He looked at the dead hand and made himself breathe once, slowly, and when he spoke his voice only shook a little.

"That is not how that works."

The ceiling disagreed by dropping a piece of itself the size of a horse into the cradles where he had stood a breath before.

He ran. There was no art in it and no remark to make about it; he simply ran, for the foot of a great stair that wound up the curve of the wall toward the dark and the noise, and went up, because up was where exits lived.

He had climbed a third of it when the chamber sent something to stop him.

It came off the wall. He'd taken the dark mass for a buttress until it stepped down onto the stair above him and turned—a warden, stone and old script, a tall blunt approximation of a man with no face and no use for one, the figures across it glowing the same dying silver as the walls. It raised an arm that ended in a thing which was not a hand, and the air around it bent the way air bends near a furnace, except that what rolled off it was cold.

He reached again for the deep work—something heavier than the lance, a press of force to break the thing where it stood—and the thin film of the current tore under the asking and gave him a fistful of dead air and a fresh spike of that same cold fear, and the warden swung for his head.

He called the Measure.

It did not ask the deep current for anything. It asked of *him*—drew straight from the body, from breath and blood and the narrow margin a man keeps in reserve against his own death—and that well, at least, was not empty. The working came down over him like a second, sharper skin. The world slowed by exactly the degree that he quickened. The warden's strike, an instant from taking the more ornamental third of his skull, arrived instead with all the urgency of a slow afternoon, and he was no longer where it fell.

Built things fight the way they were built to fight—the same shapes, over and over. He let it teach him: downstroke, cross, the heavy overhead it favored. On the fourth beat, as the overhead fell, he stepped inside the long arm where it could put no weight behind the blow, and slid the moon-grey blade into the seam beneath its shoulder, into the one narrow place where the silver script ran thin. He did not try to break the stone. He cut the *figure* underneath it.

The arm dropped off and burst on the stair. The light ran out of the rest of it like water from a tipped basin, and the warden went to statue and from statue to rubble in a single held breath.

Oscar stood over the wreck of it, breathing hard. The Measure was already presenting its bill—a deep ache starting in the muscle, a draining at the edges—and he could feel how little he had to pay it with. The blade had saved him. The blade burned the self to buy the seconds, and there was far less self here to burn than there used to be. He understood that now with a clarity he did not want.

He let the Measure go—the world snapping back to its dull speed, the heaviness flooding into his arms—climbed over the ruin, and went on up.

Near the top, where the stair gave onto a vestibule of cracked arches, one cradle stood apart from the others, alone in an alcove of its own. And someone was rising out of it.

Someone. Rising. Both words strained at more than they could carry.

There was a figure sitting up in the cradle, reaching for the edge the way Oscar had reached for the edge—and it was coming apart in the doing of it. Not bleeding. Shedding. Where the hand gripped the stone it was going to frost, pale flakes lifting off and drifting unhurried up into the cold, and the arm above it was thinning, losing its grip on the argument for its own existence, the silver script that should have bound it flickering and letting go a thread at a time. The mouth was open on a word it could not get to the front of, a word it kept beginning and beginning and never reaching. The eyes found him across the vestibule and fixed there and held.

It reached for him. The reaching hand was already half gone to frost.

Oscar did not go to it.

He told himself the reasons, and they were even true—nothing to be done, the thing past help, the tower coming down. None of it was the reason. He did not go because something far beneath the centuries looked at that frost-shedding figure alone in its cradle and *knew* it, the way you know a thing in a dream before you have any business knowing it, and the knowing was so cold and so close that he turned and climbed before the rest of him could ask what it had recognized.

Below him, fainter now, the dying walls went on murmuring. *The master has fallen. The line endures.*

"Be quiet," he said. It came out raw.

The vestibule narrowed ahead to a single great seal of a door, round as a coin, graven edge to edge with the frost-script. When he laid his wrong new hand against it, the figures beneath his palm brightened—glad, eager, the way no lock has ever brightened for any hand but the one that holds its key. And the small closed loop on the door's face, the knot drawn without ends, flared brightest of all, twin to the one on the blade, twin to the one healed into his own skin.

The door knew him. It had been waiting for him. The cold notion he had trodden on twice now got to its feet, walked to the center of his mind, and sat down where he could not look away from it.

He did not try to name it. He was not ready to, and there was a door, and the door was open.

The seal turned. The air that came through was not the mineral cold of the tower but something older and wider—dust, deep stone, the flat enormous silence of a great space sealed a very long time.

He stepped through with the sword in his hand, expecting open sky.

He stepped onto a balcony of carved stone, and the world dropped away beneath him into a city.

A city. Underground. Beneath a sky of solid rock so high and far above that its darkness could have passed for an honest night. Streets, towers, the long pale bones of bridges; roofs beyond roofs marching into a gloom he could find no end to; all of it grey, all of it dead, all of it furred here and there with the same soft frost that had been eating the figure in the cradle. A buried city, sealed beneath the skin of the world, so old that Oscar—who was old, who had known this continent when half its kingdoms were arguments and the other half were trees—could not place a single line of it.

He stood with the dead city below him and felt, for the first time in longer than he could have said, entirely and honestly lost. Not turned-around lost. Lost the way a sentence is lost when someone has torn the last word off the end of it.

"Right," he said, very quietly. There was no audience for it and no joke in it. "Right."

Then, off to his right, a small, deliberate, mechanical sound: the dry click of a crossbow drawn to the full.

He turned his head slowly, with the care of a man who would like it on the record that he was being entirely reasonable.

A woman crouched on the broken lip of the balcony, not six feet off—weathered, motionless, unsurprised in the way of people who have outlived enough that surprise stopped being worth the effort. The crossbow she held level with his face was old, well kept, and did not move; the bolt sat aimed at the exact point between his eyes with a competence he noted, professionally, even then.

"Don't," she said, "do any of that with your hands."

He kept the hands still. He did it without being told twice, and without a word, because the part of him that had survived three hundred years recognized the part of her that had survived whatever she had survived, and knew better than to test it.

"My good woman." He found the warm, reasonable voice—the one that had talked him past border guards and customs officials and one regrettably literal-minded demon. "I think you'll find this is all a considerable misunder—"

Her eyes dropped to the sword. To the small closed loop struck into the steel below the guard.

The change in her was not large and not loud, and that was what put the bottom out of him. Her face simply closed, the way a door settles when it has decided to stay shut—and under the closing was something he had not expected to find pointed at him down the length of a crossbow bolt. Something that looked, impossibly, like grief.

"You came up out of the dead-house," she said. Flat. Not a question. "Through a door that doesn't open. Carrying *that*."

"It opened for me," he said, before the wiser parts of him could intervene.

"I know it did." That was worse than anything she could have shouted. She had got somewhere first, and she was waiting—not unkindly—for him to catch up. "I know."

Far below, down in the dead grey streets of a city sealed and silent longer than memory ran, something the size of nothing good began, without the least hurry, to move.

## Chapter Two — The Salvage Crew

Below, in the dead streets, the sound came again—a long dragging shift of weight, stone on stone, or something worse than stone. Closer than before.

The woman did not look down at it. That told Oscar more than the crossbow did. A person who could keep her eyes on a stranger while something that size stirred beneath her had already decided which of two threats she could afford to ignore, and she had decided it was not him. He filed that away. It was not flattering, but it was information, and he had survived longer on information than on flattery, whatever his enemies liked to claim.

"I'm going to lower my hands," he said. "Slowly. You'll find I'm very dull to look at once you've got past the dramatic entrance."

"Keep them up."

He kept them up.

Boots on broken stone, behind her—two sets, coming fast and unhidden, which meant they were hers. Oscar held still and let them arrive, because the alternative was to give a frightened woman with a steady aim a reason, and he had not clawed his way up out of a falling tomb to be killed by good marksmanship on a balcony.

The first to round the arch was a big man, broad through the shoulders in the way of someone built for the work rather than the look of it, a long-hafted hammer held low and ready. He wore a small iron token at his throat, worn bright by a thumb that had touched it often, and the first thing he did—before the weapon, before the stranger—was take in the whole scene with a quick, careful sweep, the way a man counts everyone in a room to be sure of who might get hurt.

The second was a girl. Sixteen, perhaps seventeen, all elbows and nerves, and the air around her hands was doing something the air around hands is not supposed to do—a faint heat-shimmer, a crawl of light at the fingertips that came and went as though she could not entirely decide to stop it. Oscar's attention went to her and stayed, because in a tomb full of cold she was the only warm thing, and warmth that leaks out of a person unbidden is rarely good news for the person.

"Kessa." The big man's voice was low, even. "What've we got."

"He came up out of the deep door." Kessa, then. The crossbow had not moved. "The one that doesn't open. Came up out of it carrying a sword with the wheel-mark on the guard."

Something passed over the big man's face. Not the girl's—she only looked delighted, in the manner of the young, who have not yet learned which doors are the ones you do not open. But the man went still in the way Kessa had gone still, and his thumb found the iron token at his throat without his seeming to notice it had.

"Ah," he said, very quietly. "One of those."

*One of those.* Oscar disliked the phrase intensely, and disliked more that two people who had never met him plainly knew a category he belonged to, and that the category came with grief attached. He had spent three hundred years being unprecedented. He was not accustomed to being *recognized*.

"I'm going to spare us all a great deal of time," he said, in the warm and reasonable register he generally reserved for tax officials and very large dogs. "Whatever the wheel-mark means to you, I assure you it is a misunderstanding. I am a scholar. A traveler. I was caught in a working that went badly, and I woke in that—" he chose the word "—*place*, with a headache and a great many questions, and the door let me out, and now there is a crossbow in my face, which I feel is an overreaction to a man who is simply trying to find his way home."

"Where's home," said Kessa.

"Evermere." He said it the way you say a thing everyone knows. The spires. The high cold halls. The galleries. "Or near enough. I'll want to make for the coast road, the one that runs under the Larchwall—"

He stopped, because all three of them were looking at him now in the same way, and it was not the look he had expected. He had expected ignorance. Frontier people, salvagers grubbing in a hole in the ground; of course they would not know the great roads, the old kingdoms, the names that had mattered. He had braced to be patient with them.

Instead the big man's careful face had gone gentle, which was worse. It was the gentleness people put on for the very ill.

"Friend," he said. "There's no Larchwall. There's not been a Larchwall in—" he glanced at Kessa, as if for a figure, and she gave him nothing. "A long time. Longer than the maps go back."

"That's absurd," Oscar said. The reasonable voice had thinned. He heard it thin and could not stop it. "I walked it. I walked it not—"

*Not.* When. The clean edge was right there again, the torn-off end of the sentence, and past it nothing. He had walked the road under the Larchwall. He could feel the memory of it, the cold of the stone, the smell of the larches—and he could not, when he reached for the *when* of it, find any ground beneath the recollection at all. It hung in him like a picture cut out of a book, beautiful and flat and attached to nothing.

He shut his mouth.

For a moment he reached, by old reflex, for the small dry voice that would have made this bearable—Quill, perched and unimpressed, ready to cut the thing down to a size a man could carry. The shoulder was as empty as it had been an hour ago. He had simply not yet learned to stop reaching for it, and there was no one left to mock him for that, either. There was only the silence of the dead city and three strangers watching him discover, in real time, that something was wrong with him that ignorance could not explain.

"How long," he said, finally, and the question came out without any of the voice in it at all. "How long is longer than the maps go back."

None of them answered. That was an answer.

The girl broke it. "Are you really out of the deep house?" She had drifted a step closer, the light fizzing at her fingers, her eyes bright. "Because the stories say nobody comes out of those. They say the ones who go down—" The big man set a hand on her shoulder, not roughly, and she subsided, but only partway. "I'm just saying, Cael, he came *out*. That's got to be worth something."

"Wick." Just the name, from Cael, mild and immovable.

So: Cael, and Wick, and Kessa. Oscar collected the names without trying to, the habit of a man who has spent centuries learning that names are the cheapest power there is and the most often underestimated.

Below them, the dragging sound came a third time, and now it had company—a second weight, and a third, a slow uneven shifting all through the streets beneath the balcony, as though the dead city were a sleeper turning over by degrees in a bed it had lain in for an age.

Kessa's jaw tightened. "It's getting worse. Whatever he is, it started when he came up. The deep place lit, and the cold ones started moving, and that was him." She still hadn't looked down. "We put him back or we put him down. Either way it stops."

"We don't know that it stops," Cael said. "We don't know it was him at all. And I am not"—the iron token again, the thumb finding it—"I am not going to stand on a ledge over a waking grave and execute a confused man on a guess. We've done enough things down here I'll have to answer for."

There was a whole history in that sentence. Oscar heard it and chose, for once, not to pick at it.

"He can't even do anything," Wick put in, helpfully, gesturing at Oscar with both fizzing hands. "Look at him. If he were really one of the—the deep things, he'd have done magic by now. They always do magic in the stories. Big terrible magic." She frowned at him, faintly disappointed, a child shown a famous beast that refuses to roar. "Do some magic. Prove you're not going to murder us."

And there it was.

Oscar looked at the girl, and at the gentle dangerous man, and at the woman who would shoot him the moment the arithmetic came out wrong, and at his own right hand, the new and wrongly smooth hand with the wheel-mark healed into the web of it, the hand that had reached an hour ago for the simplest figure he knew and closed on nothing.

He could call the Measure. He could feel it there, the one well that hadn't run dry, waiting under his skin. But the Measure was not what they meant by magic; the Measure was a blade and a body moving faster than they had any right to, and a man who answered *prove you're not a monster* by suddenly moving like one would not improve his standing here. And he had so little of it to spend. He had felt the bill it presented, downstairs, and felt how thin the purse was.

The deep work, the great work, the work that had made him what three centuries of people had feared and courted and underestimated—

He reached for it anyway. Some stubborn animal part of him simply would not believe it was gone, and reached, the way a man whose leg is off will still, in the dark, try to stand on it. The deep current was there and it was a film over nothing, and what came up through his fingers when he asked was a sputter of pale light, a guttering coin's-worth of cold radiance that hung in the air between them for a breath and went out.

It was nothing. It was the magic equivalent of clearing one's throat.

Wick's whole face lit up. "*There* it is," she breathed. "I knew it."

She had no idea. None of them had any idea what they had just watched, which was a man find out, in front of an audience, exactly how far he had fallen. He had meant to call down a working that could have unroofed a keep. He had produced a candle.

"Yes," Oscar said. The word cost him. "Well. There's rather more where that came from."

It was the first lie he'd told them that he knew, as he said it, to be one.

Kessa watched the dead light wink out, and something in her shifted—not trust, nothing so warm, but a recalculation. A wizard, however small, was a wizard. And the city was waking, and they were three, and three was not enough for whatever was coming up out of those streets.

"The way we came in is gone," she said, to Cael, not to Oscar. "Collapsed when the deep place lit. I scouted it while you two were arguing about his soul." Her eyes finally left Oscar and swept the dark below, professional, quick. "There's one other road up and out that I know of. It runs through the low quarter. Through *that*." A tilt of her chin at the moving dark. "We'd have wanted four bodies for it before today. Now we'll want luck on top."

"So he comes," Cael said.

"So he comes." She looked back at Oscar, and the grief he'd seen flicker when she first marked the sword was gone now, packed away somewhere she could carry it. What replaced it was simpler and harder. "You walk in front where I can see you. You do as you're told. And the first time you so much as look like the stories—" she let the crossbow say the rest, lifting it half an inch and lowering it again.

"My dear woman," Oscar began.

"Kessa."

"Kessa. I have spent a very long time being many people's worst day. I have no intention of being yours." He found, to his mild surprise, that he meant it. "Lead on. I'll be charming and useful in roughly that order."

Wick fell into step beside him as the woman turned for the stair down off the balcony, the heat still crawling at her fingers, looking up at him with an expression he had not been on the receiving end of in longer than he could—than he could remember, he made himself finish, because the longer he was honest about the gaps the less they could ambush him.

"Can you teach me?" she said, low, conspiratorial. "Cael won't let me near a real teacher, says my—says it's too much, that I'll burn something down. But you came out of the deep house. You're not scared of anything down here, I can tell." She said it as a compliment and as a fact, with the total faith of the young. "Will you teach me to do it properly?"

He looked at the girl trailing dangerous warmth in a city of cold, and at the empty place on his shoulder where a voice would once have told him exactly what to say, and he discovered he did not have the heart to tell her that the great wizard she'd been delivered could not, at this moment, light a pipe.

"We'll see," he said, which was what he'd always said to apprentices when the true answer was *almost certainly not, and you'll be the better for it.*

They went down off the balcony into the dead city, four of them now, into streets where the frost lay thick on doorsteps and the windows of houses stood like the eye-sockets of the long drowned. Cael went first with the hammer; Kessa drifted to the flank, soundless, the crossbow her whole attention; Wick crackled faintly in the middle like a coal that would not bank. And Oscar walked where he was told, useless and watchful, reading the bones of a city he had never seen and could not have built and somehow, in some buried animal way he refused to examine, knew the smell of.

They had gone perhaps three streets when the first of the dead stood up.

It came out of a doorway directly ahead of Cael—what had been a man, once, before the cold and the years had had their long quiet conversation with it. Grey, and rimed white, the frost growing on it the way frost grows on a thing that has held still for a very long time. It rose without hurry, the way everything down here did, joints cracking the ice that had sealed them, and it turned its furred and eyeless face toward the four warm bodies that had walked into its street, and from every doorway down the length of the low quarter, from every black window and every frozen stair, the city began, unhurriedly, to do the same.

"Right," said Kessa, very flat, raising the crossbow. "There it is."

## Chapter Three — The Dead Don't Knock

The dead of the buried city did not lurch, and they did not moan, and Oscar found he hated them the more for it.

He had met the walking dead before, in his long career of being places he should not have been. They were, as a rule, theatrical—all groaning and grasping, a great show of appetite. These were not. These rose from their doorways and their frozen stairs in a terrible unhurried silence, hundreds of them, and they came on with the patient certainty of a tide, and the only sound in the whole low quarter was the soft continuous crackle of old ice breaking off old joints, like a forest in a thaw.

"Tight," said Cael, and the word was not a question and not a comment but an instruction, and the other two answered it before Oscar understood it was an order. Kessa put her back to the big man's left. Wick crowded in at his right, the heat-shimmer round her hands flaring brighter now, fear or excitement, the two so close in the very young you cannot always tell them apart. The three of them made a small bristling knot in the middle of the street, and there was a space at Cael's back exactly the shape of a fourth person, and Oscar understood, with a flash of something that was almost insulted, that the space was for him.

He stepped into it. There was, he reflected, a first time for everything, including being filed under *baggage to be protected.*

The first husk reached Cael, and Cael broke it.

It was not elegant and it did not need to be. The long hammer came around in a flat economical arc and took the thing where its neck met its shoulder, and the husk did not fly apart the way a living man's bones would have flown; it *cracked*, all down one side, frost puffing out of the fracture in a pale breath, and folded sideways and did not get up. Cael had already turned to the next. He fought the way a man chops a winter's worth of wood—no waste, no flourish, a thing to be done before dark.

Kessa's first bolt took a husk through the eye-socket from fifteen feet, and the second was already on the string before the first had finished falling. She did not miss. Oscar made a private note that she did not miss, ever, and that this was a quality he wished to keep on the friendly side of him.

And Oscar—

Oscar reached, by reflex, by three hundred years of reflex, for the deep current, for a working that would have swept the whole street clean. He reached down past the cold surface of the world for the great river that had always answered—

—and got the film over nothing, the held breath, the candle where an ocean should be. The fear of it was getting familiar, which did not make it smaller. He shut it off and stopped reaching for what was gone.

Which left him standing in a street full of patient corpses with a sword he could barely afford to swing and a head full of useless centuries, and one thin coin of power he had not yet spent.

So he spent it on *looking.*

It cost him to do it—not the deep work, but the next thing down, the smallest true figure he had: a way of laying his attention against the world until the world showed him its stitching. It was a beginner's tool. He had learned it first of anything, the way every student learns to read before they are allowed to argue, and he had spent his whole long life outgrowing it, because a man who could move the ocean had no need to study the shape of a single wave. He had used a version of it on the warden, by feel, in the dark. Now he made it on purpose, narrowed it to a thread, and put the thread on the nearest husk—and did not yet know that he was learning his trade over again from the bottom, or that the bottom was where the rest of it was going to be built.

The world thinned, the way it does when you give all of yourself to one sense. He stopped hearing Kessa's bowstring. He stopped feeling the cold. There was only the husk, and laid over it like a drawing on glass, the work that held it up: a webwork of pale script running through the dead thing, knitting frozen joint to frozen joint—and the script was *threadbare*. Old. Stretched thin by the pulse that had woken it, pulled taut over far more body than it was ever meant to animate. There were seams in it. There were places where one clean cut would drop the whole construction like a puppet with its strings let go.

He saw exactly where.

"Cael—the next one, the seam's under its arm, the left, *there*, not the head—"

Cael did not argue and did not ask why, which Oscar would think well of him for later. The hammer dropped from the high arc it had started and came in low and short, under the husk's lifting arm, into the unremarkable patch of frost-furred ribs that held the whole binding together—and the thing came apart with half the effort the last one had taken, dropped in a single soft collapse, spent.

"Again," Oscar said. He was already looking, already moving his one thread of attention to the next, and the next. "The one on Kessa—behind you, woman, the throat, the *throat*—"

Kessa did not turn to look at him. She turned and put a bolt through the exact hollow of the husk's frosted throat as it reached for her shoulder blade, and it folded, and she was already reloading, and only afterward did her eyes cut sideways to him for half a breath, fast and flat and recalculating.

It was not gratitude. He had not expected gratitude. But it was the first time she had looked at him as a thing that might be worth keeping alive on purpose rather than on principle, and Oscar, who had been worshipped and feared and consulted by kings, found the flat reassessing flick of a frontier scout's eyes more satisfying than any of it, because he had earned it in the last ten seconds with the only thing he had left.

For a little while it almost worked.

That was the trap of it, and he saw the trap even as he fell into it. He could hold the seeing-thread on one husk at a time, and only one; the moment he tried to track two, the figure frayed and the world blurred back to ordinary and useless. One target. Then drop it, find the next, lay it again. He was a single clear voice in a fight that needed ten, calling seams as fast as he could find them—and the dead kept coming, and they did not tire, and Cael's breath was starting to saw, and Kessa's quiver was not bottomless, and behind them all the heat off Wick's hands was climbing, climbing, a kettle past its whistle with no one watching the fire.

He should have been watching the fire.

"There's too many," Wick said, high and fast. "Cael, there's too many, they're behind us now, they're—" and Oscar, three husks deep into the seeing, attention spent down to the floor, heard the pitch of her voice go past frightened and into something with no bottom under it, and turned his head a half-second too late.

"Wick—*don't*—"

She did not so much cast as *spill.*

There was no figure in it. That was the horror of what he saw in the quarter-second he had to see it: no shape, no argument, no asking the current for anything in particular—just a child who was too full of power and too frightened to hold it opening her hands and letting all of it go at once, the way you drop something too hot. A wall of raw heat went out from her in every direction without caring in the least which directions had her own people standing in them.

The husks in front of her did not burn. They *shattered*, the cold things meeting that much sudden heat and simply coming to pieces, a dozen of them, more—

—and the street went with them. The blast slapped Cael off his feet and into a wall hard enough that the iron token sang against the stone. It knocked Kessa flat. It took the frost off the buildings in a hissing rush of steam, and it ran up an old façade above them and found the ice that had been holding three hundred years of cracked masonry politely in place, and melted it, and the façade groaned, and shrugged, and began to come down.

The heat washed over Oscar and through him and he barely felt it, because the Measure had come up over him on pure animal instinct the instant before, fast enough to fling him flat under the lee of a doorway—and there was the bill again, the deep ache, the drain, his thin reserve emptying to buy that one half-second, so that when the world stopped roaring he was on his hands and knees with the grey crowding in at the edges of his sight and nothing left to spend.

Silence. Steam. The patter of small stones coming down, and then larger ones.

"Up," he croaked. "*Up*, everyone up, the front's coming—"

They got up. They were soldiers of a sort, all three, and they got up. But the way ahead was a wall of fallen stone now, and the street behind was a churn of steam already crawling with more of the dead drawn by the noise, and in the middle of the wreckage stood Wick, both hands clamped over her own mouth, staring at the place where Cael had hit the wall with an expression Oscar knew far too well from the inside. The expression of a person discovering exactly how much harm they are capable of and that they have no idea at all how to stop.

"I didn't—" she said, behind her hands. "I didn't mean—Cael, are you—I didn't—"

"I'm whole." Cael was up, one arm not quite right, blood at his hairline, but up. "I'm whole, Wick, look at me, I'm whole." The mercy in him did not even have to be reached for; it was simply the first thing out. But under it Oscar heard the strain, and saw Kessa's hand white on the crossbow, and understood that this had happened before, that the girl's power had cost them before, and that the crew was one more such moment from deciding she was a danger they could not afford in close quarters.

And the dead were coming, and there was no road out but the one through more of them than four people could fight, and Oscar had nothing left to spend.

Except the one thing that did not run on the deep current at all.

He crossed to the girl. He did it slowly, hands open, the way Cael had wanted to be approached an hour ago on the balcony, and he put himself between her and the wreckage so that the only thing she could look at was his face.

"Wick. Look at me. Not at Cael. At me." She looked. Her eyes were enormous and her hands were shaking and the heat was already building again, fear feeding it, a fire that fed on the very panic of being a fire. "Good. Now—what did you just do?"

"I—I let it go, I didn't mean to, I never mean to—"

"No." Plainly. Not unkind. "You didn't *do* anything. That's the trouble. You had a great deal of power and you gave it no instructions, and so it did the only thing power does when no one tells it where to go, which is *everything, everywhere, at once.*" The husks were thirty feet off and closing. He did not look at them. He kept his eyes on hers, because the whole game now was making his calm more interesting to her than her terror. "Power isn't the rare thing, child. You've more of it than I've seen in a century. The rare thing is the *aiming.* And aiming I can give you."

And here was the strange part, the part he would turn over later in the few quiet moments this place allowed: as he said it, he *felt* it come back to him. Not the power—the power stayed gone. But the *understanding.* The shape of the craft, the thing he had known so long he had stopped knowing he knew it, the thing that lives so deep in a master's hands that he can no longer find the words for it because he no longer needs them. Saying it aloud to a frightened girl, slowly, in words simple enough for her to catch—it put the words back in his own mouth. It made the buried thing speak.

He had forgotten, somewhere in three centuries of being effortless, that you learn a thing twice: once when you struggle to it, and once when you teach it.

"Pick one," he said. "Not all of them. *One.* The tall one, in front, do you see it? Put everything you have into a place the size of my fist, right *here*—" he reached out, careless of the heat, and laid two fingers against the air over the husk's frost-furred breastbone, where his old, returning, half-dead seeing told him the binding ran in a single overstretched thread "—and *nowhere else.* Hold it small. Smaller than your fear. Can you do that for me?"

"I don't—"

"Can you do it for *me.*" Not a question now. The voice he had used on apprentices who needed permission to be brave. "I have never once been wrong about who can do a thing. Ask anyone. Ask the wall I just hid behind."

A laugh got out of her, cracked and wet and half a sob, but a laugh—and the heat at her hands stopped roiling and drew *in*, gathered, condensed, a child holding a tantrum down to a single white point of light at the end of one trembling finger because a strange arrogant man had told her she could.

"There," Oscar breathed. "There. *Now.*"

She loosed it. One thread, into one fist-sized place, into the seam he had shown her.

The tall husk did not shatter and did not throw anyone into a wall. It simply *unmade*, the overtaxed binding cut clean at its single weak thread, and the cold thing fell down into its own frost and was a heap of rime on the cobbles, and the power Wick had been so terrified of had gone exactly where it was sent and nowhere it wasn't, and she stared at her own hand as though it belonged to someone braver.

"Oh," she said softly.

"Oh," Oscar agreed.

Then there was no more time for revelation, because the dead had closed the last of the distance, and the four of them fought—truly fought, the way a crew fights and not the way four strangers do. Oscar called the seams as fast as his fraying sight could find them; Cael broke what he was told to break; Kessa killed the things that got past both of them; and Wick, white-faced and steadier with every shot, put her impossible power through a hole the size of a fist, again, and again, dropping the husks Oscar marked for her one clean unmaking at a time. It was not a rout. It was four tired people and a great many patient dead, and it cost them—a bad gash on Cael's forearm, the last of Kessa's bolts, very nearly the last of Oscar himself—but when it was over, it was *over*, and they were all four still standing, which was more than the arithmetic an hour ago would have promised.

They did not celebrate. There was no breath for it. They simply stood in the steaming ruin of the street, among the drifts of frost that had been the city's dead, and breathed, and lived.

Kessa was the one who said it, of course. She had walked to the wall of fallen stone where the road out had been and laid her hand flat against it and stood there a moment, reading it the way Oscar read seams, and when she turned back her face had gone past grim into something quieter and worse.

"It's gone," she said. "The high road's under all of that, and it's not coming back out, not for the four of us, not with hands." She looked at the steam, and the dark, and at last down—down the long throat of the dead city, toward the place where the streets sloped away into a deeper black, toward whatever sat at the bottom of all that silence and had lit when Oscar woke. "There's one way left that I know runs through to open air. It goes down before it goes up. It goes through the middle of the whole dead place."

She did not say *toward the deep house.* She did not have to. Oscar felt the pull of it in his teeth, the same eager wrongness the door had brightened with, the cold notion that had sat down in the center of his mind and not left—and he understood, before any of them said another word, that the city was not done with him, and that the only road home ran straight through the heart of the thing he had crawled out of.

Wick came and stood next to him, close, the way the young stand next to the thing they have decided is safe.

"You said you'd teach me," she said. "You did, though. You just did. So that counts." A beat. "Does that count?"

"It counts," Oscar said, and was surprised to find he wanted it to.

## Chapter Four — What the Fire Showed

They found the room three streets down and a level deeper: a squat stone chamber that had been a counting-house once, by the look of the slotted shelves and the long dead ledgers fused to them in blocks of ice. It had one door. Kessa walked the whole of it twice before she let anyone sit, running her fingers along the seams of the walls, testing the single entrance, and only when she had satisfied some private arithmetic did she nod, once, and drop her pack.

"We rest here," she said. "Short. Cael's bleeding and the girl's spent and I won't take the deep road tired. Two hours. We keep the fire small and behind the door, and one of us watches it the whole time."

No one argued. That, Oscar was learning, was how the crew agreed on things now—not by anyone deciding, but by no one objecting, a kind of governance by exhaustion. He found it inefficient and said nothing, because the one time he'd offered an opinion on the balcony it had nearly got him shot.

Wick built the fire. She did it without using her gift, he noticed—gathering the splintered remains of a shelf, striking a flint with hands that still shook a little from the street, refusing even to look at the easier way, as though she did not trust herself with so much as a spark after what the easy way had done an hour ago. Oscar marked that and filed it under things to mend later, if there was a later.

The fire caught. And for the first time since he had clawed up out of the cold, there was light that was warm instead of silver, light that threw real shadows and put real color back into the world, and in it Oscar finally *looked* at the three people who had decided, for reasons of their own, to keep him alive.

Kessa first, because Kessa was the one most likely to end him. By firelight she was older than her movement had suggested—past forty, he'd guess, though the frontier aged people early and she might have been less—with a face all weather and economy, brown skin gone leathery at the cheekbones, pale eyes set deep and always moving. Her hair was cropped short and shot through with grey she hadn't bothered to hide. She was lean the way working people are lean, no spare anything, and she sat with the crossbow across her knees the way other people sit with their hands folded. She was, he understood, entirely mortal—no flicker of the deep current about her, no granted light, nothing but the long hard discipline of a body that had kept itself alive in dangerous places by being better at noticing than the things that wanted to kill it. He had spent his long life among the powerful, and had nearly forgotten how formidable an ordinary person could be, given enough years and enough reasons. She had taken out a folding knife and a whetstone and was working one slow against the other while she watched the door, and Oscar noticed—because noticing was the one trade neither of them had lost—that the knife was wrong for her hand. The grip was worn pale and smooth to a broader hold than hers; she had to choke up on it to keep the edge true. It was not her knife. She sharpened it anyway, the way you keep a blade keen for an owner who is going to come back wanting it.

Cael had stripped back his sleeve and was seeing to the gash on his forearm, and the firelight made a study of him: a big man, broad and slab-muscled, with the kind of plain open face that looks built for laughing and had clearly not done much of it lately. Younger than Kessa—thirties, maybe—with a nose that had been broken at least twice by something that hadn't apologized. The iron token at his throat caught the light: a simple thing, a hearth-mark, the sign of one of the small kindly gods Oscar had known of in his day mostly as a footnote. And when Cael laid his free hand over the gash and murmured something too low to catch, a faint and gentle warmth gathered under his palm—not the deep current at all, not the cold writing Oscar's whole life had been built on, but something else, something *given*, a borrowed grace that knit the torn edges of the wound a little closer and left Cael grey and tired for the spending of it. It was not his to command, only to ask for: it answered when he knelt and not when he reached, and it always took more out of the man than it put back into the hurt. So. A different river, that. Power that came from kneeling rather than from study. Oscar had always been faintly snobbish about it and found, watching it close a wound by firelight, that the snobbery had less ground under it than he'd assumed.

And Wick. Wick sat closest to the flames with her knees pulled up, and the fire showed her for what she was: a half-grown girl, sixteen at the most, thin in the way of someone who hadn't been fed reliably, with a sharp clever face and a great deal of dark hair escaping a braid that had given up hours ago. There was a mark at the side of her throat, just below the ear—he'd taken it for dirt, in the dark, and saw now that it was under the skin, a faint reddish branching like the ghost of a lightning-scar or the vein of a leaf, the kind of thing a person is born with and not given. He did not know what it was. He knew it was the reason the heat lived in her hands, and he knew she did not know what it was either, by the way she kept her collar pulled up over it. The wild-touched were rare, and rarely happy, and almost never taught, because the people who could have taught them were afraid of them. He had been one of the people who could have taught them. He had, on occasion, been one of the people who were afraid.

"You're staring," Wick said, without looking up.

"I'm cataloguing," Oscar said. "It's different. Staring is rude. Cataloguing is scholarship."

"It's still rude when the scholar does it." But there was no heat in it; she almost smiled, and pulled her collar higher, and he let the mark go unmentioned, which she noticed, and which earned him a small and grudging softening he had not been angling for and was obscurely pleased to have.

He needed water. There was a cracked basin near the dead shelves where melt had pooled and refrozen and thawed again at the fire's edge, and he went to it to drink, and that was how he saw himself.

The water was still and the firelight behind him made a dark mirror of it, and Oscar looked down expecting nothing in particular—expecting his own face, which he had worn for three hundred years and could have drawn blind—and instead went very quiet.

It was his face. The long bones of it, the pale skin of the old long-lived blood, the silver hair, the eyes that held a little of the deep current's cold light the way all his people's eyes did. All of it his, unmistakably, the face that had argued with kings and outlasted kingdoms.

And it was *new.*

There was no other word that fit, and he turned the wrong word over and over looking for a better one and did not find it. Three centuries leave marks even on the long-lived; not age, exactly, but *wear*, the small accumulated record of a life—the thin scar through one eyebrow from a duel he could not now recall the cause of, the weathering at the corners of the eyes from squinting at small script in bad light for two hundred years, the particular set a face takes on from being a specific person for a very long time. He knew that face. He had shaved that face. And the face in the water had none of it. The eyebrow was unbroken. The eyes were unweathered. The skin was smooth as a thing just finished, just lifted from the mold, just—

*Made.*

He did not let the thought finish. He had got very good, in the last several hours, at not letting certain thoughts finish; it was becoming his chief remaining skill. He drank, and did not look at the water again, and when he sat back down by the fire his face was arranged into its usual mild superiority and only his hands, had anyone been watching them, were not quite steady.

Someone had been watching them. Kessa's eyes came off the door for half a breath, found his hands, and went back to the door, and she said nothing, and somehow her saying nothing was the loudest thing in the room.

"You should sleep," Cael said. To all of them, but mostly, Oscar thought, to fill the quiet that had got too large. "I'll take the first watch."

"You're grey as ash," Kessa said. "I'll take it. You spent yourself on your arm."

"And you've not slept in two days."

"I've not slept in two days a great many times." She did not move from the door.

It was Oscar who said the thing out loud, because of course it was. He had been turning it over since the balcony—the gap in the formation, cut to the size of a fourth body; Kessa's road that "wanted four"; the way the three of them governed by exhaustion, no one willing to put a hand up and decide. He was clever enough to fit the pieces together, and, as ever, nowhere near wise enough to leave them fitted in the privacy of his own skull.

"You've lost someone," he said, into the middle of the small argument about the watch. "Recently. The one who used to make the calls." He said it the way he said most true things—as though being right about a thing were the same as being permitted to mention it. "You move like a cart with a wheel off. Three of you, and a hole shaped like a fourth, and not one of you will stand in it. Who were they?"

The argument stopped. Something went out of the room and something colder came into it, and Oscar understood—a half-second too late, the way he understood most things that weren't written on a page—that he had walked his clever feet directly into a grave.

"You just *say* it." Kessa's voice had gone very flat. Her hand had found the crossbow again, not lifting it, only resting there. "Whatever's in your head. Straight out the mouth, no toll at the gate." She looked at him with something that was nearly hatred and nearly grief and could not seem to settle on which. "I've put a bolt in men for less and slept the night through."

"That was unkindly put," Oscar conceded, and meant it, which did not help. "I have a gift for the unkindly put. I'm given to understand it's among my warmer qualities."

For a moment he thought she might do the thing the balcony had only threatened. Then Cael let out a long breath and set his mended arm across his knee and looked into the fire, and when he spoke it was not really to Oscar at all.

"There were four of us," he said. He turned the iron token at his throat without seeming to know he did it. "Kessa, the girl, me. And Doss—Doss made four. He found these places—knew how to read the old wards, knew which doors would hold and which would drop on you, knew when a thing was worth the risk and when it wasn't. We followed him into a dozen dead towns and came out of every one because he said *no* more often than he said *yes.* He was the one who said no."

He stopped. Kessa had gone still at the door—not watching it now, watching nothing—and the slow scrape of the whetstone, which had not paused once since they sat down, paused. She laid the knife flat across her knee and left it there. She did not say anything. Whatever she had to say about Doss she had said a long time ago and was done saying; the rest of it she kept in her hands, and her hands had stopped.

"There was a vault," Cael went on. "Deep in the last place, deeper than we'd gone. Doss said leave it. Said the wards on it weren't broken, they were *sleeping*, and a sleeping ward is one nobody's tripped yet, and that meant whatever it kept was still in there. We were tired and we were poor and we'd come a long way for nothing, and the three of us—" his jaw worked "—the three of us put it to a vote. Like it was a thing you could vote on. Like the dark cares how many hands go up. We outnumbered him. He came because he wouldn't let us go in without him."

"You don't have to," Kessa said, low.

"They've asked." He let the words settle, and went on, steady and not unkind, telling it now because telling it was owed. "The ward wasn't sleeping for nothing. It woke the way this place woke, all at once, the cold things coming up out of the floor, and there was a moment—there's always a moment—where one person can hold the door so the others get clear, and Doss took it, because Doss always took it, and we got clear, and he—" Cael's voice did not break. It simply stopped, the way the dying voice in the tower had stopped, on a word it could not get to the front of. "He didn't."

The fire popped. No one filled the silence. Oscar, who had spent his life being the one who filled silences, had three or four things he could have said—he always had three or four things—and he looked at the iron token turning in Cael's fist, and the stilled knife on Kessa's knee, and he set every one of them down unsaid and let the quiet stand. It was, he thought, possibly the first wholly kind thing he had done since he woke, and it had cost him only the pleasure of being heard.

"So when you came up out of *your* deep house," Cael said, finally, lifting his eyes to Oscar, and there was no accusation in them, which was worse than accusation, "and Kessa said *put it back or put it down,* and I said no—you should understand I wasn't being kind. I've voted to go through a sleeping door once already. I'm not doing it twice. I'd sooner carry a stranger I'm not sure of than make that call again and be wrong the same way." He almost smiled, and didn't. "That's not faith. That's just someone who's learned the one lesson he was ever going to learn."

Oscar looked at the three of them—the watchful woman who would not leave the door, the gentle ruined man who had run out of *no*, the wild girl who carried a mark she didn't understand and a guilt that wasn't hers to carry—and understood, with the sudden clarity of a thing he should have seen hours ago, that he was sitting in a dead man's place. The road wanted four. The formation had a gap. They had been moving since they lost Doss in fits and lurches, voting on things no one should vote on because the one who used to *decide* was holding a door somewhere in the dark forever. And some part of him, the part that had commanded and been obeyed for longer than these three had been alive, was already leaning into the gap, already reaching for the empty place at the head of the crew the way an hour ago he had reached for the empty place on his shoulder.

He did not take it. Not yet. It was not his to take, and he had learned, watching Cael, what it cost to want a thing for the group and be wrong. But he felt the shape of it, the wheel-shaped gap, one more space cut exactly to his size that he had not asked to be cut, and he wondered—he let himself wonder this much—how many such spaces there were in the world, and why they all seemed to be waiting for him.

"Sleep," he said. It came out gentler than he meant it, and with more authority than he had any right to, and he watched all three of them hear the authority in it, and watched none of them object. "Kessa keeps the door. I'll wake whoever's next. I don't sleep much, and I sleep badly, and I'd as soon spend the bad hours being useful." A pause. "And Cael—I'm sorry about Doss. For whatever the sorrow of a stranger is worth, which I'll grant is not much."

"It's worth something," Cael said. "You'd be surprised what it's worth."

They slept. Wick first and hardest, curled small as a child, the mark at her throat faintly warm in the firelight. Cael after, grey and heavy. Kessa did not sleep at all, of course, but she let her eyes rest on the fire instead of the door for a little while, which from her was nearly the same thing.

And Oscar sat his watch in the warm uneven light, in a dead city under the bones of the world, and did not reach for his shoulder, because he had decided he was done discovering the absence over and over like someone worrying a bruise to be sure it still hurt. He missed the weight. He missed the voice. He missed it the way you miss a thing that is part of the architecture of you, so that its loss is not a wound in you but a wound in the *shape* of you, a load-bearing piece gone and the whole structure quietly waiting to find out if it will stand without it.

"You'd have something to say about all this," he told the fire, very low, where no one could hear. "Something unforgivable. I'd pretend to hate it."

The fire said nothing. The dark beyond the door said nothing. And somewhere far below them, deeper than the deep road, deeper than the heart of the dead city, something that was not quite a sound and not quite a thought turned over once in its long sleep, the way a name turns over behind your teeth—and was still again before Oscar could decide whether he had felt anything at all.

## Chapter Five — Old Hands

The deep road did not announce itself. It simply began, the way the cold did, by degrees—the counting-house giving onto a stair, the stair onto a colonnade, the colonnade sloping down and down into a quarter of the dead city that had been grander than anything above it and was, for that reason, worse to walk through. Up top the buildings had been homes and shops and the ordinary machinery of people living. Down here they had been *temples*, or schools, or whatever this lost people had built when they wanted to feel close to the thing they worshipped, and the frost-script ran everywhere on the walls, thick as ivy, so dense in places that the stone underneath had vanished entirely behind it.

Oscar could not stop reading it.

That was the trouble with being what he was. A scholar does not get to choose whether to understand; understanding simply happens to him, the way a draft happens to a person standing in a doorway, and the script on these walls poured meaning at him whether he wanted it or not. Most of it he could not parse—the hand was old past any tongue he knew, the figures shaped by minds that had thought differently about the world than anyone now did. But the deep grammar of it, the bones of how a working was made to hold, those had not changed in all the ages between that people and his own, because they could not change. They were not a language. They were the rules underneath language, the rules the deep current ran on, and a thing that is true does not get old.

"They wrote it into everything," he said, mostly to himself, trailing his ruined new hand a finger's breadth from the wall, not touching. "Look. This isn't decoration. The whole city is one working. Every wall a clause. They didn't *cast* down here—they *built* the spell and then lived inside it." It was, he thought, magnificent and insane in roughly equal measure, the way the very best ideas usually were. "No wonder it woke all at once. You can't wake part of a sentence."

"Is that why the cold ones came up?" Wick had drifted close again, collar up, eyes on the walls. She'd been quieter since the fire, since Doss, but the questions still leaked out of her the way the heat did—pressure that had to go somewhere. "Because the city's a spell, and the spell turned on?"

"Near enough." He found he liked teaching her. He had forgotten he liked it. "Something switched the whole working live again after a very long time off. The dead weren't raised, exactly—they were *included.* The spell counted them back in, the way it was always written to, the moment it had power to spend." He paused at that, because the thought arrived with a barb on it he hadn't seen coming. "Which raises the question of what, precisely, switched it on. A city this size doesn't wake from a draft."

He did not say *I woke when it woke.* He did not say *the door knew my hand.* He had got very good, in a single day, at the small craft of not saying things.

Kessa, ahead, raised a fist. They stopped.

The colonnade ended at a wall. Not a doorway with a wall behind it—a wall *across* the road, floor to vaulted ceiling, seamless grey stone, and set into the middle of it a circular plate the size of a cart wheel, graven edge to center with the densest script Oscar had seen yet, spiraling inward to a single closed figure at the hub.

A closed loop. A knot drawn without ends. He did not let his eyes stay on it.

"This is the way through?" Cael set a careful hand to the wall on either side of the plate, testing, finding it solid. "There's no door here. There's no seam."

"There's a door." Kessa had crouched to read the floor, the dust, the small story the ground always told her. "Doss taught me this. You don't look for the door. You look for the wear. People used a way for an age, there's a path worn into the stone even under the frost, even after everyone's dead—" She pointed. A faint smoothing in the floor, a track polished by a great many feet over a great many years, leading directly into the blank wall beneath the plate. "Through there. It opens. We just don't know how."

Three of them looked at the plate. Oscar looked at the worn track, and the wall, and then, because he could not help it, at the script.

"It's a question," he said.

They looked at him.

"The plate. It's not a lock you pick, it's a question the wall asks, and the door opens for the right answer." He came closer, hands behind his back now, deliberately, because his hands had a way of reaching for things and he did not yet know what this thing did to a careless hand. "Here—this whole outer ring is the asking. They've written the question into the stone so that anyone who can read it can answer, and anyone who can't, can't pass. It's elegant. Cruel, but elegant. A lock that only admits the lettered." He was already half lost in it, the old pleasure rising in him despite everything, the pure clean joy of a hard text yielding. He had come up out of the tower with no power worth the name and a head full of borrowed centuries, and he was beginning to understand that this—the reading, the standing between a shut thing and the people who needed it open—was the whole of what he had left to be. Not the hand that wrote the working. The hand that could tell you what it said. "Most of it's beyond me. The dialect's wrong. But the structure—the structure I know. They want a name. The wall wants you to *name the thing that built it.*"

"And do you know it?" Cael asked. "The thing that built it?"

"No," Oscar admitted, which cost him, because he was unused to the word and it tasted strange. "Their god, their founder, the architect of the working—some name this whole dead people knew in their bones and wrote into their front door so a stranger never could." He frowned at the spiral. "It's the perfect lock. You can only open it if you already belong."

"So we're stuck," Kessa said.

"I didn't say that." He had been following the spiral inward as he spoke, reading what he could, skipping what he couldn't, the way you read a page in a language you half-know—and the spiral ran inward and inward to the closed loop at the center, the knot without ends, and there the old dead dialect gave way, just for that one figure, to the deep grammar underneath, the bones that did not age. And the bones, here, said something Oscar understood perfectly.

The loop at the hub was not part of the question. It was a *signature.* Someone had come to this lock, long after its makers were dust, and answered it—and then, in the way of a certain kind of person, had not been able to resist signing the solution, leaving a mark on a working that wasn't theirs, the way a lesser scholar scrawls in the margins of a book they did not write. The loop was a later hand. A familiar hand.

His hand. The hand on the sword. The hand healed into his own skin.

"Someone's been here before me," he said, very quietly, and the *me* got out before he could trade it for *us,* and he saw Kessa hear it. "Someone solved this already and left their seal on it. And the seal is keyed so that—" He stopped. He understood what was about to happen and there was no version of the next ten seconds that did not make things worse, and he did it anyway, because the alternative was four people standing at a dead end while a city woke around them, and because some stubborn part of him needed to know if he was right. "Stand back."

"Oscar—" Cael began.

He laid his palm flat against the closed loop at the center of the wheel.

The script did not ask him the question. That was the horror of it, clean and immediate: the wall did not test him, did not weigh him, did not demand the name that this whole dead people had hidden in their door against strangers. The loop under his hand simply *brightened*—glad, eager, recognizing—and ran its gladness outward along the spiral in a wave of waking silver, and the wall remembered it had once been told the answer by a hand exactly like this one, and the worn track in the floor proved true, and the stone drew back along a seam that had not been visible a breath before.

The door opened for him the way the tower door had opened for him. Because it knew him. Because, somewhere in the unthinkable depth of years this city had lain sealed, a hand that was his hand had already been here, and signed its work, and gone.

No one said anything for a moment. The new dark beyond the door breathed out at them, older and colder.

"You said you'd never been here," Kessa said. Flat. Not quite an accusation. Giving him the chance, almost, to have an answer.

"I haven't." It came out steadier than he felt. "I have never in my life seen this city. I would stake my considerable reputation on it." Both true. Both, he was beginning to understand, beside the point. "And yet."

"And yet," Kessa agreed, and that was all, and somehow it was the most frightening conversation he'd had since he woke, because she had stopped pushing. People stopped pushing when they'd decided a thing and were only waiting to see it confirmed. She'd looked at his unweathered face and his too-new hands and a door that opened to his touch, and she had arrived, hours ago, at a conclusion he was still refusing to reach, and she was simply, patiently, walking him toward it.

They went through.

The space beyond had been a vault, or a vestry, or the private study of someone the dead city had thought important—a long low room with shelves still standing and the rotted ghosts of books still on them, and a worktable in the center under a fall of pale frost. And on the floor before the table, where the frost lay thin, was a body.

Not a husk. Not one of the cold drowned dead of the upper streets, grey and rimed and patient. This was recent. This was a person who had come down here, as they had come down, breathing and warm and afraid—a salvager by the look of the gear, the boots, the half-unrolled tools—and who had died here not long ago at all.

And they had not died the way people die.

Oscar crouched over the body, and the scholar in him looked before the rest of him could flinch, and what he saw turned even that cold, detached part of him to ice. There was no wound. There was no blood, no frost-burn, no mark of the cold dead's slow hands. The body was whole and unhurt and entirely, wrongly intact. But it was *unfinished.* That was the only word that fit, and Oscar hated that it was the same word the water had handed him by the fire. Pieces of the person were simply *gone*—not torn away, not destroyed, but *removed,* the way a careful hand lifts a wrong word off a page. One arm thinned to nothing below the elbow, the edge of it clean and pale and final. Half the face smoothed away to blankness, like a carving abandoned mid-cut—except it had not been abandoned. That was the detail Oscar's eye kept snagging on and his mind kept declining: the blank was *finished.* Not raw, not red, not the open surprise of a wound, but smooth and complete and considered, as though that edge had always been meant to be the edge, as though whatever did this had not removed a face so much as decided the body had never properly had one. And where the parts had been taken, the flesh did not bleed or scar. It simply *ended*, neat as a margin, as though whatever had done this had not killed the salvager so much as *edited* them, reading down the length of a living person and striking out the parts it had no further use for.

"Don't look," he said to Wick, too late, and put himself between her and it, which he should have done before he spoke and not after—his body always a half-beat behind his mind.

"What did that?" Cael's voice had gone very low. The iron token was in his fist. "Oscar. What does that to a person?"

Oscar straightened slowly. The cold in the room had nothing to do with the frost. He had seen a great deal in three centuries; he had seen people unmade in nearly every way the unmaking could be done; and he had never once seen this, a death by deletion, a person taken apart by something that treated the living the way an editor treats a draft—keeping what it wanted, removing what it didn't, and feeling, he was suddenly and absolutely certain, nothing at all about either.

"I don't know," he said. And then, because he could not help being right out loud, even now, even here: "But it wasn't the cold dead. The dead are a flood—they take everything or nothing. This was *chosen.* Every cut deliberate. Whatever did this looked at a living person and decided, piece by piece, which parts were worth keeping." He looked at the clean pale end where an arm had been. "It wasn't hungry. It was *correcting* something."

The warmth he'd carried down from the fire had gone entirely out of him. Somewhere in this dead working of a city, sharing the dark with four tired people and a great many patient corpses, there was a thing that did not kill so much as *revise*—and it had come down here, recently, on the same road they were walking, toward the same heart they were bound for.

And it had left the salvager's face half-finished, and taken the rest, and Oscar found he could not stop thinking that the cuts looked deliberate in a way he recognized, the way the loop on the wall had looked familiar, the way the door had known his hand: not as a stranger's work at all, but as the work of something that belonged to the same dreadful family of things he was beginning to suspect he belonged to himself.

"We're not the only ones down here," Kessa said. She had her back to the wall and the crossbow up and her eyes on the dark beyond the worktable, where the vault ran on into the deeper dark, toward the heart. "Are we."

"No," Oscar said. "I rather think we are the second-most interesting thing in this city. And I would very much like to keep it that way."

## Chapter Six — The Redactor

They did not run from the vault, because running was how you died in places like this; Doss had apparently taught the crew that too. They went on at Kessa's pace, which was quick and quiet and never quite hurried, deeper along the working's spine toward the heart, and they did not speak of the edited body behind them, and the not-speaking of it walked with them like a fifth set of feet.

Oscar kept reading the walls. He could not stop, and now he had a reason past habit: somewhere ahead or behind was a thing that took people apart like sentences, and the only weapon he had brought up out of the tower was the one he'd always had, which was understanding things faster than they could kill him. So he read. And three turns past the vault, where the script thinned over a long gallery of fallen columns, he found the thing's handwriting on the world.

"Stop," he said. "Don't touch the left wall. Any of you."

They stopped. Cael looked where Oscar was looking and saw nothing but stone. "What is it?"

"It's been *here.*" Oscar held his ruined hand a careful span from the wall, not touching, the way you hold a palm near a fire to test it. The script on the left-hand stone was wrong. Not damaged—the cold dead damaged things, broke them, froze them. This was the opposite of damage. Whole stretches of the old working had been *cleaned,* the dense frost-script simply gone and the stone beneath left blank and smooth, the edges where it ended neat as a knife-cut, neat as the salvager's vanished arm.

And along the cleaned stretch, something was missing that took him a moment to name, because absences are slow to announce themselves: the cold. Not warmed—*gone.* Cael's breath fogged white everywhere in the gallery except in front of the blank stone, where it came and went invisible, as though the air there had forgotten the habit. Oscar crouched and laid two fingers to the floor below the blank and found no frost on it at all, on a floor that had worn frost for an age.

"It isn't only erasing the writing," he said slowly, and the slowness was dread. "It's striking out the *rules.* Somewhere in all this the old working has a line that says *let it be cold here*—and that thing read the line, decided the city had no further use for it, and now this stretch simply isn't cold anymore. It doesn't fight what's in its way. It removes the part of the world that was holding the thing up." He looked down the gallery, toward the dark where the heart waited, and the rest of it came clear and worse. "And it's following the sentence to its end. The end is the heart. The same place we're going—and it's editing as it reads."

"Then we go a different way," Kessa said.

"There is no different way. You said it yourself—this is the road. It's the road for us and it's the road for that." He looked down the gallery toward the dark where the heart waited. "We're behind it. It's between us and where we need to be, and it's moving slower than we are because it's stopping to *correct* everything, and the moment we catch up to it—" He did not finish. He had a gift for finishing sentences nobody wanted finished, and for once he set the gift down.

"Then we go quiet," Cael said, "and we go careful, and we don't catch up to it."

It was good advice. It would have worked, very probably, if Wick had not been sixteen, and exhausted, and walking through the dark afterimage of an edited corpse with a power in her hands that fed on exactly the kind of fear this place was made to produce.

It was not her fault. Oscar would be firm about that, later, with anyone who suggested otherwise, including Wick. The gallery floor was old and the frost made it treacherous and a slab tilted under her boot with a crack that was not loud at all—but in the held silence of that place it was a shout, and Wick's whole body flinched, and the heat that lived in her hands jumped at the flinch the way it always jumped, a reflexive flare of light and warmth, there and gone in half a breath.

Half a breath was enough.

Down the gallery, in the dark past the fallen columns, something that had been still became attentive. Oscar felt it the way you feel a person across a room turn to look at you—a shift in the weight of the place, a cold intelligence orienting. The blank stretches of cleaned wall, the ones it had already edited, brightened faintly, all at once, like the eyes of a thing opening.

"It heard," he said. "Move. *Move,* now, back, into the columns—"

It came up the gallery toward them, and it did not hurry, and the not-hurrying was the most frightening thing Oscar had seen in a day full of frightening things.

He had braced for a monster. He understood monsters; he had catalogued a great many. This was not a monster. It walked upright and it was roughly the size and shape of a person, robed or shrouded in something that drank the light, and where its face should have been there was only a smooth pale blankness, unfinished, the exact blankness of the half-edited salvager—as though the thing had done to itself, deliberately, what it did to others, and kept only the parts it needed. It carried no weapon. It did not need one. As it came it trailed one hand along the right-hand wall, the wall still thick with old script, and everywhere its fingers passed the script *thinned and vanished,* the working coming apart at its touch, the stone going blank and smooth in its wake. It was reading the wall as it walked. It was editing the world simply by moving through it, the way a careless reader leaves a book changed.

And it was reading toward them.

"What is it?" Wick's voice was very small. The heat had drained out of her hands entirely; even her power knew enough to be afraid.

"Later," Oscar said. He had pushed her behind him, behind a fallen column, and Cael had put his bulk in the gap beside them with the hammer up, and Kessa had melted sideways into the dark with her crossbow, the way she did, finding the angle. "Cael—do not let it touch you. Whatever else happens. It doesn't *hit.* It *removes.*"

Kessa's bolt took it in the chest from the side, a perfect shot, dead center.

The bolt struck the shrouded thing and stopped, and hung there a moment, quivering—and then, where it had buried itself, the dark shroud and whatever was under it simply *was not,* a clean small absence opening around the wound, and the bolt dropped through the new gap to the floor, having pierced nothing, because the thing had edited out the part of itself the bolt occupied rather than suffer to be pierced. It did not stagger. It did not bleed. It turned the smooth pale blank of its face toward the dark where Kessa was, marking her, filing her, and went on coming.

"It's correcting itself," Oscar breathed. The understanding came up through him cold and total and, beneath the horror, lit with the terrible involuntary delight of a scholar watching an impossible thing prove real. "It doesn't take damage. It takes *edits.* You can't wound it because it simply removes the wounded part and goes on. You can't kill a thing that treats its own body as a draft."

"Then we can't fight it," Cael said.

"No." And there—*there,* in the saying of *no,* in the flat dead end of it—the other thing arrived in Oscar, the thing that had always arrived for him at exactly this kind of wall, three hundred years of it, the leap that came only when every ordinary door had shut. "No, we can't fight it. So we won't."

He did not have the deep current. He had a candle's worth of power and a head full of useless centuries and one small true figure he could still afford—the seeing-thread, the narrow lay of attention that showed him the stitching of a thing. He had used it on the husks to find their seams. He had used it on the warden to find the cut. He reached for it now, and laid it not on the Redactor's body—the body was hopeless, the body rewrote itself—but on the *working it was reading.* On the wall.

And the wall lit up for him the way it always did, the old dense script resolving into structure, into grammar, into the long unbroken sentence of the dead city running away down the gallery—and Oscar saw, with the clean joy of a problem coming apart in his hands, that the Redactor was not free in this place. It was bound to the very thing it edited. It followed the working because the working was *how it read,* how it found the next thing to correct, a finger tracing a line of text to keep its place. Take away the line, and the finger had nothing to follow.

"It reads the city to move," he said, already moving himself, already scrambling along the gallery to the place where the old script ran across a single narrow span of arch—a clause, a hinge, a place where the whole long sentence of the working necked down to one slender passage before it widened again toward the heart. "It's reading the sentence. So we *cut the sentence.*"

"Oscar, it's almost on us—"

"I know how close it is, Cael, thank you, I find the information unhelpful." He pressed his ruined hand flat to the arch, over the narrow place where the working pinched, and he did the one thing his gutted power could still do—not break, never break, he had no force left to break anything—but *unstitch.* He found the single thread where the whole sentence of the city passed through this one arch, the load-bearing clause, the seam, exactly the way he'd cut the warden, and he put his last candle's-worth of will into severing not the stone but the *meaning,* the continuity, the thread of working that ran from the half they stood in to the half the Redactor needed to read its way forward.

The script under his hand did not shatter. It went *out,* a long dimming rush racing away down the gallery in both directions as the great sentence of the city lost its verb, the working falling dark span by span, the line the Redactor had been reading simply *ending,* mid-thought, in the middle of the floor.

The Redactor stopped.

It stood three columns from them, one hand still raised to the now-dead wall, and the smooth pale blank of its face turned slowly along the line of darkened script to the place where the sentence ended and there was no more to read. And for the first time it did something other than advance: it *hesitated,* a thing built to follow a text standing at the place the text ran out, casting—not for them, Oscar understood, but for the *line,* for the thread it had lost, the way a reader who has lost their place runs a finger back up the page.

"Now," Oscar said. "While it's lost. Down, toward the heart, *quietly*—it can't follow what it can't read, and I've just torn out the page between us."

They went. They went fast and low through the fallen columns, past the hesitating thing, into the deeper dark toward the heart, and behind them the Redactor stood at the edge of the silence Oscar had made, patient, methodical, already beginning to cast about for some other line to follow—because a thing like that did not give up. It only lost the thread. And a lost thread, Oscar knew, with the cold certainty of someone who had spent three centuries finding threads others had lost, could always, given time, be found again.

"What *was* that," Wick managed, when they had put distance and darkness between themselves and it. "You said later. It's later."

Oscar did not answer at once. His hand—the new, smooth, wrong hand—was shaking, and not only from the spending of his last power. Because he had felt it, in the moment he laid his seeing on the thing: the family resemblance, and underneath it the one difference that mattered. They did the same work from opposite ends. He read to understand and changed nothing; it changed everything and understood nothing. It had moved down the sentence of the city exactly the way he moved down it—and taken in none of it, kept none of it, wanted none of it. Not the salvager. Not the city. Not him. He had spent the whole encounter braced for a thing that meant them harm, and the part that would keep him awake was that it did not mean anything at all. It did not hate. It *corrected.* It treated bodies as drafts the way the tower had treated him as a draft. It belonged to the same order of things as the door that knew his hand and the loop signed into the wall, and as, he was beginning to be unable not to know, *himself.*

"I don't know what it is," he said, which was the truth, and then, because the rest of it was crowding up behind his teeth and he had never once in his life successfully kept that kind of truth behind his teeth, "but I know what it's *for.* It's a corrector. Something made it to go through places like this and read them and strike out what doesn't belong." He looked back, once, into the dark where it waited. "And I came up out of a place exactly like this, through a door that knew my hand, and the city woke when I did. So I find I'm extremely interested in what it considers *belongs*—and what it doesn't."

He did not say the last of it. He did not say: *and I am increasingly afraid I am a thing it was made to correct.*

But Kessa was watching his shaking hand again, with that same patient, terrible, unpushing look, and he understood that he had not needed to say it. She had read that line a long way back. She was only waiting, the way you wait at the end of a long sentence, for the word the whole thing had been building toward.

## Chapter Seven — The Heart of the City

The road did not end at a door this time. It ended at an edge.

The gallery opened without warning into a void, and the four of them stopped at the lip of it, and even Kessa, who did not waste breath, let one out. They stood at the rim of a vast round shaft sunk through the floor of the dead city, so wide a thrown stone would tire before it found the far wall, so deep the bottom of it was a guess. And it was not dark. After so long in the cold silver and the frost, the light at the city's heart hit them like a struck note: a slow, pale, living radiance that rose up out of the shaft and turned slowly in the air, and it took Oscar a moment to understand what he was looking at, and when he did the understanding sat him down inside himself.

The whole city's script was running *here.* Every clause, every wall, every frost-fine line of the great working they had walked through for hours—all of it poured inward and down along the shaft's curved sides in rivers of moving light, spiraling, gathering, feeding into something at the heart that he could not yet see and already did not want to. The dead city was a sentence. This was the word it had been built to say.

A stair wound down the inside of the shaft, hugging the wall, and they took it because there was nowhere else to go, and as they descended the light grew, and the air grew warm—warm, the first true warmth that did not come from a fire since Oscar had clawed up out of his cradle—and the warmth was somehow worse than the cold had been, because it was *wrong* down here, an exhale where everything else was held breath.

The heart of the city was a font.

That was the only word Oscar had for it, and it was not enough, but nothing would have been. At the bottom of the shaft, where all the rivers of script gathered, there was a great basin of pale stone, and it was full—not of water, but of the working itself, the deep current made visible and brought to a boil, a slow luminous something that was not liquid and not light and turned over on itself with the unhurried patience of a thing that had been waiting a very long time. The cold place in the air where Oscar reached to do magic, the deep current he had spent his whole life arguing with—it was *here,* pooled and naked and close enough to touch, more of it in this one basin than he had felt in all his three hundred years put together.

And it was reaching for him.

He felt it the moment they came down off the last of the stair onto the floor of the heart. The light in the basin *leaned,* the way a plant leans at a window, the slow current of it turning toward him and away from the other three, and the pull came up through the soles of his feet and into the marrow of him, gentle and total and dreadful: a sense of homecoming so strong it made his eyes sting, a feeling that he had been cold and incomplete and only half-real his whole short life and that the warm completing thing was *right here,* and all he had to do was step into it and be finished.

*Be set,* said the city.

The voice came out of the walls again, here as in the tower, the great gathered script flaring as it spoke—but down here it was not failing, not stuttering, not a dying mechanism repeating itself. Down here it was strong, and close, and almost kind.

*"The master has fallen. The line endures. You have come to the heart. Be set, and be whole, and inherit what is yours."*

"Don't," Kessa said. She had her crossbow up, not at anything, at everything, her eyes moving over the basin and the leaning light and Oscar's face, which had gone slack with longing in a way he could not feel and would have hated. "Whatever it's telling you. Don't."

"It's not telling me anything." His voice came out strange. "It's *offering.*" And it was. That was the horror under the homecoming: the font was not commanding him, not dragging him. It was holding the door open and letting him feel, in his bones, exactly how much he wanted to walk through. He had spent a day discovering all the ways he was diminished—the candle where the ocean had been, the wear missing from his face, the empty place on his shoulder—and here was the thing that would mend all of it at once. He could feel that it would. The font was not lying. That was what made it terrible.

"It's a forge," he said, and made himself say it, dragging the scholar up over the longing the way you drag a cold cloth over a fever. "Look at the architecture of it. The whole city pours its working into this basin and the basin—" he swallowed "—the basin *makes* things. Or finishes them. Completes a thing that came out unfinished." He thought of his too-new hands. He thought of the salvager edited on the vault floor. He thought, and could not stop thinking, of a frost-shedding figure in a lonely cradle, reaching, coming apart for want of exactly this. "It's where they were meant to be set. The ones from the cradles. The ones like—"

He did not finish. He was getting better at not finishing. But Kessa finished it for him, very quietly, not unkindly, the way you say a true thing to someone who has nearly arrived at it:

"The ones like you."

The word sat in the warm air and did not echo.

Wick had drifted toward the basin's rim. Of course she had; she was sixteen and the light was beautiful and the warmth was the first kind thing this city had offered. "It's not scary," she said, soft, reaching out a hand toward the slow luminous something the way she'd reached for the font in the—the way she reached for any bright thing. The mark at her throat had begun, faintly, to glow in answer to it. "It feels like—Cael, it feels *good,* it feels like—"

"Wick." Cael had her wrist before Oscar could move, drawing her back, gentle and immovable, and his other hand was at the iron token at his throat, and his plain face had set into something Oscar had not seen on it before—not fear, but a deep devotional unease, the look of a person of faith standing in front of a holy thing that belongs to the wrong god. "Don't touch it. I don't care how good it feels. *Especially* if it feels good." He looked at the basin the way he had looked at the edited body, and named it the way his kind named things: "This is a miracle. I just don't think it's a kind one. The kind ones don't have to talk you into them."

Three of them, three readings, and Oscar standing in the middle of them with the pull in his marrow: Kessa saw a curse, and Cael saw a wrongful miracle, and Wick saw the first warm beautiful thing in a city of cold, and not one of them was wrong, and none of them could feel what he felt, which was the basin's slow certain promise that it could make him *whole.*

And he wanted it. That was the thing he would not be able to explain to them afterward, if there was an afterward: that some large and exhausted part of him, the part that had reached for a familiar shoulder and a familiar current and found both gone, wanted nothing in the world so much as to climb into that warm pale light and stop being a candle and stop being unfinished and stop being whatever half-made thing the water by the fire had shown him. The longing was not a trick worked on him from outside. It came from the truest part of him. That was why it was dangerous.

He realized his hands were cold.

He looked down. The warmth of the heart was on every other surface of him, but his hands—the new, smooth, wrong hands—had gone white at the fingertips, and a fine pale rime was creeping up from the nails, frost, the same soft frost that furred the dead and had eaten the figure in the cradle, growing on him now the longer he stood at the edge of the thing that wanted to finish him. As if some part of him knew that to be unfinished was to be coming apart, slowly, all along, and that the nearer he stood to the cure the more honestly his body admitted the disease.

He closed his fists over it before the others saw. He was getting practiced at hiding what his body kept trying to confess.

"We can't be here," he said. "All of us feeling differently in front of a thing that wants something—that's how it gets one of us. We find the way up and out, the high road Kessa promised, and we leave this for—"

The light in the basin shuddered.

It was small, and it was wrong, and every one of them felt it: a flinch in the great slow current, a stutter run back up the rivers of script along the shaft, the way a held note wavers when something leans on the string. The kind voice of the city faltered mid-promise. And far up the shaft, at the lip where they had entered, a single long stretch of darkened wall flickered—and *lit.*

Oscar's stomach dropped.

"It found the thread," he said.

The Redactor's silhouette resolved at the top of the stair, small with distance, the smooth pale blank of its face turned down toward the heart, toward them, toward the font. It had cast about at the edge of the silence he'd made, patient, methodical, until it found where the great sentence picked up again—and the sentence picked up *here,* in the brightest, densest, most-written place in the whole dead city, the one passage it could not possibly resist reading to the end. It was not coming for them. Oscar made himself hold on to that, because it was the worst thing about it: it had no interest in them at all. It was coming because the sentence ran on, and a thing built to read could no more leave a line unfinished than water could decline to fall downhill. It would strike them out the way it would strike out a stray mark—without heat, without notice, in passing, on its way to the end. It started down the stair. It trailed one hand along the wall as it came, and behind its fingers the city's gathered light began, river by river, to thin and dim and *go out.*

"It's reading the heart," Oscar breathed. The understanding arrived with the floor still dropping out from under it. "It's going to edit the *font.* It's going to read this whole working to its last word and strike out whatever it decides doesn't—"

He stopped.

Because the font was the word at the end of the city's long sentence, and the working that held the whole buried place up—the ceilings, the shaft, the stair under their feet—was the same working now dimming span by span under the Redactor's trailing hand. Cut the sentence and you stopped the reader. He had learned that an hour ago. But you could not cut a sentence in half twice without the half you were standing in coming down, and the Redactor was not lost now; it had its place; it was reading straight toward the load-bearing heart of everything, and when it struck out the font, the font would take the city with it.

The warm light flickered again, dimming from the top down, and the first low groan of failing stone rolled around the great shaft, and the frost crept another knuckle up Oscar's hidden hands, and the basin at his feet went on leaning toward him, patient and certain and kind, offering, still offering, *be set, be whole, be finished,* while a faceless thing came methodically down the stair to unmake them all.

"Right," said Kessa, very flat, putting her back to Oscar's and raising the crossbow at a thing she could not kill. "There it is."

## Chapter Eight — Setting

The heart came apart from the top down.

The Redactor descended the long stair without hurry, trailing one hand along the wall, and everywhere its fingers passed the city's gathered light went out—river by river, clause by clause, the great working that had held this place up for an age unspooling in its wake. And as the working failed, the city failed with it. Up in the dark of the shaft a ceiling let go and came down in a roar that never seemed to reach the bottom. The stair shuddered under them. Dust and stone began to fall through the dimming light like the first of a long snow.

"It's reading the heart to its end," Oscar said. His voice did not sound like his own. "When it strikes out the font, the font takes the city. All of it. We have—" he did not know how long they had; he never knew, he only ever guessed; he guessed "—we have no time at all."

"Then we go *up,* past it—" Cael started.

"It's between us and up, and it can't be killed, and the stair behind us is already gone." Oscar's mind was running faster than it had run since he woke, faster than the falling stone, and for once the speed was no comfort, because every road it ran down ended at the same wall. He could cut the sentence again. He had done it once and it had saved them. But he could not cut the heart-passage of a working that was holding up the ceiling over their heads without bringing the ceiling down himself, and so the one trick he had left was the one trick he could not use.

Which left the font.

It turned over at his feet, slow and warm and patient, the whole deep current of the world made naked and brought to a simmer, more power pooled in that one basin than he had felt in three hundred years—and it leaned toward him still, gentle, certain, holding its door open.

*Be set,* said the dying city, faint now, pleading almost. *Be whole. Inherit. Step in, and be finished, and all of this will hold for you.*

*Set.* He had been turning the word over since the tower, the way you worry a loose tooth, and standing at the brim he finally let it show him all its faces. You set a jewel: fixed it in a claw of metal, made it admired and unable to move. You set a bone: forced the broken thing straight, whether or not straight was where it wanted to go, so it would knit the way the setter intended. You set a spell: locked a working to a single task forever, no longer free to become anything else. And you set a man—in a chair, in a role, in his ways—until the shape was the whole of him and there was no growing left to do. The city meant all of them at once. *Be set* was not an offer of life. It was an offer to be finished in the colder sense of the word: completed, and therefore over.

And it would. That was the thing he understood standing at the brim with the stone coming down: the offer was true. If he climbed into the font and let it complete him—let it pour the whole inheritance into his unfinished shape, fill the candle back into an ocean, smooth the wrongness out of his new hands, make him at last the thing the cradle had been struck to make—then the working would know its master, and steady, and hold. The city would not fall. He would walk out of here whole and vast and *himself,* the self he remembered, three centuries restored in a single warm breath.

He would walk out as the man he had been copied from. Faithful. Perfect. Set.

The frost had reached his elbows.

He noticed it the way you notice a thing at the very end—dimly, from far off. The rime had climbed his forearms while he stood there wanting, pale and final, and his fingers had gone past cold into a numbness that was not cold at all but *absence,* the slow honest admission of a made thing that to be unfinished is to be coming apart, and that the longer he stood at the edge of his own completion the faster the unfinished parts of him let go. He was fraying. He could feel, distantly, the edges of himself beginning to lift and drift like the script off the walls. The font offered to stop it. The font offered to make him whole, and the price was simply to be no one new.

"Oscar." Cael had him. Both broad hands closed hard on his shoulders from behind, turning him bodily back from the brim, and under the hands came the warmth—not the font's warmth, a smaller and a kinder one, the borrowed grace that had knit a wound by the fire, poured now into the fraying edges of him, an anchor, a hand on a kite-string in a gale. The frost stopped climbing. It did not retreat, but it stopped, held at bay by a hill-and-hearth god Oscar had once thought beneath his notice and would never think so again. "I don't know what that thing's telling you," Cael said, low and fierce against the roar, "but you're shaking apart, and I've got you, and I am *not* losing another one to a door I could have held. Whatever you mean to do, do it now. I'll keep you here while you do it."

Across the heart, Kessa had put herself in the Redactor's path.

She could not kill it; she knew she could not kill it; she shot it anyway, and Oscar saw the genius of it a half-second after she did it. Each bolt struck and the thing edited out the struck part of itself and dropped the bolt through the gap—and each edit cost it a heartbeat, a pause, a small correction it could not skip. She was not trying to wound it. She was *interrupting its reading,* one bolt at a time, making it stop and tidy itself instead of trailing its hand down the wall, buying seconds the only way a mortal with a crossbow could buy them off a thing that could not die: by being annoying enough, precisely enough, that even an editor had to keep stopping to fix the typos she kept putting in it.

"Wick," Oscar said.

She was at his side already, white-faced, the mark at her throat blazing now in the font's light, her hands full of the heat she was so afraid of.

"I need it open." He could barely hold the seeing-thread; his cohesion was going; but he laid it on the basin one last time and found the place, the single seam where the pale stone of the font had been stressed thinnest by an age of holding the current—and he set two numb fingers against the air over it, the way he had over a husk's breastbone a lifetime ago. "Here. The size of your fist. *Everything you have,* into here, and nowhere else. You did it before. Do it for me."

"What happens to you if I do?"

It was, he thought, the first genuinely wise question anyone had asked all day, and it had come from the youngest of them, and he did not have the heart to lie to her and did not have the time to tell the truth.

"Something new," he said. "Now, Wick. *Now.*"

She loosed it. One thread, into one fist-sized place—all that terrible power she'd spent her short life dreading, gathered to a single white point and put exactly where he'd shown her. The font's seam took it and cracked, a clean bright line opening across the basin, and the deep current came up through the break in a slow luminous flood, naked and complete, more of the work than the world ever let anyone touch—

—and Oscar plunged both his fraying, frost-eaten hands into it.

The ocean came up his arms.

For one moment he had all of it. He wanted it recorded—and there was no one to record it, only himself, only the screaming city—that for one single moment he held in his hands the whole of what he had lost and more, the current he had starved for, the power to do anything, to mend himself, to step in and be finished and be vast and be *Oscar* again the way Oscar was meant to be, whole and unbroken and three hundred years deep. The font sang it into him. The cradle's promise, kept. *Be set. Be whole. Keep it. Keep all of it. It is yours; it has always been yours; come home.*

He did not keep it.

He had spent the last day learning, against his will and one hard lesson at a time, the only thing this place had to teach him—and he had taught it first, to a frightened girl in a ruined street, before he understood he was describing himself. *Power isn't the rare thing,* he'd told her, with all the easy certainty of a man who had always had oceans of it. *The rare thing is the aiming.* He had meant it as a kindness. It turned out to be a prophecy. He could not hold great power; he had tried, in the falling tower, and it had run through him like water through a cracked jar. He was not a vessel anymore. He was something else now, something the arc of these hours had made of him without his leave—a reader, a writer, a hand that did not *keep* the work but *spent* it, that did not break things but found the one thread and pulled. He was not built to drink an ocean. He was built to *aim* one.

But aiming takes time, and a place to stand, and a hand steady enough to write with, and on his own he had none of the three. He had them on loan. Cael's grace was the only reason his hands had not yet drifted off the ends of his arms—Cael was spending a god's borrowed warmth to hold him in one piece, and going greyer with every breath of it. Kessa was the only reason the Redactor had not already reached the basin and struck it out from under all of them; every bolt she put into the thing was a second she did not have, paid out on him. And the font lay open at all only because a girl who had spent her whole short life terrified of her own hands had put every terror she owned into a space the size of a fist—because he had once told her, in a ruined street, that she could. He was not doing this. The three of them were doing it, and letting him hold the pen.

So he aimed it.

He did not climb into the font. He turned it inside out through himself like light through a lens, and with the whole inheritance of a dead people running up his arms and out through the wreck of his own unfinished hands, he wrote one figure—the largest, last, and finest figure of his long strange life—and he wrote it *outward,* away from himself, spending every drop of the completion that had been struck and saved for him on three exhausted strangers and the thing that meant to edit them out of the world.

The figure was the only craft he had left, made vast: not a blow, never a blow, an *unstitching.* He found the threads that bound the Redactor to the city's text—all of them, every line that let it read its way through the world, the way he had cut one such thread in the gallery—and where before he had snipped a single seam, now he severed the whole binding at once. He cut the editor loose from the book. Every thread it had been following went dark in the same instant, and the faceless thing that had never once hurried *stopped,* hands rising, casting, finding nothing, no line, no page, no place—blind, at last, in a city it could no longer read—and the collapse it had started took it, swept it from the stair into the falling dark, lost not because he had killed it but because he had taken away the world it knew how to follow.

And with the same working, the same outward-spent flood, he caught his crew. He did not decide to; there was no deciding left in him; the figure simply *was* the wanting, and what he wanted was for them to live. The current rose around Kessa and Cael and Wick and lifted them, shielded them from the coming-down of the city, and opened—tore—*held*—a single bright passage up through the failing shaft toward air, toward out, toward the surface none of them had seen in days.

The forge set him while he spent it.

That was the part he had not understood until it was happening, and could never have chosen on purpose, and would spend a long time afterward trying to forgive himself for being glad of. The font had been made to finish a thing by filling it. He had refused to be filled. But he had stood in the heart of it with the whole current running through him, and a working cannot help but *shape* the thing it runs through, and so the font finished him anyway—not into the smooth restored faithful master it had meant to make, not into the man he was copied from, but around the shape of what he was *doing,* which was the most un-faithful, un-masterful, un-Oscar thing imaginable: pouring out his entire inheritance on people he had known for a single day.

It set him crooked. It set him *his.*

The frost did not retreat up his arms. It *fell,* sloughing off him in a fine pale rain, and underneath it his hands were—still new, still unlined, still the wrong smooth hands of a thing freshly made—but they held. They were solid. They were *staying.* The fraying stopped, not held back now but ended, the loose drifting edges of him drawn in and closed and made to stay closed, and where there had been a candle's-worth of deep current scraped over nothing, there was, of a sudden, a well. Not the ocean. He had spent the ocean. But a true well, real and deep and *his,* refilled by the forging that ran through him, the first honest power he had held since he woke—and on the back of his right hand the closed loop, the knot drawn without ends, the mark of all the Alberich line, blazed once, bright as the font, and went quiet, and stayed.

And in the blazing of it, for one half of one heartbeat, he felt something far away turn toward him. Not the city. Not the Redactor. Something small and warm and old, a weight the exact shape of a thing that ought to sit on a shoulder, stirring at the edge of the world the way a name stirs behind the teeth when you have almost remembered it—reaching, recognizing, *waking*—and then the working closed and the moment was gone, and he could not have said, after, whether he had felt anything at all.

He came up out of the font on his own two staying feet, emptied and remade and altogether new, and the city was coming down around them, and there was a bright torn passage open to the air above, and his crew were alive inside the working's last shielding light, staring at him.

"*Move,*" Oscar said, and his voice was his own again, and there was something under it that had not been there in the tower—not the old effortless certainty, something smaller and truer and more frightened and more alive. "Up. The way's open and it won't stay open. I've nothing left to spend on it. *Move.*"

They moved. Up through the dying heart of the buried city, through the one bright passage a made thing had torn for them with a borrowed ocean, while behind and below them a city that had been a single long sentence finished, at last, the only way it had left—

—not *set,* as it had meant to be. Not whole.

Spent.

## Chapter Nine — The Thaw

They came up out of the world into the world.

The bright passage Oscar had torn held just long enough—a throat of forced light up through the dying shaft, the city folding in on itself below them as they climbed, the roar of it falling away by degrees until there was only the scrape of their own hands and feet and breath—and then the passage gave onto a crack in a glacier, and the crack gave onto the open, and the four of them spilled out onto a field of snow under an honest sky for the first time in days.

Oscar lay on his back in the snow and looked up and could not, for a moment, do anything else.

It was night, or near it, the long blue dusk of the high country, and the sky was *enormous.* He had forgotten. Days under the bones of the earth with a roof of solid rock for a heaven, and he had let himself forget that the real one had no ceiling at all, that it simply went up and kept going into a dark scattered with more stars than the dead city had held dead, and the size of it after the close cold dark below was almost too much to bear. Cold air moved across his face. It smelled of nothing—of snow, of distance, of a world that did not know his name and had never been written into a single long sentence by anyone. He breathed it the way a drowning person breathes air. His hands, when he lifted them against the stars, held their shape and did not fray, and the loop on the back of the right one was only a scar now, quiet, the way a scar should be.

"That," said Wick, flat on her back beside him, staring up at the same impossible sky, "is a lot of sky."

"It is the customary amount," Oscar said. "You've simply been somewhere with less of it."

But he said it gently, and he did not look away from the stars, and Wick laughed—a real laugh, cracked and tired and young—and for a moment the four of them lay in the snow at the edge of a glacier that had swallowed a city, breathing, alive, and no one said anything at all, and it was the best silence Oscar had stood inside in three hundred years.

He caught himself on the number.

It was a small thing, and no one heard it but the inside of his own skull, but it caught him all the same, the way a stone in a shoe catches you on the third step and not the first. *Three hundred years.* He had said it to himself a thousand times in a thousand rooms, the warm weight of it, the proof of him—*I have done this before, I have seen everything, I am three centuries deep and you are not.* And lying in the snow with a new heart beating in a new chest and the loop of a long, long line scarred quiet into his hand, he found the number had gone strange in his mouth. It was true. He *remembered* three hundred years; he could walk through them; the spires, the galleries, the slow accumulation of a self. But he remembered them the way you remember a road you have never walked, and he was beginning to understand—lying there, not letting the thought finish, then letting it finish, because he was tired of the cowardice of not finishing things—that the three hundred years were not, strictly, *his.* They belonged to the one whose face he wore. He was, what. A day old. A day, and an inheritance of centuries he had not earned and was no longer certain he was allowed to count.

"Three centuries," he said aloud, experimentally, into the cold. And then, because it had to go somewhere and it always went out the mouth: "Or near enough. Give or take. The figure may want revising."

"What?" said Wick.

"Nothing. A piece of scholarship. Don't trouble yourself."

Kessa had not lain down. Of course she had not. She stood a little apart at the lip of the crevasse they'd climbed from, crossbow loose in her hands, watching the dark fissure as though something might yet come up out of it, and Oscar understood that for Kessa the habit of watching the door would outlive every door there was. After a while she said, not turning around: "It's not dead. That thing."

"No."

"You cut it loose, you said. Blind. Lost." She did turn then, and her weathered face in the starlight was doing the arithmetic it was always doing. "But not dead."

"It can't be killed. I'd stake my—" He stopped, because his reputation, like his three centuries, had begun to feel like borrowed property. "I'm fairly sure it can't be killed. It treats its own self as a draft; you can't end a thing that simply removes the part of it you've ended. What I did was take away the book it was reading. It's loose in the world now, blind to the line it followed, and that will hold it a while." He sat up in the snow. "It won't hold forever. A lost thread can be found. I should know. I spent—a long time—finding threads other people had lost."

Cael had gone to one knee in the snow, and his lips were moving, the iron token in his fist, and Oscar realized he was praying—not for himself, by the shape of it, but *for the city,* for the dead they had left finishing themselves in the dark below, a hill-and-hearth god's small mercy spoken over a grave too large to comprehend. When he was done he stayed kneeling a moment, then rose, heavily, and looked at the three of them in the snow.

"We can't stay up here," he said. "We'll freeze before dawn, and the nearest hold's two days' walk if the passes are open, and I don't fancy explaining any of this to anyone who asks." He almost smiled. "So. There's the question no one's said yet, and I'll say it, because someone should, and Doss isn't here to."

The name landed quiet in the cold. No one flinched from it. That, too, was new.

"You came up out of a dead-house," Cael said, to Oscar, plainly, without the wariness that had been in it on the balcony a lifetime ago. "Through a door that knew your hand. Into a city that woke when you did, with a thing in it built to read places like that and strike out what doesn't belong. And you tore yourself half apart to haul three strangers into the open instead of stepping into that font and walking out a king." He let that sit. "I don't know what you are. I don't think you do either. But I know what you *did,* tonight, and a person is mostly what they do. So." He put out his broad scarred hand, the one that had held Oscar together while he came apart. "You're short a crew and we're short a fourth. Come down off the mountain with us. Sort out the rest after."

Oscar looked at the hand.

He had been offered a great many things across three centuries—across *the* three centuries, the borrowed ones; he would have to find some way to talk about this that did not require a footnote every time—thrones and apprenticeships and the gratitude of kings, and he had taken most of them as his due, because they were. No one had ever offered him a place in a formation because of a thing he'd done in the last hour rather than a thing he'd been for an age. It was, he thought, a much smaller offer than a throne. It was also, he found, the first one he had ever entirely wanted. And he noticed—because he was, whatever else had been taken from him, still a man who noticed—that he was about to say yes to it without knowing the first true thing about himself: not what he was, not whose memories he carried, not whether the face he wore had any claim at all on the centuries stacked behind it. He was going to choose these three before he had a single answer. He found he did not want to wait for the answers. He suspected they would be worse than the question, and that the choosing would come out the same regardless.

He took the hand. Cael hauled him up out of the snow, and did not let go at once, and the two of them stood a moment in the starlight with the dead city under their feet, and then Cael clapped him on the shoulder—the empty shoulder, the one that ached—and let go.

"Right," Kessa said, already turning, already reading the slope, already the eyes of the crew whether anyone had voted on it or not. "Single file, in my steps, and nobody touches anything that glows. We've had enough of things that glow."

They went down off the glacier into the long blue dark, four of them, into a world Oscar did not recognize and would have to learn from the snow up, and he walked where he was put, in the steps of a mortal woman who would never trust him and had decided to keep him anyway, behind a wild girl who hummed under her breath now instead of crackling, beside a kneeling kind of man who had run out of *no* and offered him a hand instead. He was a day old. He was three centuries deep, or said he was, or had been told he was. He had crawled up out of a cradle built to make him into someone dead and had spent the only inheritance that came with it on people who hadn't asked, and the doing of it had made him into something the cradle never meant—real, and his own, and small, and free.

That night they made a fire in the lee of a ridge, and they slept in turns, and Oscar took the last watch because he did not sleep much and slept badly, and somewhere in the cold hours before dawn, for the first time since he had come up out of the dark, he slept.

And he dreamed.

He dreamed he was somewhere warm and high and full of light, a gallery he knew the way you know your own hands, shelves to the ceiling and a window full of summer, and there was a weight on his shoulder—talons in the wool, a brush of feathers at his ear—and a dry voice saying something cutting and fond that he strained to hear and could not, quite. And he was not afraid, in the dream. He was *happy,* with a whole and easy happiness he had not felt awake, the contentment of a person exactly where they belong, doing exactly what they were made for, with everything they love close to hand. He turned to the window. There was a face reflected faintly in the summer glass, his own face, and it was lined at the eyes from squinting at small script in bad light, and scarred through one brow from a duel, and *old,* worn and old and earned, the face the water by the fire had refused to show him—

—and then he woke, in the snow, in the cold, a day-old thing with a new smooth unmarked face and an empty shoulder, and the dream went out of him like warmth through a cracked door, and left behind it only the ache of having stood, for a moment, inside a life that fit—and the slow cold certainty, settling in him with the dawn, that the life had been real, and warm, and happy, and entirely, perfectly, not his.

He did not wake the others. He sat his watch, and watched the light come up grey over a world he would have to start learning today, and somewhere out past the edge of everything a small warm weight he could not yet reach stirred again in its sleep, closer than the night before, and was still.

"You'd hate all this," Oscar told the cold, very low, where no one living could hear. "The mountain. The company. Me, like this." A long breath, fogging. "Come and hate it to my face, you wretched bird. I'll be here. I find I'm going to be here a while."

The fire burned down. The dawn came on. And below them, sealed again under the bones of the world, the dead city kept its long silence, and kept its loop-marked doors, and kept—though none of the four climbing down away from it knew yet to count them—its cradles. Most were empty. Not all. And this was only one dead-house, under one cold mountain; the loop was an old line and a long one, and somewhere under other mountains other doors waited for other hands, and other fonts turned over slow and patient and kind, ready to make a master of whatever climbed up to be set. Not one of those sleepers had a crew. Not one of them had yet been given a single reason to refuse.
