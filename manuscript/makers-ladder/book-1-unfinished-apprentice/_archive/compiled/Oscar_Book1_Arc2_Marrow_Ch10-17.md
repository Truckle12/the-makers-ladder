# Oscar — Book 1, Arc 2: "Marrow"

*Chapters 10–17, combined for an editorial pass.*

## Chapter Ten — Marrow

It took six days to walk out of winter.

Oscar had not expected that to be a thing a person could do on foot, but the high country gave way to lower country the way a fever breaks, by degrees and then all at once, and on the sixth morning he woke in the lee of a stand of black pines to find the snow gone patchy and grudging and a smell on the wind that was not cold at all. Wet earth. Green things deciding whether to risk it. He lay still a moment and breathed it and felt, under the ordinary relief of not being about to freeze, a smaller and stranger relief he did not examine too closely: that the world had more than one weather in it, and that he had lived long enough now—a week, give or take, with three centuries stacked behind it like someone else's library—to have seen it change.

They had become, in those six days, something that resembled a crew.

It had happened without anyone deciding it, which was apparently the only way this particular crew did anything. Kessa took the front and the watches and the choosing of the ground, because no one else could read a slope for what wanted to kill you on it. Cael took the heavy work and the cooking and the long patient labor of keeping three difficult people fed and roughly civil. Wick took, mostly, to following Oscar around with questions, which he had stopped pretending to mind. And Oscar took the rear, and the maps that were wrong, and a growing private list of all the ways the world had moved on without consulting him.

"How old are you, though," Wick said, on the fourth day, apropos of a long argument about whether a particular river had always been where it was. "Actually. You keep saying old. Old like Cael, or old like a tree?"

"Older than the river, evidently," Oscar said, "since the river's in the wrong place." Which was a deflection, and she let him have it, but he noticed he had not given her the number. The number had been his favorite thing about himself for longer than—for a long time. *I am three hundred years old.* He had deployed it like a title. And lately it sat in his mouth like a coin he wasn't sure was his to spend, because the three hundred years were a place he could visit and not a place he had been, and a man ought to be honest, at least in the privacy of his own arithmetic, about the difference between remembering a life and having lived one.

He said none of that. He said, "Old enough to know better and young enough to keep not doing it," which made Wick laugh, and which had the additional virtue of being, for once, entirely true.

The drift-cats came on the sixth afternoon.

Kessa stopped them with a raised fist a full minute before Oscar saw anything, which was the usual order of operations. She crouched, read the ground, read the air, and when she spoke it was very low. "Cats. Six, maybe seven. Drift-pack." A pause. "They shouldn't be down this low. And they shouldn't be hunting in daylight."

"Are they dangerous?" Wick, too loud; Kessa's hand flattened the air and she dropped to a whisper. "Are they?"

"Pale things. Lean. They take the weak off the back of a herd and they run from anything that stands its ground." Kessa's eyes never stopped moving. "So no. Not usually. But these have come forty miles off their range with the snow melting under them, and they're hunting in the open, in the light, toward four people who are very much standing their ground. Which means something's wrong with them, or something's driven them, and I don't love either."

Oscar felt it before he could have said what it was—a thinness in the day, a wrongness he was coming to know the shape of. "The pulse," he said. "When the city woke. It will have rung out a long way through everything that lives near the old places. I imagine a great many things have come a long way from where they ought to be, lately, with the wrong ideas in their heads." He did not add *and I'm the reason*, because he had decided, somewhere in the last six days, to stop confessing things no one had asked him to confess. It was a new discipline. He was bad at it.

Then the drift-cats came out of the rocks, and there was no more time for discipline.

They were beautiful and they were wrong, both at once: long pale bodies the grey-white of old snow, moving low and fast and utterly silent—and their eyes caught the light and threw it back wrong, a flat reflective shine like a frozen pond, and their breath did not fog. Whatever the pulse had done to them had taken the warmth out somewhere it shouldn't have come out. They did not howl. They did not slow. They came in a fan, the way clever pack things come, two wide to flank and the rest driving straight up the middle at the softest target, which their flat shining eyes had correctly identified as the girl.

"Wick, *behind* Cael—" Oscar started.

Cael was already moving. He stepped into the lane the cats had chosen and planted himself there with the long hammer up, a wall where a girl had been, and the first cat hit him and learned what a wall costs. The hammer came down across its shoulders and the silence of the thing broke at last into a short ugly sound, and it folded and did not rise, and Cael was already turning for the next.

Kessa's bow spoke twice. The first bolt took a flanker through the chest mid-leap and dropped it in the dirt; the second missed, which Oscar had not known she could do, and told him more about how fast the things were than any amount of watching.

And then one was inside the line, low and pale and silent, going past Cael's hip for the soft middle of the group with its flat eyes fixed on Wick, and there was no one between it and her but Oscar.

He did the thing he had been failing to do since the morning he woke in a stone box.

He reached—not for the great deep work, he knew better now than to grab for the ocean, but for the small first figure, the lance, the hard bright line of force that was the first true working any hand of his tradition ever learned. He had scored it ten thousand times across a life that might not be his. He had scored it in a falling tower a week ago and felt it die in his fingers like a wet match. He scored it now, fast and clean, the gesture his hands remembered even when the rest of him doubted—

—and it *took.*

It cracked out of his open palm as a short hard bar of white light, no longer than his forearm and bright as a struck nail, and it caught the drift-cat across the ribs in the air and *threw* it—lifted the whole silent pale length of it sideways off its leap and slammed it into the rocks with a sound like a dropped door. It did not get up. The lance winked out, spent, a curl of cold light unraveling off his fingers, and his hand was steady, and the figure had held.

It had *held.*

He had perhaps half a breath to feel it, in the middle of everything—the clean savage joy of his own craft answering him again, the well in him real and deep enough now to bear a little weight, the proof in white light that he was getting *back* what the tower had scraped out of him. *There,* said the part of him that had been so afraid. *You see. The power comes back. The power was always going to come back, because it's yours, because you're him, because nothing is really wrong that a little time won't—*

He shut that thought off too. It was getting to be a habit, the shutting-off. But he had noticed, lately, the particular shape of the lies he liked best, and that one—*you're him, nothing's wrong*—was the one he reached for every time the power answered, the way a tongue goes to a sore tooth. The stronger he got, the easier it was to believe the font had been a bad dream. He filed that away too, in the growing private list, under *things to be suspicious of in myself.*

Wick finished the last of them.

He turned in time to see it: the final cat breaking past Kessa, and Wick not flinching, not spilling, not loosing a wall of panic in every direction the way she would have a week ago—but lifting one hand and putting a single tight lash of heat into one fist-sized place on the thing's flank, exactly the way he'd taught her in a ruined street, and the drift-cat dropped mid-stride and slid to a stop at her feet and was still. She stood over it breathing hard, the mark at her throat fading from its brief warm glow, and looked at her own hand with an expression caught precisely between terror and delight.

"Did you see," she said. "Oscar. Did you *see* it. I aimed."

"I saw," he said, and meant it, and the meaning of it surprised him a little, the way her small triumph mattered to him more than his own. "Textbook. Better than textbook—I never wrote anything that clean at your age." Which was a lie, since he had no idea what he'd written at her age, since the age behind him belonged to someone else; but it was the kind of lie a teacher is supposed to tell, and he found he didn't mind that one.

Kessa was already among the bodies, knife out, checking them with a frontier thoroughness. "Don't eat these," she said, to no one, to all of them. "Whatever's in them, it's not for eating." She rolled one with her boot, studied the flat dead shine of its open eye, and her mouth went thin. "Forty miles off range, in daylight, with no fog to their breath. I've worked this country thirty years." She stood. "I've never seen that. Things are coming loose out here. Let's get behind a wall before we find out what else has."

The wall they found, two days on, was Marrow.

It announced itself first as smoke, then as smell, then as sound, in that order—a smudge on the horizon, the reek of forge-coal and tanning and too many people, the low working din of a place that did something for a living. And then they came over the last rise and there it was below them in a river valley, and Oscar stopped, because he had been picturing a frontier hold, a stockade and a well and a hundred hard people, and Marrow was not that.

Marrow was built on bones.

The old world had left something enormous in that valley—the broken half of a structure so vast that the living town had simply moved into the corpse of it, the way wasps move into a skull. Stone arches the size of hills marched down the valley in a long shattered colonnade, and between and beneath and inside them the town had grown, timber and tile and tarp packed into the ribs of a thing no one now living could have built, smoke rising through gaps that had been windows for giants. Carts moved on roads that had been the floors of halls. And everywhere, on every approach, on the broken stone and the new timber both, salvagers worked—hauling up out of the deep places of the ruin the relics of the dead world and bringing them, in barrows and on backs, down to the town that ate them.

"They live *on* it," Oscar said. He could not quite keep the thing out of his voice—not horror, exactly, but a cousin of it. He thought of a frost-shedding figure in a lonely cradle. He thought of a font, and what a font was for, and what the dead world had built its dead cities to do. "They live on the bones of the old places and they don't—they can't know what's in them. What they're built on top of."

"They know there's coin in them," Kessa said, not unkindly. "That's most of what anybody knows about anything, in my experience. Come on. And let me do the talking at the gate, because you, with respect, talk like a man who's never paid for anything in his life."

"I have paid for a great many things," Oscar said, with dignity.

"With money?"

He elected not to answer that.

The gate was not a gate. It was a chokepoint—the one road down into the valley narrowing to a single span between two stone ribs, and across it a timber barrier, and at the barrier a knot of people who were not soldiers and were very plainly not friendly. They wore no uniform, but they wore the same thing all the same: a hard easy confidence, the look of people who do not have to ask. A cord of knotted leather at the wrist, every one of them, dyed deep red. One of them sat on a stool with a ledger open on their knee, and that, Oscar understood at once, was the dangerous one, because the ones with the weapons only hurt you, and the one with the ledger owned you.

"Salvage tithe," the one with the ledger said, not looking up. They had a quiet, bored, accountant's voice, and a long scar that pulled one corner of their mouth into a permanent small skepticism. "Crew of four. What're you bringing in, what're you taking out, and who's your factor."

"We've nothing to bring in," Kessa said, easy, level, a different person than the one who read slopes—this was a person who had stood at a hundred such gates. "We're not salvaging. We're passing through. Resupply and a roof and we're gone."

"Nobody passes through Marrow." The accountant turned a page. "There's nowhere to pass through to. There's the ruin, and there's Marrow, and there's two hundred miles of nothing in every other direction, which I'd guess you came across, which means you came from the ruin, which means you've been salvaging without a factor, which is the one thing the Tithe takes personally." Now they looked up. "So. Let's try again. What did you bring up out of the deep, and where is it."

"We brought up nothing," Cael said, and there was a flat finality in it, the voice of an honest person stating a fact, and the accountant's scarred mouth twitched as if that were the funniest thing they'd heard all day.

"Everybody brings up nothing," they said. "Right up until we find the something." They made a small mark in the ledger. "Day's grace. You eat, you sleep, you don't trade, you don't dig. Tomorrow you see a factor and you square what you owe for crossing Tithe ground, or you and your nothing find out how much Marrow loves strangers." They waved two fingers; the barrier dragged aside. "Welcome in."

The crew filed through, and Oscar with them, and he had nearly made it past the stool when the accountant's bored eyes came up and landed on him and—stopped.

It was a small thing. It was nothing; he told himself it was nothing, the way he had told himself the wrongness of his own hands was fatigue. But the accountant's gaze went over him once, idly, the way it had gone over the others—and then came back, and sharpened, and dropped, for half a breath, to the worn grey hilt of the sword at his hip, where a small closed loop sat struck into the steel below the guard. And the bored skepticism on that scarred face shifted, very slightly, into something attentive. Something that was already, quietly, doing sums.

"Long way to carry a blade like that," the accountant said, mild, "for a crew that brought up nothing."

"It was a gift," Oscar said, which was the first thing that came to him and was, he realized as he said it, possibly true in a way he did not enjoy.

"Mm," said the accountant, and made another small mark in the ledger, and did not explain what it was for, and went back to being bored. But Oscar felt the eyes on his back the whole long walk down into the bones of the town, and the old animal part of him, the part that had bolted from a frost-shedding figure without waiting to ask why, was wide awake now and did not like it at all.

"Made a friend?" Kessa murmured, not turning her head.

"I make them everywhere I go," Oscar said. "It's a burden." But he found his hand had drifted to cover the loop on the hilt, and he made it stop, and he did not feel, walking down into Marrow with a day's grace and no coin and a stranger's sums being done at his back, even slightly like the man three centuries deep he kept catching himself pretending to be.

## Chapter Eleven — A Day's Grace

The trouble with a day's grace, Oscar discovered, was that it was extremely difficult to spend a day being graceful when you had no money.

They had four coppers between the four of them, two of which were Wick's and of a denomination Oscar didn't recognize and suspected were no longer legal tender anywhere. Marrow ran on coin and on credit, and credit in Marrow ran through the Tithe, and so by the middle of the morning Oscar had assembled a thorough and depressing picture of exactly how the trap was built.

It was, he had to admit, beautifully built. He could appreciate a good working even when he was caught inside it.

You could not sell salvage in Marrow except through a factor, and every factor wore the red cord, which meant every factor was the Tithe, which meant the Tithe set the price you got for what you risked your life to drag up out of the dead places—and set it low. You could not buy supplies for the next run except on credit, and the credit was the Tithe's, at the Tithe's interest, which meant the moment you came up short on one run you owed against the next, and the next, forever. You could not work the ruin without a factor's license, and you could not get a license without being in good standing, and you could not get into good standing without working the ruin. Every door in the town opened onto the same room.

"They don't even have to be cruel," Oscar said, with the flat admiration of a man examining a rival's elegant proof. "That's the genius of it. They've made the arithmetic do the cruelty for them. Every salvager in this valley is one bad week from owing their life to a ledger, and not one of them can point to the moment it was done to them, because it was never done in a moment. It was done a copper at a time." He looked around the crowded market street, at the hard tired faces hauling barrows of old-world stone past factors' stalls hung with red cord. "It's the best-run prison I've ever stood in, and I've stood in some celebrated ones."

"Could you not," said Kessa, low, "admire it quite so loudly. In the prison."

She had a point. He was working on it. It was, like not-confessing, a discipline he was bad at.

The relic trade itself was the part that turned his stomach, once he understood it, and he understood it standing at a factor's stall watching a salvager—a gaunt woman with frost-scar up one arm—sell a season's haul for a price that wouldn't winter her family. Most of what came up out of the dead places was just stone and dead metal, worth its weight and no more. But every so often the deep digging turned up something that still *held*—a worked thing, an old-world figure still live in some object, a lamp that lit without flame or a lock that knew one hand or a small grey box that kept whatever you put in it from ever growing cold. Those were the prizes. Those were what the salvagers broke their backs and buried their dead for. And those, the factor explained to the gaunt woman in a bored voice, "go south, to the proper buyers," at a price the factor named and she could not argue.

*South. The proper buyers.* Oscar filed it away. Somebody, somewhere up the long chain of red cord, was paying well for old-world workings—paying well enough that an entire town could be made into a machine for digging them up—and he found he was very curious who, and what they wanted them for, and decided this was a curiosity to keep behind his teeth for once.

They got their roof through luck and through Cael, in that order.

The luck was a boarding house tucked up under the high curve of one of the great stone ribs, its timber frame nailed into the ancient masonry like a swallow's nest under an eave. The Cael was that he carried the gaunt salvager's haul to the factor's scale for her when her scarred arm failed halfway, and would not be talked out of it, and the woman—her name was Brida, and the boarding house was hers, salvaging being a thing she'd done until the arm—looked at the big stranger doing a kindness that would profit him nothing and decided, against every hard lesson Marrow had taught her, to trust four people who could not pay.

"You'll sleep in the loft and you'll be gone before the cord comes asking," she told them, in the low back room that smelled of tallow and old stone. "I can't be seen feeding strays. They don't like generosity here. Generosity's competition." She said it plainly, without self-pity, ladling thin stew into bowls that had seen better decades. "Eat. You've the look of people who've not, in a while."

"Why," Cael asked her—gently, the way he asked the hard things. "Why risk it. For nothing."

Brida was quiet a moment, working the stiffness in her scarred arm without seeming to know she did. "My man went down into the deep claim four winters back," she said. "The good one. The one the Tithe keeps for itself, that they let no crew work but their own, because it's the richest and the worst. He went because we owed, and the only way to clear what we owed was the good claim, and the only way onto the good claim was to take the Tithe's terms." She looked at them. "He didn't come up. And the debt didn't die with him. It's mine now. Everything I make in this house, half of it's owed against a hole in the ground that ate my husband." She picked up her ladle again. "So when I see somebody do a kindness for free in this town, I feed them. It's the only coin the cord can't take a cut of. Eat your stew."

The room was quiet. Wick had stopped fidgeting. Cael's hand had found the iron token at his throat. And Oscar, who had a gift for filling silences and a worse gift for filling them badly, opened his mouth—

—and, for once, closed it again. Because the thing he had been about to say, the clever bitter true thing about the elegance of a debt that outlives the body it killed, was not a thing this woman needed to hear said cleverly. He had watched the words form and had set them down unspoken, and it had cost him only the small vanity of being heard, and he was beginning to suspect that this—this exact small swallowing—was a thing a person was supposed to learn to do, and that he had reached three centuries, or a week, without ever quite learning it.

"Thank you," he said instead. Just that. It came out plainer than anything he'd said in days, and Brida nodded as if it were the right thing, which surprised him, because he so rarely said the right thing on purpose.

It was the lamp that undid the morning's small peace.

Brida had one—an old-world thing, a salvage piece she'd kept back from a haul years ago and never sold, a hand-sized grey lozenge that gave a steady cold light when you turned it in your palm, and lately had begun to flicker and fail. "It's dying," she said, turning it, the light stuttering across her tired face. "They do. Everything the old world made dies eventually, same as everything else. I only wish it'd lasted me out."

Oscar took it because he could not not take it. That was the truth of him, the truth under all the rest: a thing that held a working put itself into his hands the way a question puts itself to a scholar, and he turned it, and he *looked*—the small interior figure of it resolving for him the way the husks and the wards and the great dead city had resolved, the old grammar plain as print. And he saw at once what was wrong: not the working, which was sound, but a single hairline fracture in the figure's binding, a place where the centuries had worn one line of it thin. The kind of thing he could mend with a thought, now that he had thoughts to spare again.

He mended it. He barely had to reach for the work; a touch, a single small true figure laid over the fracture the way you splint a cracked bone, and the grey lozenge flared up clean and steady and *warm* for the first time in years, bright enough to throw shadows, and Brida made a small sound and nearly dropped it.

"That's—" she started. "How did you—nobody can do that. The factors say it can't be done, that when they die they're dead, that's why you have to keep buying—" She stopped. Her tired eyes went sharp, and then, a half-second later, afraid. "You can *read* them. The old work. You can read it and you can mend it."

"It's not difficult," Oscar said, which was the wrong thing, and which he knew was the wrong thing the instant Kessa's head came up.

Because it *was* difficult. It was, he understood a beat too late, watching the fear arrive on Brida's face, very possibly the most valuable thing a person could do in a town built on dying old-world relics that everyone had to keep replacing through the Tithe—and the most dangerous thing to be known for, in a town where the Tithe took a cut of everything and did not tolerate anyone who could give the salvagers a reason not to keep buying.

"You can't let anyone see that," Brida said, very low, pressing the warm bright lamp into his hands as if to hide it. "You understand? Not anyone. There are people in this town who'd pay a great deal to own a man who can do that. And there are people who'd pay more to be sure no one else could."

"Bit late," said Wick, from the window.

She had drifted to the loft's one small gap that looked down on the street, the way Wick drifted toward any view, and she said it lightly, the way she said most things, and then she did not say anything else, and the quality of her not-saying brought every one of them off the bench.

Down in the street, where the morning crowd was thinning toward the dead heat of midday, the scar-mouthed tallyman from the gate—Sarn, a salvager had called them, with the particular hush people used for the cord—stood at the mouth of the lane that led up to Brida's, and was not bored anymore. They had three of the red-corded with them, and a folded paper in one hand, and they were looking up the lane at the boarding house with the patient certainty of someone who has finished doing their sums and arrived at a number they like.

"That's about me," Oscar said. He knew it the way he'd known the door in the tower, the way he'd known the frost-shedding thing in the cradle—an animal certainty under the clever centuries. "That's not the debt and it's not the lamp. That paper's about me." He stepped back from the window. "Brida. Is there another way down out of this house that doesn't end at that lane."

"Through the back, along the rib, there's a salvager's run—" She was already moving, fear making her quick. "But they'll have watched the runs, if they're cord—"

"They'll have watched the runs," Kessa agreed, crossbow already in her hands, reading the room and the angles and the trouble the way she read a slope. "Which means we don't take the runs. Cael, the back. Wick, you stay on Oscar and you do not light anything unless I say, you hear me, not one spark in a crowded town. Oscar—" her flat eyes found him "—whatever that paper says you're worth, I'd like to keep it theoretical. Move."

They moved. And as Oscar went down the back stair of a kind stranger's house with a mended lamp still warm in his coat and a folded paper with his name—or his shape, or his *kind*—on it climbing the lane behind him, he understood two things at once, and neither was comforting.

The first was that he was not, in this town, an anonymous stranger passing through. Somebody had wanted him specifically enough to put it on paper and pay the cord to read it.

The second was that he had no idea why. Not the faintest idea. He knew he was made; he had stood in a font and learned that much and spent every day since trying to unlearn it. He knew, now, that he was wanted. But the line between those two facts—the *why* of it, the *what it meant*—was a dark he could not see the bottom of, and he was running down a stranger's stairs away from people who knew something about him that he did not know about himself.

He found he liked that least of all. He had spent three centuries—

He had spent, he corrected himself, even now, even running, a long time being the one in the room who knew the most. He did not at all care for being the one who knew the least, especially when the subject was himself.

Behind them, up the lane, a fist hammered on Brida's door, and the tallyman's bored quiet voice said something Oscar didn't catch, and the day's grace, such as it had been, was spent.

## Chapter Twelve — Kindling

The hardest thing in the world, Wick had decided a long time ago, was holding still while you burned.

Not real fire. She wished it were real fire; real fire you could put out. This was the other thing, the thing that lived under her skin and especially under the mark at her throat, the heat that came up when she was scared or angry or just *too much* of anything, and right now she was all three, running crouched through the dark under Marrow with the cord somewhere behind them and her whole body wanting, wanting, *wanting* to let go.

*No sparks,* Kessa had said. *Not one. Not in a town full of people and old dry timber.*

So Wick held it. She held it the way you hold your breath underwater, with her jaw tight and her fists tighter and the heat banked down to a coal in her chest, and it hurt, the holding, it always hurt, and nobody ever seemed to understand that the not-using was the hard part. They all looked at her like she was a problem that might go off. None of them got that she spent every waking minute *not* going off, and that it was the most exhausting thing she did, more exhausting than running, more exhausting than being hungry, which she also was.

Oscar got it, maybe. A little. He'd said the aiming was the hard part, but she thought he'd had it backwards, or half-right—the aiming was hard, sure, but the *holding* was harder, and he was the only one who'd ever made her feel like the holding was a thing she was strong for doing instead of a bomb they were all standing next to.

She'd have walked into the deep dark for him just for that. As it turned out, she more or less did.

They'd gone down into the bones to hide. That was what the salvagers called it, *the bones,* like the town sat in the skeleton of some dead giant, and they all said it that way, low and spooky, because *the bones* sounded better than the truth. But it wasn't bones. Wick had worked that out her first hour in Marrow, just by looking. It was a *city.* An old one, an impossibly old one, so huge that the town up top had grown inside it the way moss grows in a cracked pot—a whole second city buried under the first, tunnels and shafts and rooms built for nothing human-sized, all of it stone, all of it just *city,* and not one inch of it ever alive enough to have a bone in it. Grown-ups liked the giant better. The giant was a story, and *very large dead town* wasn't a story, it was just sad, and you couldn't sell sad to a salvager. Either way the cord didn't like to follow down here, because even the Tithe couldn't watch every corner of a buried city the size of a country. Brida had told them a way in before they ran. Kessa was leading. Cael was at the back being a wall. And Oscar was in the middle reading the walls the way he always did, his lips moving a little, his eyes catching that faint cold shine that elf-eyes did in the dark, and Wick was supposed to be on him, right on him, that was her one job—*stay on Oscar, don't light anything*—

—and that was the exact moment she heard the cord coming up the cross-tunnel ahead. The way ahead. The way Kessa was about to lead them straight into.

There wasn't time to say it right. There was barely time to think it. If she shouted, the cord would hear. If she did nothing, they'd walk into a patrol in a dead-end dark with the kid's-one-job-was-to-not-make-noise, and Oscar would be the one they wanted, and the whole thing would go wrong, and it would be her fault for not—

She did the stupid brave thing. She was good at the stupid brave thing. It was practically the only kind of brave she had.

She peeled off sideways into a side-shaft, the one going the wrong way, the one going *toward* the cord, and she let exactly one spark go—one, small, the size of a coin, flicked off her fingers into a heap of dry salvage-tinder twenty feet down the side-shaft—and it caught with a *whump* and a flare of orange light, and she heard the cord's bored voices turn sharp and excited and go *that* way, toward the fire, toward the noise, toward her, away from the cross-tunnel where her crew was.

It worked. That was the thing she'd hold onto later, when the rest of it had gone bad: it *worked.* They went for the bait. Kessa's path was clear.

And then Wick was alone in the bones of Marrow with a fire behind her and the cord between her and her people, and the only way left was down.

She went down.

She wasn't scared yet, not really, not for the first while. She was *proud,* which she knew even then was the kind of feeling that gets people killed, but she couldn't help it—she'd done it, she'd been useful, she'd been the one who saw it and acted, not the liability, not the bomb, the one who *saved* them—and the pride carried her down three turns and a long sloped shaft into the deep bones before it ran out and left her standing in a dark so complete it had a texture, breathing air that tasted of cold stone and older things, with no idea at all where she was.

The pride ran out somewhere down there in the dark, and the thing that came in to take its place was the cold thing, the *alone* thing. She had never been properly alone before—not like this, not in a dark with a texture to it and no bottom and no Kessa's-steps to set her feet in. She kept one hand flat on the wall and the other curled around the small banked coal of her own heat, the only warm thing left in the world, and she told herself she'd find a shaft, find air, find them. She told herself a great many things. The dark ate every one.

She didn't have long to be frightened of it, because that was when the thing in the dark found her.

She heard it before she saw it, which was wrong, because she couldn't see anything—a dry scraping shuffle, many-legged, coming along the tunnel toward her, and a smell like a cellar that something had died in. She backed up. It came on. And she realized, with the heat already rising in her without her telling it to, that it was coming *straight* for her, unerring in the pitch dark, and she knew why: it couldn't see her either. It could feel her. It could feel the heat coming off her, the coal in her chest, the warm living furnace that she was and couldn't ever fully bank, and down here in the cold bones she must have lit up to a thing like that like a bonfire on a hill.

*No sparks,* said Kessa's voice in her head. *Not one.*

"Yeah, well," Wick said, out loud, to the dark, her voice cracking on it, "Kessa's not *here,*" and she let go.

She meant to do it the way Oscar taught her. One thread, one fist-sized place, aimed. She honestly meant to.

Then it came into the light of her own kindling, and the aiming went clean out of her hands.

It was a spider. Not the word she'd have said out loud—out loud she'd have had a worse word—but that was the body her eyes found in the orange light: eight legs, jointed and bone-pale and long as her arms, folding and reaching and feeling at the floor, carrying a swollen sac of a body low and heavy behind a smooth blind head. No eyes anywhere on it that she could find. Just that pale head, and underneath it a busy wet working of mouthparts that opened and opened. It had no color on it at all; nothing down here did. And it came straight at the warmest thing in the dark, which was her, fast and certain and patient all at once—not a monster, that was the worst part, just a *beast,* an honest blind animal that had spent its whole life in the bones eating whatever wandered down, that wanted to eat her and felt nothing at all about it either way.

She reached for the heat the way she had in the ruined street the day she nearly killed everyone—reached for *all* of it—

—and there was more there than there had ever been.

That was the part she would never be able to explain, after, even to herself. The heat she knew was a coal in her chest, a thing with a bottom to it, a thing she could fill her hands with and then empty. But cornered and alone and more afraid than she had ever been in her life, she reached down *past* the coal—and there was no bottom there. Past the coal there was more, and past that more there was *more,* a depth opening under her like a trapdoor onto a furnace with no floor: something vast, and old, and *alive,* something that had always been down there behind the mark at her throat, on the far side of a door she'd never once found the back of. And when she reached into it, it reached *back.* It noticed her. She felt the notice happen—an attention swinging toward her up out of the deep the way a great slow head turns in the dark, ancient and unhurried and *glad,* glad clean through, that she had finally, finally opened the door.

The power it poured into her was not the coal. It came up through her in a roaring flood that was hers and not hers at the same time, and the spider did not so much burn as simply *end*—a single white clap of heat that lit the tunnel like the inside of a forge and left a scorch and a smell and nothing else—

—and she couldn't stop.

That was the thing. That was the thing that turned the pride to terror and would stay with her long after the burn was gone: she reached to shut the door, to bank it, to *stop,* the way she always could, the one thing about herself she had ever been sure of—and it would not shut. The thing on the far side had her in its attention now and it was *pouring* through, eager, endless, glad, and she felt her edges going thin and bright, felt the heat begin to burn her *outward,* felt the hungry gladness come climbing up through the open door to take the rest of her—and not to kill her. That was what she understood, with an awful child's-clarity, in the half-second she had to understand anything. It did not want to kill her. It wanted to *keep* her. To draw her down through the door and make her part of whatever it was, until there was no Wick left at all, only the door, and the thing that came through it.

She slammed the door with everything she had. With her whole self. With the part of her that wanted to live more than it wanted to be powerful, which she hadn't been totally sure existed until right then.

It shut.

She came back to herself on her knees in the dark, shaking, the mark gone cold and quiet at her throat, the tunnel black again and stinking of ash, her own breath sawing loud in the silence. Whatever she'd touched was gone, back down below the floor of her, sleeping or waiting or whatever it did. She was alive. She was herself. She'd almost not been, and nobody would ever, ever know how close it had been, and she made a decision right there on her knees in the dark that she did not examine and would not have been able to defend: she was not going to tell them. Not Kessa, who'd look at her like the bomb again. Not Cael, who'd pray over her. Not even Oscar.

*Especially* not Oscar. Because Oscar would understand it, and she could not bear, just then, to be understood.

She didn't have to find her way back. They found her—she heard them before she saw the bobbing light of Brida's mended lamp, heard Cael's big careful voice calling her name with an edge of real fear in it that made her eyes sting, heard Kessa's flat *there she is* that somehow had more relief in it than a shout would have. And then there was light, and there were her people, and Cael was crushing her into a hug that smelled of sweat and iron and safety, and she let him, and she did not cry, and she was very proud of not crying.

"That was reckless," Kessa said, but her voice wasn't hard. "And it worked, and it saved our skins, and if you ever do it again I'll tie you to me with a rope. Both can be true." Which, from Kessa, was practically a song.

And then there was Oscar, crouching down to her level the way grown-ups never did, looking at her with those cold-shine elf-eyes, and she braced for him to *know,* to see the ash and the shaking and put it together, because he saw everything—

—but he didn't look at her like she was a problem. He looked at the smear of ash where the thing had been, and then back at her, and what was on his face wasn't suspicion. It was something that looked, impossibly, like *respect.*

"You held it together," he said quietly. "Down here, alone, with something coming at you. You held it together and you walked away." He didn't ask how. She loved him a little, in that moment, for not asking how. "That's the whole job, Wick. That's the entire job. Everything else is just decoration."

And then he stood, and turned, and read them a way out.

That was the part she'd remember—not the thing in the dark, not the door that wouldn't shut, but *that.* The way he laid his hand against the old dead wall of the deepest bones of Marrow and the cold shine in his eyes brightened and he simply *knew,* the way nobody could know, where the tunnels went and which ones would hold and which way was air. The walls talked to him. The dead world's bones, the thing that had nearly eaten her alive from the inside, the place that had opened a door in her she hadn't known was there—he walked through it like it was his front hall, like it was *his,* unafraid of any of it, reading a way home for all of them out of a language no one living was supposed to be able to read.

He was magnificent. He was the most magnificent thing she had ever seen.

She wanted, with a want that was bigger than the heat and steadier than the fear, to be like that someday. To stand in the dark with all of it coming at her and not be afraid, because she *understood* it. She wanted him to teach her until she did. She'd been calling herself his apprentice in her head for days now, secretly, the way you try on a coat in a shop you can't afford—and watching him read them out of the bones, she stopped trying it on. She just decided it was true.

There was one thing. One small thing she noticed and didn't know where to put, so she put it away with everything else she wasn't saying. It was the way the dead place *let* him. The bones had nearly eaten her alive from the inside; they'd opened a door in her she'd spent the last hour trying to forget; they scared her down to the soles of her feet. And Oscar walked through them like a person crossing his own front hall in the dark—touching the walls, never once afraid—and the walls, it was a silly thought and she knew it was silly, the walls seemed almost *glad* to tell him the way. Like the dead city knew him. Like it had been waiting a long time for him to come home.

She decided that was the most wonderful thing about him. Not the power; she had power, and look what hers had nearly done. It was the other part—that the dark wasn't dark to him, that the thing everyone else was afraid of just *answered* when he asked it nicely. That was what she wanted. To not be scared anymore, because she finally understood.

So she followed her teacher up toward the light, and she kept her own door shut, and she told no one—not Kessa, not Cael, not even him—what had reached back through it in the dark, or how glad it had been.

## Chapter Thirteen — The Writ

For two days they lived like rats in the walls of the world, and Oscar discovered he was better at it than a man of his former standing had any right to be.

They had gone to ground in a forgotten pocket of the deep city—a high dry chamber Oscar had read them to off the maps in his head, a place the cord had no reason to know and the salvagers had long since stripped and forgotten. It had one way in, which Kessa loved, and a hairline crack to the open air that let the smoke of a small fire out somewhere it wouldn't be seen, which Cael loved, and absolutely nothing else to recommend it, which nobody loved but everybody accepted. They ate what Kessa could buy quietly with their four coppers and what Cael could charm out of the few salvagers who'd risk a kindness. They slept in turns. They did not go up into the light, because up in the light there was a paper with Oscar on it, and until they knew what the paper said, up in the light was a place a person could disappear from.

Oscar spent the two days being, for him, remarkably quiet.

The others noticed. Wick kept shooting him looks, the worried kind, and Cael kept finding small reasons to sit near him, and even Kessa's flat attention snagged on him more than usual. He knew why. He had stopped performing. The arch voice, the cataloguing, the small constant theatre of being the cleverest thing in the room—he hadn't the strength for it, not since the lane, not since he'd watched the cord come up the hill for him specifically and understood, in the part of him that didn't lie, that the thing he'd been telling himself for days was about to stop being tellable.

Because he had told himself a story. He could admit that now, in the dark, with nothing to do but admit things. He had told himself that the font had been a fever. That he'd been thin and frightened and half-unmade and a dying city had whispered something cruel and he'd believed it the way you believe a nightmare, and that every day since—every day his power came back a little, every figure that held, every drift-cat thrown and lamp mended—had been proof that he was, after all, only *himself,* Oscar, who had simply been ill and was getting well. He had been so good at telling himself that story. He'd had three hundred years of practice telling himself flattering stories. Give or take. Fewer, lately, by his own private accounting.

A standing bounty does not care for a man's flattering stories. That was the trouble.

Kessa came back on the second evening with the paper.

She'd been gone since midday, up in the light wearing a salvager's hood and someone else's walk, and she came back down into the chamber and crouched by the small fire and did not say anything for a moment, which from Kessa was a whole paragraph. Then she drew a folded square of cheap paper from inside her coat and set it on the stone between them, flat, the way you set down a thing you don't want to be holding.

"Cost me the last of the coppers and a lie I'll have to keep straight," she said. "There's dozens of them about. The cord all carry one. I lifted this off a drunk one in a salvager's tap, so if a cord comes looking for a missing writ, that's where it went, not here." She nudged it toward Oscar with one finger. "I can read a slope. I can't read this hand worth a damn—it's southern, lawyer's writing. But I know the knot they've drawn at the head of it, or I know its like, and I went and spent our last coin to make sure I was wrong about it." Her pale eyes found him, and there was something in them he hadn't seen there before. Not suspicion. Nearer to dread. "I wasn't wrong. You're the scholar. Read us what we're worth."

Oscar unfolded it.

It was, he saw at once, a careful document, and careful in a way that frightened him more than crude would have. Crude would have meant a local grudge, a bar fight, a debt. This was a *contract,* clerked and copied and circulated, the southern hand precise and bloodless, and at the head of it, drawn small and exact, was the closed loop. The knot without ends. The mark on his sword. The mark, faint as an old scar, healed into the web of his own right hand.

He read it twice. The first time for the words. The second time because the first time his eyes had simply refused.

"It's a standing contract," he said. His voice came out level, which surprised him, because nothing else in him was. "Not a one-off. It doesn't expire. It *renews*—there's a clause, here, it's reissued each season by a factor in the south and paid against a southern house, which means whoever wants this has wanted it a long time and means to go on wanting it." He made himself keep going, because stopping was worse. "It's well-paid. Absurdly. More than a season's salvage. More than this whole town clears in a—" He stopped. "It's a great deal of money, is the point, and it's good money, southern gold, the kind the factors here would crawl over their own mothers for."

"For you," Wick said, very quietly. She'd come to sit close, the way she did. "It's all that money. For you."

"Not for me." And this was the part that had been waiting at the bottom of the page, the part his eyes had refused. "Not for Oscar. There's no name on it. There's no face. There's only—" he turned the paper so they could see the small exact drawing at its head "—this. The mark. The contract isn't for a man. It's for *anyone who carries this,* delivered to a factor of the southern house, and—" his throat did something unhelpful "—delivered *alive.* Whole. Unhurt. There's a penalty clause for damage. They want the marked brought in unspoiled." He set the paper down because his hand had begun, very slightly, to betray him. "Someone with a very great deal of money has a standing, permanent, patient interest in collecting people who bear this mark, and they want them brought in *alive and undamaged,* and they have wanted this for longer than anyone in this room has been—"

*Alive.* He kept catching on it. He had been hunted before, across the borrowed centuries; he knew, somewhere, the shape of being wanted dead. A man understood being wanted dead. You had angered someone, or you stood between them and a thing, and they wished you gone, and there was a grim sense to it. But *alive.* Whole. Unspoiled, with a penalty for damage, like a crate of something fragile. Someone he had never met, in a south he had never seen, wanted him delivered intact, for a purpose the contract did not name and did not need to, because everyone in that southern house already knew what the marked were *for*—everyone but the marked.

"There's a word here I don't know," he said, and hated how small it came out. "An instruction. For the cord. It says—" he found it again, the southern clerk's neat hand "—it says the marked are to be taken *before they are set.* That a marked one already set is of no value and may be left or struck. They only pay for the *unset.*"

The fire cracked. No one said anything.

He knew that word. That was the thing. He did not know what the contract meant by it—did not know what it was to be *set,* in the sense a southern lawyer meant it, did not know why an unset one was worth a southern fortune and a set one worth nothing at all. But he knew the *word,* knew it in his teeth, because a dying city had said it to him at the bottom of the world. *Be set, and be whole, and inherit what is yours.* The font had wanted to set him. And here was a contract, copied a hundred times and carried by a hundred thugs, that wanted him taken *before* it could—and the two facts sat side by side in his head and would not, would not, combine into anything he could understand, and the not-understanding was a worse fear than any monster in any tunnel, because the monster he could at least see.

He knew he was made. He had known that since the font. He knew, now, that he was wanted—wanted alive, wanted whole, wanted *unset,* by money that reached up from the south through a hundred red cords. But the line between those things, the *why,* the *what it was all for*—that was a dark with no bottom, and he had spent two days in the literal dark and found it more comfortable than this one.

"You'd know what to do with this," he said, very low, to no one—to the cold spot on his shoulder where a dry voice would once have said something unforgivable and made the whole crushing thing a size a person could carry. There was no weight there. There hadn't been since he woke. But it was nearer now, that absence; he'd felt it for days, a pull at the edge of him like a word on the tip of a tongue, closer every time, and never close enough to reach. He let it ache. He'd stopped pretending it didn't.

Kessa let the quiet stretch, and then she set down the thing she'd carried into the chamber and hadn't wanted to lay on the fire-stone with the rest.

"I've heard of you," she said. "Not you. *That.*" She tipped her chin at the paper without looking at it. "Salvagers tell it, late in a long season, when the drink's gone and the dark's leaning in. The ghost-writ, some name it. A king's fortune in southern gold, standing forever, for a marked one brought in *breathing.* No name, no face, just a knot with no ends—and you fetch the one who wears it, and you keep them whole, and you never ask what for." Her jaw worked. "Thirty years I've heard it, a hundred different ways, and I never met a soul who'd seen one, nor one who could say what the mark meant, nor what became of a marked one after the lid came down. I shelved it with the talking wolves and the warm rivers under the ice. A tale folk tell so the world still feels like it's got money and mystery left in it." She looked at him at last. "And here you sit by my fire."

"I've heard it too," Cael said. The quiet way of it turned every head; he was not a man who volunteered. "A different road, a different mouth, the same bones to the thing. A bounty none can collect, for a man none can find, paid out of a house none has seen." His hand rested on the iron at his throat. "My order keeps a saying for stories shaped like that one. *A true thing in a liar's coat, so the world won't look straight at it.* I always took it for a comfort. I am less comforted tonight."

So that was the size of it, Oscar thought. Not unknown. *Worse* than unknown. Known at every fire in every town from the ice to the south—as a story, a shiver, a pleasant impossible thing that befell no one and never would. The whole world had heard of him. Not one soul had believed in him. And in all those tellings, across all those years, no single voice had ever carried the answer to the one question that had him by the throat, which was *why.*

"All right," Kessa said at last, the story put away and the working flatness back in her voice. "So somebody rich and far away wants you in a box, unbroken, for reasons that'd probably turn the stomach. That's bad. But it's also two hundred miles away and slow, and the people who actually want to put you in that box are *here.*" She tapped the paper. "This came up from the south through the cord. The Tithe's the hand that'd grab you. Which means the Tithe is the thing in front of us, and the south is the thing behind it, and we've only got the one of those we can punch."

"We could run," Cael said—but he said it like a person checking a door he already knew was locked. "Slip out the way we came, lose ourselves in the back-country, go where there's no cord and no writ."

"And leave Brida to answer for us," Kessa said.

That landed in the middle of them and sat there.

"They went up her lane," Kessa went on, flat, merciless because the truth was merciless. "With a writ. To a house that fed us. The cord doesn't forget a thing like that, and they don't make the stranger pay—they can't find the stranger. They make the one who's still standing there pay, because that's how you teach a town not to feed strays. We run, and the bill for us lands on a woman with a dead husband and a bad arm and a debt she can't carry as it is." She looked around the fire. "I'm not telling anybody what to do. I've run from worse and slept fine. I'm only saying what the running costs, since somebody at this fire's got a habit of saying the hard thing and he's gone quiet on us."

Oscar looked up.

It was, he thought, the most pointed kindness anyone had done him in a week—Kessa handing him back his own worst trait and asking him to use it. He had a gift for saying the unbearable true thing. He'd wounded a grieving man with it by a fire down in the dead city and learned, slowly, to swallow it. But there was a difference, he was beginning to understand, between swallowing a cruelty and swallowing a truth, and the truth here was not cruel. It only needed saying.

"We're not going to run," he said.

He said it plainly, and he felt the whole chamber turn toward the plainness of it, the way they'd turned toward him on a glacier when he'd told them to move. He was doing the thing again, the thing he kept not-quite-doing, stepping into the wheel-shaped gap at the head of a crew that had been lurching without a head since they lost the one who used to fill it. He felt the gap take his weight. He let it.

"Here is the arithmetic, since we're being honest about arithmetic." He laid his hand flat on the writ. "I cannot outrun a contract that renews itself every season for the rest of a very long life. I cannot hide from a mark that's healed into my own skin. The only place the answer to *why* lives is south, behind the cord, in a house I can't reach—and the only road south that doesn't end with me in a box runs straight through the thing that owns this town. So. The Tithe is between me and the truth, and it's between Brida and a free breath, and it's between every soul in this valley and a life that isn't a ledger. That's a great many reasons pointing the same direction, and I have learned to be suspicious of my own cleverness, but even I can't argue with a sum that clean." He looked around the fire—at the woman who read slopes, the man who'd run out of *no,* the girl who'd nearly come apart in the dark and told no one. *His* crew. He hadn't meant to start thinking of them as his. "We break the Tithe. We free the town that sheltered us, we cut the hand that reaches for me, and somewhere in the breaking we find the thread that runs south, and I follow it until I know what I am." A breath. "I'd rather do it with you. But I'll understand the one who'd rather not."

Wick said "I'm in" before he'd finished the sentence, which he'd expected. Cael set his big hand over the iron token at his throat and said, "I came down here to do a hard thing and went home having done a shameful one. I'd like to swap those, if it's all the same," which he hadn't expected, and which cost the man something to say, and which Oscar would not forget.

And Kessa looked at the three of them for a long moment, the firelight working in the lines of her weathered face, and then she reached out and turned the writ over so the loop didn't have to look at them anymore.

"A salvage town run by thieves," she said, "with a vault nobody can crack, a town full of people who hate them, and the four of us with no money and a price on the wizard's head." Something that was almost, almost a smile touched the corner of her mouth. "Doss would've called that a bad job and walked away." The smile went. "But Doss isn't here, and we already learned what it costs to leave a thing to the dark because the odds were ugly." She picked up her crossbow and laid it across her knees, which was Kessa for *yes.* "So. Tell me about this vault."

## Chapter Fourteen — The Descent

The good claim had a reputation, and the way down to it earned every word.

Kessa took them by the back route, the one the Tithe didn't watch because the Tithe didn't know it was there—a fault in the old city's bones that Oscar read open ahead of them turning by turning, hand to the cold stone, the dead grammar unfolding the safe way down through a place that had a great many unsafe ones. They had what they'd come with and what they'd decided: take the deep haul, take the proof, prove the thing could be done. What they did not have, descending, was any illusion left about the cost. Kessa had said it plainest, before they started, in the way she said the things that mattered.

"People go down to the good claim," she'd said. "Fewer come up. The ones that don't, mostly, are still down there. So watch where you step. You'll be stepping on them."

She had not been speaking poetically.

The first body was a quarter-mile down, folded into a side-niche as if it had crawled there to be out of the way of its own dying. Salvager's leathers, gone stiff. A pick still in one hand. Wick made a small sound and then made herself stop. Cael stood over the dead stranger a moment with his head bowed and his lips moving, the small private mercy he did for everything that had stopped living, and then they went on, because there was nothing else to do and a long way still to go.

There were more after that. The good claim had been eating crews for a long time.

It was the third body that changed the shape of the day.

This one had died sitting, back to the wall, in a wide gallery where the old workings in the walls had begun, faintly, to wake—a salvager in better gear than the others, and propped against their knees, half-fallen, was a shield. Not a salvager's buckler. A real shield, broad and old, the metal of it dark and strange and worked all over with a fine raised tracery that Oscar felt pull at his attention from twenty feet away, the way a held figure always did.

He crouched and looked at it, and the cold legible shine came up behind his eyes, and he read it, and went still.

"This isn't dead work," he said. "It's sleeping. There's a whole figure in it, intact—a good one, better than good, better than anything I could write." He ran a finger just above the tracery without touching it, following the shape of the working the way you'd follow a line of beautiful handwriting. "It's a ward. A real one. You set it, you feed it, and it raises a wall of warding around the anchor—stops a blow, stops a fall of stone, stops most things that come at you with force behind them." He sat back on his heels. "It's been asleep for an age because there's been no one to wake it. The charge it held ran out a hundred lifetimes ago. It just needs power, and a hand to give it." He looked up—not at himself. At Cael. "And it wants a protector. I can read that much in how it's made. It was written for someone whose whole purpose was standing between."

He lifted it free of the dead salvager's stiff hands, which took some doing, and held it out. "This one's yours."

Cael took it the way a man takes something he's afraid to want. It was heavy; he was not. He turned it, and looked at the dark old tracery, and then—because Oscar told him how, low and careful—he closed his eyes and did the thing his faith had taught him, reached for the warm granted strength that came to him when he asked it to protect, and he *poured it in.*

The shield woke.

The tracery filled—not with the cold blue-white shine that the old work always gave under Oscar's hand, but with a deep and living *warmth,* a glow the color of banked coals, of lamplight under a door, of a hearth at the end of a hard cold road. It spread up the dark metal and hung there, steady and golden, and the air around Cael went suddenly close and safe in a way none of them could have named, and Wick actually stepped toward it the way you step toward a fire on a freezing night.

"Huh," said Oscar, with the particular delight of a man watching his understanding of the world get larger. "It ran cold for me. It runs warm for you. Same figure—the relic's only the shape of the thing; the power that fills it is yours, and yours comes with the temperature of what you are." He was almost laughing, here in a tomb, over a dead stranger's shield. "I have spent a very long time thinking I understood how this all worked. I love being wrong about it. Genuinely. It's the best thing that happens to me."

Cael wasn't listening to the lecture. He was looking at the warm shield, and then down at the salvager he'd taken it from, and the joy went out of the moment for him in a way it took Oscar a beat too long to follow.

"It didn't save them," Cael said quietly.

And there it was, the cold under the wonder. The finest protective working any of them had ever seen, a hearth you could carry, a wall against the world—and it had been sitting in the lap of a corpse, in the dark, a quarter-mile above the thing that had made the corpse. Whatever was down there, the shield had not been enough. Oscar watched Cael understand that, and watched him decide to carry the thing anyway, and thought that there was probably no clearer picture of what Cael was than a man who would shoulder a shield *because* it had failed the last hand that held it, as if to argue with the fact.

Kessa, meanwhile, had been doing what Kessa did, which was robbing the dead with brisk unsentimental respect, and she came up from a fourth body—red cord at the wrist, a Tithe man, gone the same way as all the rest—holding a pair of boots.

"Tithe crew," she said. "Their own. The good ones get the good gear, and these are old-world made." She turned them over; the soles were of some dark supple stuff, seamless, strange. "Quiet-soles. You walk in these and the ground doesn't hear you. The Tithe works the claim in silence, remember—wraps their boots in rag. These poor fools didn't need rag." She was already pulling off her own worn boots, pragmatic as ever. "Didn't help him in the end either. But it might help us get past the thing that got him." She laced them, stood, took two steps—and made no sound at all, none, a hunter's tread gone utterly silent on old stone. Something that was almost satisfaction crossed her face. "Well. That's unfair. I like it."

"You're robbing his grave," Wick observed.

"He's robbed half this valley from up top," Kessa said. "I'm evening a ledger. Move."

They moved—better armed than they'd come, and quieter, and more afraid, because the gear of the dead is a fine thing to have and a terrible thing to think about.

The spiders came at them in the dark below the galleries, and the spiders were running.

Oscar understood that almost at once, which was the only reason the first rush didn't end someone. They came not as hunters but as a flood—a dozen of the pale bone-legged things, the kin of the one that had nearly taken Wick, pouring up out of the deeper dark in a panic, not stalking but *fleeing,* and only turning on the warm living crew because the crew was in the way and warm and there. They hunted by heat. Four bodies and a girl who burned hotter than all of them, in the cold, lit them up like a bonfire, and a fleeing thing that runs into food does not keep running.

"Down!" Kessa had a bolt away before the word finished. "Cael—the shield, now, give us a wall—Wick, *you do not light up,* you'll call every one of them and worse besides—"

Cael drove the shield's edge down into the grit and poured himself into it, and the hearth-dome bloomed—a half-sphere of warm golden warding, ten feet across, and the first spiders hit it and could not come through, their bone-pale legs scrabbling at a wall of force they couldn't see and couldn't pass. It held. It *held,* and for one breath the crew had a fortress in the dark.

But the dome didn't move, and it didn't tire the spiders, and there were a dozen, and Oscar saw the trap of it as fast as the salvation: turtle here and Cael drained dry while the swarm massed, and then the wall would fall with all of them inside it. The shield bought time. It didn't spend the threat. They had to do that.

"We can't wait it out," he said. "Cael holds the dome—the rest of us go out and end this, fast, before he's empty. Kessa, left. I'll take right. Wick—" he caught the look on her face, the terror and the awful straining want, the fire in her screaming to be let loose against the things that haunted her own worst night "—Wick, you've got the cleanest aim of any of us, so you make it count. Small. Aimed. One at a time. Nothing past what you can hold—" he held her eyes "—nothing more than that. You don't need it. You never needed it. The aiming was always the rare thing."

She heard him. He watched her hear him. And then she did the thing he could not have taught her, the thing that was hers and hers alone: she reached for what she could hold and *only* that, no further, not a spark past it, and she put a tight lash of fire through one spider's body, clean, and it dropped, and she didn't spill, and she didn't break, and her hands stayed her own.

It was, all told, a short and ugly and well-fought thing. Oscar took the right with the lance and the Measure—the lance throwing them off their feet, the blade finishing what the lance started—and found, with a grim private satisfaction, that the figures came when he called them now, every time, the way they were supposed to. Kessa flowed along the left in her stolen silent boots, a bolt and then a blade, never where the spiders expected the warmth to be. Wick burned them down two and three at a time, aimed and aimed and aimed, the holding and the loosing both hers to command. And Cael knelt in the center of it with the hearth blazing golden around them all, jaw clenched, pouring out his strength, the still warm point the whole fight turned around—the one who stood between, finally with the right thing in his hands.

When it was done, the gallery was littered with pale dead, and Cael let the dome fall and sat down hard, grey-faced and emptied, and Wick went and sat against him without a word, and they breathed.

"You held us," Oscar told him.

"It held us," Cael said, one hand flat on the dark warm metal, and didn't argue further, which was as close to taking the credit as he came.

They did not talk much after that. The spiders had been running from something, and they were going the way the spiders had come.

The deeper they went, the more the old workings woke—the walls veined now with live seams that pulsed faint and slow like something sleeping, the richest the buried world had to offer, the reason the Tithe fed crews to this place season on season. And the quieter it got. Oscar found himself setting each foot down with care, found his breath going shallow, found that the silence here was not the absence of sound but a held thing, a pressure, the quiet of a room where something is listening. He raised a hand. They stopped. He pointed at his own feet, then at his own ear, and the crew understood: *from here, we are not heard.* Kessa, silent-shod, took the lead. The rest of them learned to walk like ghosts.

And then the bones opened up into the good claim, and they saw what kept it.

The chamber was vast—a cathedral of the old world, walls dripping with live workings, a fortune in light, the haul Brida's husband had died for stacked and glowing at the far end. And between, on the floor of it, among the scattered bones of everyone who had ever come this far, the thing the Tithe crews called the hush lay waiting, and it was no beast.

It was a monstrosity.

It was vast and low and pale, the grey-white of a thing that had never once needed color, bigger than a house, and it had no eyes anywhere upon it because it had long since had no use for them—only, where a face should have been, two great trembling fans of membrane spread wide to drink the dark for sound. Its hide was not hide. It was armor, and the armor was *made of the dead*—picks and shovels and salvager's iron, ribs and skulls and the long bones of crews beyond counting, all of it swallowed and fused and grown into the thing's grey flesh over an age of feeding, a carapace of everyone it had ever eaten, worn like plate. And its whole vast front, where the worst of any animal's hunger lives, was a *maw*—a slow grinding cavern of a mouth, ringed in plates that turned against each other like millstones, wide enough to take a person whole and never hurry about it.

It made no sound. That was the horror Oscar's mind kept circling even as the rest of him went cold: a thing that size, breathing, moving its bulk among the bones, and *silent,* because silence was the whole of how it lived and killed—it had spent ten thousand years learning to make no noise, so that the only noise in its dark would ever be the prey.

The haul was thirty feet past it. There was no way to the haul that did not go through it. And it had not yet heard them, because they had not yet, by some grace, made a sound it could love.

Oscar looked at the membrane fans, and the seam beneath them, and the millstone maw, and the carapace of the swallowed dead, and the long silent patient bulk of the thing between his crew and everything they'd come for—and he understood, with the cold clarity that was the truest part of him, that the plan he'd half-formed up above was about to meet the place where plans go to be proven wrong.

He raised one hand, slow, and the crew gathered close, and in the breathing dark at the edge of the good claim, they made ready to wake the hush.

## Chapter Fifteen — The Good Claim

The plan was good. Oscar wanted that on the record, afterward, when there was an afterward: the plan was good, and it worked exactly as designed, and that was the whole problem.

The hush hunted by sound, so they gave it one. Oscar marked the seam for them first—the soft place behind the great trembling ear-fans, the one spot on the whole monstrous bulk that wasn't plated in the fused bones of everyone it had eaten—and then he picked up a chunk of old masonry, weighed it, and lobbed it the length of the chamber.

It cracked off the far wall. The hush spun toward the noise with that horrible boneless speed, its fans flaring wide, the unarmored seam swinging open to the dark for one perfect heartbeat—

—and Kessa put a bolt through it to the fletching.

For one second, it worked. The thing reared. The fans spasmed. Oscar felt the small savage spark of *yes* that came whenever a plan met the world and the world blinked first.

Then the hush did something it had not done in ten thousand years of killing in the dark.

It screamed.

The sound was enormous and wrong, a grinding shriek that came up out of that millstone maw and slammed off every wall at once, and it was worse than any roar because the thing had never needed a voice and so its voice was a horror—the sound of silence itself tearing. Wick clapped her hands over her ears. Cael flinched. And the hush, half-deafened, blind, maddened with pain, stopped being a careful hunter and became something far worse: a rampage with no plan in it at all, twelve tons of armored agony that no longer cared where the noise was because *all of it* was the enemy now.

"It's not dying," Wick said, in the small flat voice of someone watching the math go wrong. "Oscar. It's supposed to be dying."

"Noted," Oscar said, and then the hush charged the place they were all standing, and there was no more time for notes.

"*Cael!*" Kessa was already moving, hauling Wick by the collar. "Wall, now—give us a wall!"

Cael drove the hearthward into the stone and threw everything he had into it, and the dome bloomed gold a half-breath before the hush hit it. The impact was a thunderclap. The whole monstrous bulk slammed into the warding and the warding *held*—Oscar felt the figure in the shield strain to its limits and hold, felt the chamber shake, saw Cael go to one knee under the load with his teeth bared and the warm light flickering—but it held, and the hush rebounded off a wall of force it could not see, more enraged than ever.

"It can't get through," Cael ground out. "But I can't—this won't last. It's too big. It's too angry. I can give you a minute. Maybe less."

"Then we kill it in a minute." Oscar's mind was already racing down its one reliable road, looking, *looking*—not at the beast now, he'd read the beast and the beast had no soft answer, the seam only made it worse and everything else was armored in the dead. So he looked past it. He looked at the *room.* "Wick. When Cael's wall drops, you let go. All of it that's safe to let go—everything you can hold, and not one spark past that, you hear me—and you put it into the thing's face. Not to kill it. To make it flinch, to keep it off us, to buy room. Can you do that?"

Her eyes were huge. But she nodded, and there was iron in the nod. "I can do that."

"Kessa." He turned to her, and she was already looking where he was looking, because of course she was. "This chamber. You read ground better than anyone alive. Tell me what's wrong with it."

Kessa's gaze swept the cathedral dark—the dripping live seams, the vaulted stone, the floor—and snagged, and sharpened. She pointed. Past the hush, past the haul, the floor of the good claim simply *stopped*: a black gulf of a shaft, an old dig gone too deep, a pit that fell away into a dark even the live workings couldn't light. And the span of floor leading to it—the broad shelf of old stone the hush was rampaging across—was veined with cracks, undercut by the same dig, held up by Oscar-couldn't-yet-see-what.

"That edge is rotten," Kessa said. "The whole shelf. They dug under it chasing the seams and never shored it. It's been waiting a hundred years to fall." Her flat eyes cut to him. "You're thinking we drop it."

"I'm thinking we drop it."

"It weighs as much as a house."

"Then we'll need a very good push." He looked at the cracked shelf, and now he let his gift run all the way out, not on the monster but on the *stone*—the same looking that read the seams of a working or the joints of a beast, turned on the architecture of a collapse. And the chamber unfolded for him the way everything did, once he stopped being afraid to look: there. And there. Two places where the whole rotten shelf hung on a single overburdened spine of rock, and if that spine went, the floor went, and anything standing on it went with it into the black. "Kessa, can you get it onto the shelf and keep it there?"

"I can make myself the most annoying thing in this room."

"You're already that," said Wick, with the reflexive loyalty of a younger sibling, and Kessa almost smiled.

"Cael holds till the wall drops," Oscar said. "Wick blinds it. Kessa baits it onto the shelf. And I bring the floor down. Everybody clear?"

"No," said Cael, straining, golden, magnificent. "But let's do it anyway."

"That's the spirit. *Now.*"

The dome fell, and the world came apart in the right order.

Wick let go. Not the wild uncontrolled thing that had nearly killed them all once—aimed fire, the kind she could hold, but there was a great deal even in that now, and she'd learned at last to spend it like a blade instead of a flood. A lance of white fire took the hush full across the ruined face, and it shrieked again and reared back, fans flailing, and that was the flinch, that was the room they needed.

Kessa ran straight at it.

She ran past it, under it, around it, a small silent ferocious thing in dead men's boots, and she did the bravest stupidest necessary thing in the chamber: she made herself loud. A bolt off its carapace, a shout, a thrown pick clanging across the old shelf—noise, noise, noise, drawing the maddened deaf bulk of it stumbling after her onto the rotten floor, over the cracks, toward the lip of the black, while the stone groaned under its weight.

"It's on!" she screamed, and dove.

"*Wick!*" Oscar grabbed her shoulder, pointed at the two failure points his gift had lit, the overburdened spine. "There—and there—everything you have left, both of them, right now—and Wick—" he caught her face for half a second, made her look at him "—only what you can hold. *Only what you can hold.*"

But there wasn't enough in the safe part. He saw it in her eyes the instant she reached—saw her scrape the bottom of what she could hold and find it not enough, the shelf too big, the stone too stubborn, the monster already finding its feet on the failing floor. What was safe wouldn't do it.

And he watched her decide.

"*No—*" he started, but she was already reaching past it, down toward whatever it was she'd spent every fight refusing to touch, and the mark at her throat flared from a glow to a blaze, and the fire that came out of her then was not warm gold but a white past white, a heat with a *pressure* to it, a thing that did not feel like it was coming out of a sixteen-year-old at all. It hit both failure points at once. The overburdened spine of rock simply ceased to exist.

The whole shelf went.

It dropped into the black with a roar that swallowed even the hush's scream—a hundred tons of old stone and one enormous armored monstrosity folding into the pit, the carapace of the swallowed dead going down at last into a dark deep enough to keep it. The hush fell silent the way it had lived, and was gone.

And Wick was still pouring.

That was the thing Oscar would not forget, the thing that turned the win to ice in his chest: the floor was down, the monster was dead, and the fire would not stop coming out of her. She stood at the very lip of the new gulf with that white blaze roaring out of her and her face gone slack and strange and *frightened* in a way that had nothing to do with the pit, and she was leaning toward it, leaning *out,* as if something down in the dark she'd just made had reached up and taken hold of her, as if it would not let go, and her boots were already sliding on the broken edge.

Oscar did not think. He lunged.

He got an arm around her as the last of the broken shelf gave under her feet, and then they were both going, both sliding toward the black, and for one heartbeat at the edge of the pit he was close enough to whatever had hold of her to *feel* it—not see it, feel it, a vastness, a heat, an ancient terrible attention rising up toward the light through her—through the girl herself, as if she were the one thin place left in a sealed world—glad, so glad, reaching—and it was nothing he had words for, nothing in any grammar he knew, and it terrified him more than the monster had.

"*Wick!*" He didn't reason with it. He couldn't. He just grabbed the only thing he understood, which was her. "Wick, it's me, come *back*—let it go, you can let it go, come back to me—come *back*—"

A hand closed on his ankle like an iron trap. Kessa. Flat on the stone, anchored to a jut of rock, hauling with everything in her wiry frame. And then a wall of warmth slammed down at the pit's edge—Cael, on his knees, gray as ash, planting the hearthward one last time with the dregs of himself so the crumbling lip stopped crumbling, so there was something solid to be pulled back over.

And then, all at once, it stopped.

Oscar felt it happen the way you feel a window slam in another room—a pressure gone, a cold absence where a moment ago there'd been that awful glad reaching—and the white fire cut off, and she sagged into him, and the four of them dragged each other back from the edge of the black in a heap of shaking limbs and ended up in a pile on the solid stone, alive, all of them, every one.

For a while nobody said anything. Below them, very far down, something enormous finished falling.

"So," Kessa said finally, to the ceiling, breathing hard. "The plan was good."

"The plan was *excellent,*" Oscar said. His voice came out wrong. He had an arm still locked around Wick and he wasn't ready to take it away. "I'd like that noted."

Wick laughed—a cracked, wet, half-hysterical sound—and didn't explain herself, and Oscar didn't ask, and Cael lay flat on his back with his ruined shield warm against his chest and said, to no one, "We pulled her back." Just that. Like he was checking it was true. "This time we pulled her back."

Nobody had to ask what other time he meant. They let it sit there, the dead man's name nobody said, and for once it didn't poison anything. It just was. They'd lost one once. They hadn't lost one today. That was the whole of it.

The haul was everything Brida had said, and the cost of reaching it was written all around them in the bones, and Oscar made himself walk to it slowly, with respect, because a place like this had earned at least that.

It was a fortune. Not in coin—in *workings,* the live old-world figures the salvagers broke their bodies to find, crated and packed and waiting: lamps and locks and the small grey cold-boxes, and stranger things, intricate enough that Oscar wanted to sit down with each one for a week. The Tithe's richest season, ready to move south. Enough, sold quietly, to arm a rebellion. Enough to change the shape of the fight.

And burned into every crate, the same seal.

He'd been bracing for it and it still landed like a stone. A southern house's mark, clerkly and bloodless—and beneath it, small and exact, the closed loop. The knot without ends. The mark on his sword. The mark on the writ that wanted him in a box.

"One hand," he said quietly. Kessa came to stand beside him, and he tapped the brand. "The relics that go south. The bounty that wants me. Same seal. Same house. They're not two problems. They were never two problems." He almost laughed, and it came out hollow. "Somebody down south is pulling the magic out of the ground and—people like me out of the dark, with the same pen, and I still don't know why, and I am getting very tired of not knowing why."

"Then we go find out," Kessa said. "After we don't die here. One impossible thing at a time."

It was Cael who found the book, propped on its own little shelf apart from the crates, set down with a care nothing else in that grasping place had been shown. He brought it to Oscar without a word, because of course it was for Oscar; everyone knew whose it was the moment they saw it.

It was a grimoire.

Oscar's hands were not entirely steady when he took it. Old—old as anything down here—and *whole,* the binding intact, the pages supple, a thing that should have crumbled to dust an age ago and hadn't, because the workings woven through it had kept it the way the old world kept its treasures. He opened it, and the cold legible shine came up behind his eyes, and the figures on the page resolved—and they were *figures he didn't know.* Workings beyond his repertoire, beyond what he'd woken with, a whole craft laid out in a patient hand: techniques, refinements, things he could *learn,* things that could make him more than the half-remembered scraps he'd clawed back since the basin. A door, on paper. A way forward.

And then he turned a page, and his breath stopped.

Because he knew the hand.

He didn't know how he knew it. He had never seen it before. But the way the figures were drawn—the particular slope of them, the small private flourishes, the choices a writer makes that are as personal as a face—was *familiar,* familiar in his fingers, familiar in the part of him that reached for a working without being told how. It was a hand he could have written. It was, he thought, with a cold that had nothing to do with the deep current, a hand he *had* written, somewhere, somewhen, in a life that might not be his—and the not-knowing opened under him like the pit had opened under the hush, black and bottomless and waiting.

He closed the book. He held it for a moment against his chest the way Cael held the shield, and he did not explain, and no one made him.

"Keeper?" Wick asked quietly. She'd come up beside him, pale and wrung-out and watching him with those too-sharp eyes.

"Keeper," he agreed, and made his voice work. "Not for selling. Some things you don't sell." He managed something that was nearly a smile. "Besides, none of you can read it, and I'd hate for it to go to waste."

Wick almost smiled back. Then she drifted, the way she did, toward the smallest crate, the one shoved in a corner, half-forgotten—and she crouched, and came up holding something Oscar almost told her to put down.

It was nothing. A small dull thing, a disc or a token, grey and unmarked and plainly worthless, the kind of object you'd find a hundred of in any salvage heap and not give a copper for. He read it without thinking, out of habit—and there was nothing to read. No figure. No working. No light. Dead salvage, inert, a pebble.

But Wick was holding it the way you hold something warm, turning it over and over, and she'd gone very still and very far away.

"Wick," Oscar said. "It's junk. There's not a spark of work in it. Put it back, we've a fortune to carry."

"Yeah," Wick said. She didn't put it back. She closed her hand around it, and slid it into her pocket, and looked up at him with an expression he couldn't read at all. "Yeah. Junk." And something in the way she said it made the hair stand up on his arms, and he filed it away with all the other things he didn't understand yet, on a list that was getting longer than he liked.

They loaded what they could carry. They left the rest. And as they turned for the long road up, Oscar stopped at the lip of the black, where a monster lay dead at the bottom of a pit, and felt the whole shape of it click into place.

"They told this town the deep belonged to them," he said. "That only the Tithe could work the good claim, that the dark was theirs by right, because nobody else could survive it." He looked at his crew—gray, bloodied, emptied, standing in the impossible heart of the place that wasn't supposed to let anyone out. "And the lie isn't the danger. The danger was real—it nearly killed us four times over." He looked down into the pit, at the dead thing folded in it. "The lie is that it was *forever.* They've told this valley the toll the good claim takes is the price of the deep itself, fixed as the cold, nobody's fault—and that only the Tithe is brave enough to pay it. But it had a cause. The cause could die. And in a hundred years the Tithe never once tried to kill it, because a monster down here guarded their richest claim for nothing and kept the whole town too afraid and too deep in debt to ask why." His voice went quiet and hard. "They didn't fight that thing. They *fed* it. Generation after generation, fed to a horror they could have ended any time they chose—because a frightened valley in debt was worth more to them than the people they buried for it." The plan arrived in him fully formed, lit end to end. "Their gold isn't their power. Their cord isn't their power. The *lie* is—and we just killed the proof of it. We show the town the body. We show them the thing they were promised could never die, dead at the bottom of a pit. And we show them whose seal is on these crates, so they finally know where their blood's been going all along."

Kessa nodded slowly, and for the second time since he'd known her, something dangerously like hope moved in her face. "That'd bring the whole thing down."

"That," Cael said, gathering a fallen salvager's name-tags from the grit with hands that still shook, "would be a hard thing worth doing." He closed his fist around the dead. "Brida's man came down here for a debt and a lie. Let's carry both back up into the light."

So they climbed—lighter in spirit, heavier in pack, a crew that had gone into the grave the good claim kept and come out whole and pointed, every one of them, in a single direction.

It was Wick who put the cold back in the day, because it was always Wick, and because she was right.

"They'll know," she said, plainly, as they climbed toward the distant light. She didn't look back at the dark they'd robbed. "When their crew comes down for the haul and finds the hush dead and the best of it gone. They'll know somebody got in where nobody gets in. And there's only one somebody in this whole town worth that kind of trouble." She glanced at Oscar, sixteen and unflinching. "We just killed the thing that kept the Tithe's secret. And took its money. They're going to come for us with everything they've got."

"Yes," Oscar agreed. He found he was almost smiling, in the grim way you smile at the top of a hill you've decided to hold. "We did rather poke the dragon, didn't we."

And far above them, up in the light of a town that ran on tolls and fear, the Tithe had not yet learned what it had lost—but the counting had already begun.

## Chapter Sixteen — The Squeeze

They came up out of the good claim like kings and walked into a town holding its breath.

Oscar felt it the moment they surfaced—a wrongness in the air of Marrow, a quiet where there shouldn't have been one. It was past midday; the salvage market should have been a roar of haggling and barrows and the clang of the scale-houses. Instead the lanes were half-empty, and the people who were out moved fast and looked at nothing, and the red cord stood in clusters at the corners where before there'd been one bored tollman with a ledger. Something had happened, or was about to. A town learned to go quiet around trouble the way the good claim had gone quiet around the hush, and for the same reason.

"They don't know yet," Kessa murmured, reading the street the way she read everything. "If they'd found the claim cracked, there'd be ten times this cord, and it'd be hunting, not standing. This is something else. This is them sitting on the lid of something." Her eyes moved. "We've got a little time. Not much."

"Then we spend it well." Oscar shifted the weight of the pack on his shoulders—a fortune in old-world workings, and under it, wrapped close, the grimoire he hadn't been able to stop thinking about, and the branded board with the seal that was going to end all of this if he played it right. "We go to Brida. We get her clear of the debt with the haul, we get the truth in front of the right ears, and we move before the lid comes off." He glanced at Wick. "All of us. Slowly. Looking like nothing."

Wick wasn't looking at the cord, or the lanes, or him. She'd been somewhere else since the pit—quieter than he'd ever known her, her hand drifting now and then to her pocket where the worthless grey token sat, her eyes going to middle distances that weren't in the room. She came back when he said her name, but slow, like surfacing.

"Yeah," she said. "Nothing. I'm good at nothing."

She wasn't, and they both knew it, and Oscar let it go because there wasn't time and because he didn't have words yet for what he'd felt at the edge of that pit—the vast glad reaching thing that had hold of her, that he'd touched for half a second and would be a long time forgetting. He didn't know what it was. He knew it had wanted her, and that she'd come back, and that something in her hadn't entirely come back with her. He filed it with the rest. The list was getting very long.

Brida's boarding house was dark in the middle of the day, the shutters drawn.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was the cord on the corner watching it, two of them, not bothering to hide it. The third was Brida herself, when she cracked the door at Cael's careful knock—gray-faced, her bad arm held tight to her side, her eyes going past them to the watchers on the corner before she pulled them in.

"You shouldn't have come back," she said, but she was already bolting the door behind them, already moving to the back room, the safe one. "You shouldn't be in this town at all. They've been here. Twice. Asking after the silver-haired one and his crew." She rounded on Oscar, and there was fear in her, but under it something harder. "What did you do? The whole town can feel it. The cord's wound tight as I've ever seen them, and they won't say why, and that means it's big, and you walk back in here grinning under a pack fit to break your back. What did you *do?*"

"We took the good claim," Oscar said.

Brida went still.

"We went down the back way," he said, gently, because the next part was the part that mattered. "We killed the thing that guards it. We walked out with the season's haul." He swung the pack down, careful, and worked the branded board free, and laid it on her scarred table where the lamplight could find the seal. "And we found this. On every crate. Brida—the good claim isn't theirs by any right. There's a monster down there, a real one; it near killed the four of us getting past it. But the Tithe told this valley it couldn't be killed—that the deaths were the price of the deep, fixed as the cold, and only the Tithe brave enough to pay it for you. *That's* the lie. Not the danger; the danger was always real. The lie is that it was *forever.*" He kept his voice gentle, because of what came next. "That thing could die. We killed it in a single day. And in a hundred years the Tithe never once tried—because a horror guarding their richest claim for free was worth more to them than every soul it ate. Your husband didn't die because the deep is unbeatable, Brida. He died because a living monster made the Tithe rich, and they were never going to give that up."

He watched it land on her. He watched four winters of debt and grief and a hole in the ground that ate her man rearrange themselves into a single white-hot line, and he thought, not for the first time, that the cruelest thing the powerful ever did was make their cruelty look like the weather—like a thing that simply happened, that no one chose, that you could only endure. Show a person the choosing, and you gave them somewhere to put the rage.

"You're saying," Brida said slowly, "it was never the claim. It was them. All of it. The debt, the terms, the—" her voice cracked and she mastered it "—the going down at all. Them."

"Them," Oscar agreed. "And here's the thing about a lie that's holding up an entire town: you don't have to fight it. You only have to show people it's a lie, all at once, where they can all see. The thing that killed your husband is dead at the bottom of a pit—the thing they swore could never die. The proof's in that pack. And there's a southern seal on the Tithe's own crates that says they've been selling this valley's blood to strangers in the south for years." He let that sit. "We can take them apart, Brida. Not with knives. With the truth, said loud enough, in front of enough people that the cord can't make them all forget."

For a moment hope and terror fought it out on her tired face. Then the door came apart.

Not their door—the front, the bolted one, kicked in off its hinges with a single boot, and the dark little boarding house filled with red cord and the bright cold midday behind them, and at the center of it, stepping over the wreck of the door with a ledger still in one hand because of course it was, came Sarn.

The tallyman looked, if anything, mildly disappointed. "You made me come into the dark to find you," they said, to Oscar, in that same bored accountant's voice, the scar pulling one corner of their mouth. "After all the trouble I went to giving you somewhere bright to walk into. That's poor manners." Their eyes flicked to the pack, the table, the branded board lying in the lamplight, and something moved behind them—the sums, running. "Ah. So it *was* you, in the claim. We weren't sure. The crew that went down this morning are still screaming about it, frankly. A dead hush. The season's haul, gone." The bored eyes came back to Oscar, and for the first time there was something almost like respect in them, which was worse than contempt. "Do you have the faintest idea what you've done? Not to me. To this town. You've made me make an *example.*"

"Let them go," Oscar said. "All of them. The crew, the woman—this is between the Tithe and me. I'm the one your writ wants. You've matched the loop, you know what I'm worth alive. So take me and let the rest walk."

"*Oscar*—" Kessa started, low and furious.

"No," said Sarn, almost kindly, and that was the thing that turned Oscar's blood to water—the *almost kindly*—because Sarn wasn't a brute, Sarn was a mind, and a mind had already worked out the move that hurt most for the least. "You misunderstand the arithmetic. I don't want you handed to me in a back room by a crew of killers I'd lose half my people taking. I want you to walk out into the square tomorrow at noon, in front of this whole town, and give yourself up to the cord like a debt being paid—so that every soul in Marrow watches the marked man kneel, and learns again, in their bones, what happens to anyone who thinks the deep belongs to them." They smiled, the scar twisting. "And to make sure you come, I'll be holding the boarding-house woman against your good behavior. She fed strays. There's a price for that. You can pay it, or she can." The ledger snapped shut. "Noon. The square. Come alone, come kneeling, and she lives. Bring your killers, or don't come, and the town watches her pay your bill instead." Sarn turned for the ruined door. "Either way, I get my example. You only get to choose whose face it wears."

They took Brida. She didn't scream—she looked back once, at Oscar, and there was no blame in it, which was somehow the worst thing in the whole rotten day—and then the cord were gone, and the door was a wreck of light on the floor, and the four of them stood in the wrecked dark of a kind woman's house with a fortune they couldn't spend and a deadline they couldn't meet.

For a while nobody said anything.

"I'm going to walk into that square tomorrow," Oscar said.

"Like hell," said Wick, surfacing all the way for the first time since the pit, fury doing what fear couldn't.

"Listen to me—"

"No. *You* listen." She was shaking, and it wasn't the door this time, it was just her, sixteen and furious and right. "You always do this. You see the clever sad true thing and you decide it's yours to carry alone, and you'd kneel in that square and let them take you south in a box because the arithmetic *works,* because it's the answer with the smallest number of dead in it, and you'd be wrong, because the number you keep leaving out of the sum is *you.*" Her voice broke and she shoved through it. "You don't get to spend yourself like it's nothing. Not anymore. We pulled you back out of that font and we pulled me back out of that pit and we are *not* a crew that lets the dark have its people. Cael said it. You heard him say it."

"We pulled her back," Cael agreed, very quietly, from where he stood with the warm shield still on his arm. "That's the kind of crew we decided to be. I'd not like to unmake that decision the day after we made it." He looked at Oscar with that steady terrible gentleness. "You gave yourself once, down in the heart of the dead city, and you chose not to. Don't undo it in a square in Marrow for a cleaner sum. There's another way. There's always another way; you taught me that, arguing with a font."

And Oscar—who had been so ready, who had felt the old pull to solve the equation by removing himself from it, the made thing's certainty that he was the most spendable coin in any purse—felt something in him that wasn't despair. He looked at the branded seal on Brida's table. He looked at the pack full of proof. He looked at the deadline, noon in the square, the whole town summoned to watch a man kneel.

The whole town. Summoned. To one place. To watch.

"Sarn's made a mistake," he said slowly, and the others heard the change in his voice and went still. "A clever mistake, which is the most dangerous kind, because clever people trust them. They want a stage. They want every soul in Marrow in that square at noon, watching, learning their lesson." He started, despite everything, to smile—the real one, the dangerous one, the one that meant the cleverest thing in the room had finally found the floor under the problem. "They've gone to a great deal of trouble to gather us an audience."

Kessa's head came up. "You're not going to kneel."

"I'm going to walk into that square at noon," Oscar said. "All of us are. With the haul, and the seal, and every word of the truth, and we are going to turn their example inside out in front of the entire town. Sarn wants Marrow to watch the marked man learn his place." The smile sharpened. "Let's let Marrow watch instead while four strangers prove the Tithe has been lying to them their whole lives—and dare the cord to silence the truth with the whole valley looking on."

"That," said Cael, after a moment, with the air of a man committing a sin he intended to enjoy, "is a hard thing very much worth doing."

"It's insane," Kessa said. "We'll be outnumbered fifty to one in the open, with Brida in their hands and a price on his head." A beat. The ghost of that dangerous hope again. "What do you need from me?"

Wick said nothing. But she'd stopped shaking, and her hand had come out of her pocket, and the look on her face was no longer somewhere far away—it was right here, fixed on Oscar, fierce and frightened and entirely present, the look of a girl who had decided, again, who her people were.

Outside, the cord watched the broken door, and the town held its breath, and somewhere across Marrow a square was being readied for an example.

It was going to get one. Just not the one anybody had ordered.

## Chapter Seventeen — The Example

By noon the square was full, because the Tithe had made sure of it.

They'd sent the cord through Marrow at first light, lane by lane, and the message had been simple: be in the square at noon, all of you, or be marked absent, and in a town where everything you ate came on Tithe credit, *marked absent* was a sentence. So they came—salvagers and scale-clerks and children and the old, herded into the open square between the great stone ribs, hundreds of them, quiet the way the good claim was quiet, the quiet of people who have learned that noise gets noticed.

At the head of the square, on the steps of the largest scale-house, the Tithe had built its stage. Brida stood on it between two of the cord, her bad arm tight to her side, her chin up. And before her, ledger in hand, waiting with the patience of someone who has already counted the outcome and found it good, stood Sarn.

Oscar walked into the square at noon, and he did not come alone, and he did not come kneeling.

He came down the central lane with Kessa on one side and Cael on the other and Wick a half-step behind, with the pack of the season's haul on his back and the branded seal in his hand, and he watched the calculation stutter behind Sarn's scarred face for the first time since he'd met them—a sum that had not accounted for this. The cord shifted, hands going to weapons. The crowd drew a single breath. Brida's eyes found Oscar's across the square, and he saw her understand that he had not come to die for her, and he watched something in her stand up straight.

"That's far enough," Sarn said. Their voice carried—a square has good acoustics, which Oscar was counting on. "This was meant to be done with dignity. You were meant to come alone. Now I'll have to do it the ugly way, in front of the children."

"No," Oscar said—and he didn't say it to Sarn. He turned, and he pitched his voice to the stone ribs and the hundreds of frightened people beneath them, and he spoke to Marrow. "I came alone, the way you asked. Look—" he spread his empty hand "—no army. Four of us. The same four who, two days ago, walked down into the good claim, the deep one, the one the Tithe keeps for itself, the one that's been eating this valley's people since before most of you were born."

A ripple went through the crowd. The good claim. The name alone was a wound in this town; every family had paid into it.

"Quiet him," Sarn said to the cord, unhurried.

"Your husbands went down there," Oscar said, faster now, louder, to the crowd. "Your daughters. They went because they owed, and the only way to clear what they owed was the good claim, and they did not come back, and the Tithe told you why. They told you the deep is simply deadly. That the good claim takes its toll the way winter takes the old—a sad thing, a natural thing, nobody's fault, the price of living on the bones of the old world." He let the contempt show now. "And they told you that only the Tithe is brave enough to broker that bargain on your behalf. That's the story. You've all heard it. You've all *paid* it."

Two of the cord were pushing through the crowd toward him now. He had maybe a minute.

"It's a lie." He said it flat and certain and it cracked across the square. "Not the danger—the danger is real. There was a monster down there, an old and terrible thing, and it nearly killed the four of us. But it was a *thing.* It had a body. It could bleed. It could *die.*" He turned in place, meeting eyes, the whole square. "And we killed it. Two days ago. In a single afternoon. The thing the Tithe swore could never be beaten is dead at the bottom of a pit, and the proof is that their own crew went down for the season's haul yesterday morning and found it gone—ask them, they're in this square, I can see the cord that came up screaming—"

"*Quiet him,*" Sarn snapped, and the bored mask was gone.

"—and ask yourselves," Oscar roared over the cord shoving toward him, "why a monster the Tithe could have killed any time in a hundred years was allowed to go on eating your families *generation after generation!*" He had them now; he could feel it, the awful held breath of a crowd at the edge of understanding. "Because a monster guarding the richest claim in the valley was the best thing that ever happened to them! It kept the claim theirs. It kept you afraid. It kept you in debt, sending your own down into a grave the Tithe never once tried to close—because your fear was worth more to them than every soul that thing swallowed. They didn't fight the monster. *They fed it. They fed it you.*"

The square erupted—not into action, not yet, but into sound, the dam of a hundred swallowed griefs breaking at once, and the cord faltered in the press of bodies that suddenly would not politely move aside.

"And here is who they fed you *for.*" Oscar held the branded board high, over his head, turning so the carved seal caught the noon light. "This is burned into every crate of the haul we pulled out of the good claim. A southern house's mark. The relics you die to dig up don't make Marrow rich—they go *south,* to buyers you'll never see, on the Tithe's own seal. You have been bleeding into the deep for a hundred years to make strangers in the south wealthy, and the red cord is nothing but their hired hand, and they have *lied to you about all of it!*"

For one heartbeat the whole square hung silent.

Then Sarn made the mistake that ended them, because even a clever mind panics when the arithmetic turns, and a panicking clever mind reaches for the oldest answer there is.

"Kill the woman," Sarn said to the two on the stage. "Now. Where they can see. Remind them what the cord *is.*"

It was meant to restore the fear. It did the opposite. It showed a square full of grieving people exactly what Oscar had just told them was true, made flesh, in the same breath—the Tithe killing a debtor to keep the rest in line—and the held breath of the crowd became a roar, and the roar became a surge, and Marrow stopped being a crowd and became a tide.

After that it happened very fast, and the crew's whole job was to keep the spark alive long enough for the fire to catch.

The cord on the stage went for Brida. Kessa was already moving—she'd been drifting that way the whole time in her silent stolen boots, unnoticed at the stage's edge, and she came up the steps low and fast and put her shoulder into the first one and her knife into the gap in the second's guard, and then she had Brida by the good arm and was hauling her off the back of the stage, swearing steadily, alive.

The cord in the crowd drew steel and the crowd drew back from the steel, and for one ugly moment the old fear tried to reassert itself—and that was when Cael drove the hearthward into the cobbles at the front of the press and poured himself into it, and the dome of warm gold bloomed up between the cord and the people, a wall of warding ten feet wide, and the blades that came down on it skidded off light and the front rank of terrified salvagers found themselves suddenly, impossibly, *protected.* Cael stood inside his own warmth with his arms spread and his voice carrying like a bell over the square: "*They cannot touch you! Look! Their cord cannot touch you—the fear was the only wall they ever had, and it's broken—STAND UP!*"

And Marrow stood up.

It was not clean and it was not bloodless and Oscar would carry parts of it a long time. But there were hundreds of them and dozens of cord, and the only thing that had ever made that math work was a fear that was coming apart in real time, and once the salvagers understood—once the dockworkers and the scale-clerks and the widows of the good claim understood that the cord bled, that the cord could be *grabbed,* that the people beside them were grabbing—the red cord drowned in the tide they'd spent a generation standing on.

Oscar fought to keep the center, and found the grimoire had been worth carrying. He'd had two days and one quiet night with it, and he'd learned exactly one new figure well enough to trust—a binding, a thing that seized and held rather than struck—and when three cord came at him together he scored it fast and clean and the air around their feet simply *gripped,* stone flowing up over boots like cold tar, holding them fast and harmless while the crowd took their blades. It cost him; new figures always cost more than old ones, and his reserve went thin and cold. But it held. It *worked.* He filed the small fierce joy of it away for later, when there was a later.

And Wick—Wick was a wonder. She put herself at Cael's flank and she did not flood and she did not reach for anything she shouldn't, and Oscar understood, watching her work, that the pit had taught her something it had nearly killed her to learn: she fought the whole battle on the controlled part of herself, aimed and precise, a lash of fire to disarm, a wall of heat to drive the cord back from a knot of children, never once near the thing that waited under her—and she'd never been more dangerous, or more in command of it, in her life. The holding was her strength. She'd finally believed it.

Sarn did not die in the square.

Oscar found them at the edge of it, at the mouth of a side-lane, in the last minutes when the thing was clearly lost—the cord broken or fled or pinned under furious salvagers, the stage empty, Brida safe in Cael's warded light. Sarn was not fighting. Sarn was *leaving,* unhurried even now, ledger tucked away, and they stopped when they saw Oscar, and the two of them stood for a moment in the noise of a town tearing free.

"You've won a town," Sarn said. No bored mask now; just a cold clear assessment, almost respectful. "Congratulations. It's a small thing, and it cost more than you know, and you have no idea what you've actually done." They studied him—the silver hair, the unlined face, the loop on the sword. "I matched you to a standing writ from a southern house. Do you understand what that means? I was going to make a quiet sum off you. But the house that issued that writ does not lose its property to a riot in a salvage town and shrug. They'll want to know who. And I am going to go and tell them—exactly who, exactly where, exactly what you can do." The scar twisted. "You didn't just break my cartel, marked man. You introduced yourself to the people who own it. They've been looking for you for a very long time. Now they know which hole to look in."

"Then I'll be gone from it," Oscar said, and kept his voice level, though something cold had opened under his ribs.

"Will you," Sarn said. "Run south, then, where the answers are and the house is. Or run north, into the ice, where there's nothing." A shrug, almost kind. "Either way you're running toward them or away from everything. That's the writ. That's what it does. I almost feel for you." They stepped back into the dark of the lane. "I'd wish you luck, but you've cost me a fortune. I'll settle for watching what finds you."

And then Sarn was gone, into the under-town, the way the cord knew and the crew didn't, and Oscar let them go—because chasing one tallyman through the bones while a town burned its chains was a fool's errand, and because some cold honest part of him knew Sarn was right, and right was worse than any blade.

When he came back into the square, it was over, and it was beginning.

The Tithe's hold on Marrow was broken—not tidily, not forever, but in the way that mattered: in front of everyone, all at once, past unforgetting. The cord were down or fled. The stage was a wreck. And the people of Marrow stood in the wreckage of their own fear looking faintly amazed at their own hands, the way people do when they discover the thing they were terrified of was always, in the end, outnumbered.

Brida found him. Her bad arm was worse for the day and her face was wet and she gripped his sleeve with her good hand, hard.

"My husband," she said. "You said it out loud. In front of everyone. That it was them. That it was never—never the weather." Her voice cracked and held. "Four winters I've carried it like it was a thing that just *happened.* And you stood in that square and gave it a name." She wasn't quite crying. "I don't know what you are. The cord said things. I don't care. You gave this town its rage back, and a town with its rage back is a town that can't be owned. Thank you."

"Thank Cael," Oscar said, because it was true and because he couldn't carry her gratitude just then, not with Sarn's voice still cold in his ears. "He's the one who stood between."

Cael, hearing it, looked up from where he knelt among the freed and the hurt, the warm shield quiet on his arm, and for the first time since Oscar had known him there was no guilt in his face at all—just a bone-deep, weary peace, the look of a man who had finally, finally been allowed to be what he was for. *The one who stands between.* He'd held the door, and this time the whole town had walked through it, and lived.

They gathered on the scale-house steps as the sun went long, the four of them, with a freed town moving around them and a fortune in old-world workings to put into the hands of the people it had been stolen from. Wick sat close against Oscar's side, worn to nothing, her hand in her pocket on the worthless grey token, her eyes far away again now that the fighting was done. Kessa stood watch out of habit, though there was nothing left to watch for. And Oscar looked south, down the long valley, the way the relics went and the writ came from and Sarn had promised the reckoning would follow.

"He's right, isn't he," Kessa said quietly, beside him. Not a question. She'd heard enough.

"Yes," Oscar said. "We won a town and woke a giant. The people who want me know where I am now, and what I can do, and they don't lose things gracefully." He was quiet a moment, and far off at the edge of his attention, fainter than a heartbeat and nearer than it had been in days, something tugged—a bond stretched thin across a great distance, an absence shaped like a familiar that knew him, pulling, almost in reach. He breathed past it. Not yet. Soon, but not yet. "So we don't wait for them to come north. We go south. We find the house behind the seal, and the answer to the writ, and whatever it is I am that's worth a fortune to bring home unbroken." He looked at his crew—the tracker, the protector, the wild girl, all of them watching him in the long gold light. "It's going to be dangerous, and it isn't your fight, and I'd understand—"

"Finish that sentence," Kessa said, "and I'll push you off these steps."

"We're a crew," Wick said simply, from his side, not opening her eyes. "Crews don't let the dark have their people. You said."

Cael only smiled, and turned the warm shield in his hands, and said nothing, because some things didn't need saying twice.

So they sat on the steps of a freed town while the light went down the valley to the south, where the answers were, and the danger, and the thing Oscar had spent his whole short borrowed life refusing to learn about himself—and for the first time since he'd woken in a stone basin at the bottom of the world, he found he was ready to go and find out.
