## Chapter Seven — The Heart of the City

The road did not end at a door this time. It ended at an edge.

The gallery opened without warning into a void, and the four of them stopped at the lip of it, and even Kessa, who did not waste breath, let one out. They stood at the rim of a vast round shaft sunk through the floor of the dead city, so wide a thrown stone would tire before it found the far wall, so deep the bottom of it was a guess. And it was not dark. After so long in the cold silver and the frost, the light at the city's heart hit them like a struck note: a slow, pale, living radiance that rose up out of the shaft and turned slowly in the air, and it took Oscar a moment to understand what he was looking at, and when he did the understanding sat him down inside himself.

The whole city's script was running *here.* Every clause, every wall, every frost-fine line of the great working they had walked through for hours—all of it poured inward and down along the shaft's curved sides in rivers of moving light, spiraling, gathering, feeding into something at the heart that he could not yet see and already did not want to. The dead city was a sentence. This was the word it had been built to say.

A stair wound down the inside of the shaft, hugging the wall, and they took it because there was nowhere else to go, and as they descended the light grew, and the air grew warm—warm, the first true warmth that did not come from a fire since Oscar had clawed up out of his cradle—and the warmth was somehow worse than the cold had been, because it was *wrong* down here, an exhale where everything else was held breath.

The heart of the city was a font.

That was the only word Oscar had for it, and it was not enough, but nothing would have been. At the bottom of the shaft, where all the rivers of script gathered, there was a great basin of pale stone, and it was full—not of water, but of the working itself, the deep current made visible and brought to a boil, a slow luminous something that was not liquid and not light and turned over on itself with the unhurried patience of a thing that had been waiting a very long time. The cold place in the air where Oscar reached to do magic, the deep current he had spent his whole life arguing with—it was *here,* pooled and naked and close enough to touch, more of it in this one basin than he had felt in all his three hundred years put together.

And it was reaching for him.

He felt it the moment they came down off the last of the stair onto the floor of the heart. The light in the basin *leaned,* the way a plant leans at a window, the slow current of it turning toward him and away from the other three, and the pull came up through the soles of his feet and into the marrow of him, gentle and total and dreadful: a sense of homecoming so strong it made his eyes sting, a feeling that he had been cold and incomplete and only half-real his whole short life and that the warm completing thing was *right here,* and all he had to do was step into it and be finished.

*Be set,* said the city.

The voice came out of the walls again, here as in the tower, the great gathered script flaring as it spoke—but down here it was not failing, not stuttering, not a dying mechanism repeating itself. Down here it was strong, and close, and almost kind.

*"The master has fallen. The line endures. You have come to the heart. Be set, and be whole, and inherit what is yours."*

"Don't," Kessa said. She had her crossbow up, not at anything, at everything, her eyes moving over the basin and the leaning light and Oscar's face, which had gone slack with longing in a way he could not feel and would have hated. "Whatever it's telling you. Don't."

"It's not telling me anything." His voice came out strange. "It's *offering.*" And it was. That was the horror under the homecoming: the font was not commanding him, not dragging him. It was holding the door open and letting him feel, in his bones, exactly how much he wanted to walk through. He had spent a day discovering all the ways he was diminished—the candle where the ocean had been, the wear missing from his face, the empty place on his shoulder—and here was the thing that would mend all of it at once. He could feel that it would. The font was not lying. That was what made it terrible.

"It's a forge," he said, and made himself say it, dragging the scholar up over the longing the way you drag a cold cloth over a fever. "Look at the architecture of it. The whole city pours its working into this basin and the basin—" he swallowed "—the basin *makes* things. Or finishes them. Completes a thing that came out unfinished." He thought of his too-new hands. He thought of the salvager edited on the vault floor. He thought, and could not stop thinking, of a frost-shedding figure in a lonely cradle, reaching, coming apart for want of exactly this. "It's where they were meant to be set. The ones from the cradles. The ones like—"

He did not finish. He was getting better at not finishing. But Kessa finished it for him, very quietly, not unkindly, the way you say a true thing to someone who has nearly arrived at it:

"The ones like you."

The word sat in the warm air and did not echo.

Wick had drifted toward the basin's rim. Of course she had; she was sixteen and the light was beautiful and the warmth was the first kind thing this city had offered. "It's not scary," she said, soft, reaching out a hand toward the slow luminous something the way she'd reached for the font in the—the way she reached for any bright thing. The mark at her throat had begun, faintly, to glow in answer to it. "It feels like—Cael, it feels *good,* it feels like—"

"Wick." Cael had her wrist before Oscar could move, drawing her back, gentle and immovable, and his other hand was at the iron token at his throat, and his plain face had set into something Oscar had not seen on it before—not fear, but a deep devotional unease, the look of a person of faith standing in front of a holy thing that belongs to the wrong god. "Don't touch it. I don't care how good it feels. *Especially* if it feels good." He looked at the basin the way he had looked at the edited body, and named it the way his kind named things: "This is a miracle. I just don't think it's a kind one. The kind ones don't have to talk you into them."

Three of them, three readings, and Oscar standing in the middle of them with the pull in his marrow: Kessa saw a curse, and Cael saw a wrongful miracle, and Wick saw the first warm beautiful thing in a city of cold, and not one of them was wrong, and none of them could feel what he felt, which was the basin's slow certain promise that it could make him *whole.*

And he wanted it. That was the thing he would not be able to explain to them afterward, if there was an afterward: that some large and exhausted part of him, the part that had reached for a familiar shoulder and a familiar current and found both gone, wanted nothing in the world so much as to climb into that warm pale light and stop being a candle and stop being unfinished and stop being whatever half-made thing the water by the fire had shown him. The longing was not a trick worked on him from outside. It came from the truest part of him. That was why it was dangerous.

He realized his hands were cold.

He looked down. The warmth of the heart was on every other surface of him, but his hands—the new, smooth, wrong hands—had gone white at the fingertips, and a fine pale rime was creeping up from the nails, frost, the same soft frost that furred the dead and had eaten the figure in the cradle, growing on him now the longer he stood at the edge of the thing that wanted to finish him. As if some part of him knew that to be unfinished was to be coming apart, slowly, all along, and that the nearer he stood to the cure the more honestly his body admitted the disease.

He closed his fists over it before the others saw. He was getting practiced at hiding what his body kept trying to confess.

"We can't be here," he said. "All of us feeling differently in front of a thing that wants something—that's how it gets one of us. We find the way up and out, the high road Kessa promised, and we leave this for—"

The light in the basin shuddered.

It was small, and it was wrong, and every one of them felt it: a flinch in the great slow current, a stutter run back up the rivers of script along the shaft, the way a held note wavers when something leans on the string. The kind voice of the city faltered mid-promise. And far up the shaft, at the lip where they had entered, a single long stretch of darkened wall flickered—and *lit.*

Oscar's stomach dropped.

"It found the thread," he said.

The Redactor's silhouette resolved at the top of the stair, small with distance, the smooth pale blank of its face turned down toward the heart, toward them, toward the font. It had cast about at the edge of the silence he'd made, patient, methodical, until it found where the great sentence picked up again—and the sentence picked up *here,* in the brightest, densest, most-written place in the whole dead city, the one passage it could not possibly resist reading to the end. It started down the stair. It trailed one hand along the wall as it came, and behind its fingers the city's gathered light began, river by river, to thin and dim and *go out.*

"It's reading the heart," Oscar breathed. The understanding arrived with the floor still dropping out from under it. "It's going to edit the *font.* It's going to read this whole working to its last word and strike out whatever it decides doesn't—"

He stopped.

Because the city had been built as one long sentence, and the font was the word at the end of it, and the working that held the whole buried city up—the ceilings, the shaft, the stair under their feet—was the same working now dimming span by span under the Redactor's trailing hand. Cut the sentence and you stopped the reader. He had learned that an hour ago. But you could not cut a sentence in half twice without the half you were standing in coming down, and the Redactor was not lost now; it had its place; it was reading straight toward the load-bearing heart of everything, and when it struck out the font, the font would take the city with it.

The warm light flickered again, dimming from the top down, and the first low groan of failing stone rolled around the great shaft, and the frost crept another knuckle up Oscar's hidden hands, and the basin at his feet went on leaning toward him, patient and certain and kind, offering, still offering, *be set, be whole, be finished,* while a faceless thing came methodically down the stair to unmake them all.

"Right," said Kessa, very flat, putting her back to Oscar's and raising the crossbow at a thing she could not kill. "There it is."
