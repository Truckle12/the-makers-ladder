## Chapter One — Surfacing

*(third-person draft, for comparison)*

Oscar came to in a stone basin, and his first coherent thought was that this was an unusual place to take a nap.

It was not a bath. There was nothing restful about it. He was lying in a shallow cradle the length of a man, carved with a density of figures so fine they looked less like writing than like frost that had decided to mean something, and the cold of the stone had gone all the way into him. Above, a ceiling he couldn't see the top of: dark, and the suggestion of more dark, and a faint silver script crawling across the underside of it like the skin of a frozen pond.

His second thought arrived with the dignity of a man who had spent three centuries being the cleverest person in most rooms. There had been a working. An important one. He had been mid-incantation, on a matter of—

The thought stopped. Cleanly, the way a sentence stops when someone has torn the last word off it.

He reached for it. It was right there, the way a name you have known your whole life can sit just behind the teeth and refuse to come forward. He had been somewhere that mattered, doing something that mattered. The spires of home, perhaps. The long galleries where he kept the real work, the kind he did not show to people who would only misunderstand it. There had been—

Nothing. A clean edge.

Oscar had meddled with planes above his station often enough to know that travel between them did ungentle things to time and to memory both, and so he told himself, sensibly, that he had simply overreached, and that the details would surface the moment he had leisure to be brilliant about it. It was the kind of lie a person tells himself to keep moving. He had always found it more useful than the truth, which tended to sit there being correct while you died.

He sat up. And noticed his hands.

They were his hands—long-fingered, clever, the hands of a man who had never lifted anything heavier than an idea if he could help it. But they were wrong in a way he could not name, and so chose not to dwell on. Too smooth. Unlined. New, almost, the way a coin is new before the world has had a chance to get at it. He turned them over, and the silver light slid across the knuckles, and for one ugly instant he had the sensation of looking at a very good drawing of his hands rather than the hands themselves.

"Fatigue," he informed the empty room, in the firm tone he used on apprentices. "And cold. The cold does strange things to the extremities."

The room did not argue. Rooms rarely did. It was one of their better qualities.

He called for Quill.

It was a habit older than some kingdoms—the familiar's name, the first word out of his mouth on waking, a creature of opinions and feathers who had attended his finest hours and most of his worst ones and could usually be relied upon to be perched somewhere nearby, looking unimpressed. The word was out before he'd decided to say it.

The silence that answered was the particular silence of a place where nothing alive is going to answer, no matter how long you wait.

"Asleep, no doubt," he said. "Or off being useless somewhere. We'll discuss it."

Something cold that had nothing to do with the basin moved through him, and he stood—faster than he'd meant to, because the alternative was to keep sitting there noticing things, and Oscar had never once in his life chosen noticing over action when action was on offer.

The chamber resolved as he rose. Enormous, and old in the specific way of things built before the builders had names anyone now living would know. Other cradles stood in rows, scores of them, most empty, all carved with the same frost-fine script. The walls climbed into dark. The air tasted of stone, and of something underneath the stone: a flat mineral cold he would later learn to associate with the moment before bad weather, and did not yet have a name for.

A sword lay across the lip of his cradle, where a careful person would leave a thing they wanted a sleeper to find.

It was his. He knew it the way a man knows his own reflection—a thin blade the pale grey of moonlight on water, the hilt worn to the shape of a hand that was the shape of his hand. He had carried that sword longer than most cities had stood. He took it up, and the weight of it settled something the hands had unsettled, and he thought: *there, everything is where it should be.* Which was the kind of thought a person has roughly four seconds before everything stops being where it should be.

The voice came out of the walls.

Not an echo off them. The figures themselves brightened as it spoke, silver script flaring in time with the words, so that the room was speaking and the sound was only how it reached him. It was not a human voice. It had been built to sound calm, and it did, the way a frozen lake is calm.

*"The master has fallen,"* it said. *"The line endures. Rise, and be set."*

Oscar had been addressed by gods, by archmages, by at least one entity that was technically a mathematical theorem with delusions of personhood. He knew the tone of a thing that expected to be obeyed, and did not care for it.

"The master," he repeated. "Which master, precisely? I'll want a name. I am extremely particular about whose deaths I am informed of before I've had so much as a cup of anything warm."

*"The master has fallen,"* the voice said again, exactly as before, with the patience of a mechanism that has said a thing ten thousand times and will say it ten thousand more. *"Ascend, and be set, and inherit what is yours."*

"Inherit." A small cold suspicion was taking shape, and he crushed it, because he did not like its outline. "I think there has been a clerical error. I am no one's heir. I am the apex of my own line. People inherit from *me.* It is rather the entire point of me."

The voice did not argue. That was the thing about constructs, and about walls. They never argued. They simply went on being true at a person until he stopped being wrong.

What it did instead was begin to fail.

The script overhead stuttered. A figure the size of a doorway guttered out in the dark to his left. Far above, a sound began that he felt in his teeth before he heard it—a long descending groan of stressed stone, the noise a mountain makes when it is reconsidering being a mountain. Dust sifted down through the silver light. An empty cradle across the room cracked in half with a report like a snapped bone.

"Ah," Oscar said. "We're collapsing. Lovely."

He lifted his free hand, found the cold place in the air where the work lived, and scored a figure he had scored ten thousand times—a simple thing, a hard bright lance of force to clear falling stone before it became falling stone *on him.* It was the first real figure he had ever learned. He could do it asleep. He had done it asleep, once, to the lasting resentment of a man who had tried to rob him in an inn.

The figure came apart in his fingers like wet paper.

He felt it go—and that was the part that would stay with him. Not a fizzle, not a misfire; those he would have understood, those happened to apprentices and the overconfident. This was the sensation of reaching for something that had always borne his weight and finding it simply *thin,* a rope worn to two threads, taking the shape of the figure and then sagging out of it, the force draining away into nothing before it could become anything. The light sputtered, small and sad, and died in his palm.

He stared at the hand. The new, smooth, wrong hand.

"That," he said, with enormous calm, "is not how that works."

The ceiling disagreed by dropping a piece of itself the size of a horse into the cradles beside him.

What followed consisted largely of Oscar running, and being undignified—two activities he was rarely required to combine. He reached the foot of a great curving stair that wound up the chamber wall toward the dark and the noise, because up was where exits lived, and down, in his long experience, was where regrets lived, and he had quite enough of those already in the queue.

He made it perhaps a third of the way before the chamber sent something to stop him.

It came off the wall. He had taken the dark shape there for a buttress, a load-bearing piece of the architecture, and understood his error only when the buttress stepped down onto the stair ahead of him and turned. It had been waiting—patient as the voice—for a sleeper to rise and try to leave without being set. A warden: stone and old script, a tall blunt suggestion of a man with no face and no need of one, the figures across its body glowing the same failing silver as the walls. It raised an arm that ended in something that was not a hand and was very much a weapon, and the air around it bent the way air bends near great heat, except that what poured off it was cold.

Oscar did the sensible thing and tried to reduce it to gravel from a respectful distance.

The figure for that was more complicated—a layered working, force folded back on itself until stone gave up the idea of being stone—and it was well within his abilities. It came apart too, worse than the first, sagging and running out of his mind like water through a cracked jar. He got perhaps half of it scored into the cold before the whole construction failed, and the warden, entirely unreduced, swung at his head.

But Oscar had not survived three hundred years by being good at only one thing.

He called the Measure.

It was not a figure a person threw; it was a figure a person *became*—a working held lit inside the body, sung rather than scored, and it cost in a different currency than the rest of the work. It did not draw on the deep well that had just failed him twice. It drew on something nearer the surface, nearer the self. So when he reached for it—half expecting that thread to be worn through as well, half expecting to feel his own self come apart like the lance of force—

It took.

It took, and the relief nearly put him on the stairs as surely as the warden's arm would have. The Measure settled over him like a second, sharper skin. The world slowed by the exact degree that he sped up. The falling dust hung. The warden's strike, which had been about to remove the more decorative third of his skull, arrived instead with the menace of a slow afternoon, and he was no longer where it landed.

There was no magic in Oscar he loved more, and he had loved a great deal of magic, some of it inadvisably. The Measure was the place where the blade and the work became one gesture. The warden was strong and tireless and felt no pain, no fear, none of the particular embarrassment of missing—but it was *built,* and a thing that is built fights the way it was built to fight, the same patterns over and over. He had spent centuries learning to read patterns. He let it teach him its rhythm across three exchanges—down, across, the heavy overhead it favored—and on the fourth, when the overhead came, he was inside its reach, where the arm could not bring its weight to bear, and the moon-grey blade went into the seam below its shoulder, into the place where the script ran thinnest. He cut the figure rather than the stone.

That was the trick, when the deep work would not come. You could not always break a thing. But everything made of the work was *held together* by the work, and a held thing could be unstitched, if you could find the stitch. He found the stitch. The warden's arm dropped and shattered on the stair; the silver light bled out of the rest of it; and it went from warden to statue to a heap of cold rubble in the space of a held breath.

Oscar stood in the slow-time of the Measure with his heart going like a hammer, and thought, with great and premature satisfaction: *there. Still myself. Whatever is wrong is not wrong with the part that matters.*

He let the Measure go—its own small grief, every time, the world snapping back to its ordinary speed and the ordinary dullness flooding back into his limbs—climbed over the ruin of the warden, and went on up.

The climb was not uneventful.

Near the top, where the stair opened into a vestibule of cracked arches, one cradle had been set apart from the rest, alone in an alcove. Someone was rising out of it.

*Someone.* *Rising.* Both words did more work than they could bear.

There was a figure in the cradle, sitting up the way Oscar had sat up, reaching for the edge the way he had reached for the edge—and it was coming apart as it moved. Not bleeding. Shedding. Where its hand gripped the stone, the hand was going to frost, pale flakes lifting off and drifting up into the cold air, unhurried, almost gentle; and the arm behind the hand was thinning, losing the argument for its own existence, the silver script that should have held it together flickering and letting go. Its mouth was open around a word it could not finish, a word it kept trying and trying to begin. Its eyes found him across the vestibule and held on.

It reached toward him. The reaching hand was already half gone to frost.

Oscar did not go to it. He told himself there was nothing to be done—that the thing was beyond help, that the tower was coming down, that grief was a luxury for people not currently being buried alive. All of it true. None of it the reason.

He did not go because some animal part of him, some part below the clever centuries and the arch voice and the three hundred years of being the smartest man in the room, looked at that frost-shedding figure in its lonely cradle and *recognized something*—and bolted, before the rest of him could ask what.

"A malfunction," he told the empty stair as he climbed. His voice did not come out the way he wanted it to. "A failed working. The place is full of them. Nothing to do with—nothing."

*The master has fallen,* the dying walls murmured, fainter now, far below. *The line endures.*

"Be quiet," he said.

It was not quiet. But it was fading, and he was climbing, and ahead the vestibule narrowed to a single great seal of a door, round as a coin, graven edge to edge with frost-script. When he set his wrong new hand against it, the figures under his palm *brightened*—eager, welcoming, the way no lock has ever brightened for anyone but the one who holds the key.

The door knew him.

He told himself this was only sensible. He was a person of considerable importance; doors *should* know him; if anything it was overdue. He told himself this even as the suspicion he had crushed downstairs uncrushed itself, stood up in the middle of his mind, and refused to sit back down.

The seal turned. The door drew back. The smell that came through it was not the mineral cold of the tower but something vaster and older—dust and deep stone and the flat silence of a very great space that has been shut up for a very long time.

Oscar stepped through, sword in hand, expecting open sky. Expecting the spires of home, or a courtyard, or at worst a long and tedious flight of more stairs.

He stepped out onto a balcony of carved stone, and the world fell away beneath him into a city.

A city. Underground. Beneath a sky of solid rock so high and so far that the dark of it could have passed for night, if night were made of mountain. Streets and towers and the long bones of bridges; rooftops upon rooftops marching away into a gloom without an end he could find; all of it grey, all of it dead, all of it furred here and there with the same pale frost that had taken the figure in the cradle. A buried city, sealed under the skin of the world, vast past reckoning—and so old that Oscar, who was old, who had known this continent when half its kingdoms were arguments and the other half were trees, did not recognize a single line of it.

He stood with the dead city spread below him and felt, for the first time in longer than he could have said, genuinely and entirely lost. Not turned-around lost. Not which-corridor lost. Lost the way a word is lost when the last word of the sentence has been torn off.

"All right," he said softly—to the absent familiar, to the master who had fallen, to the city that had no business existing. "All right. We will find out where we are, and who is responsible, and then I will have a great many sharp things to say to all of them."

It was then that he became aware of a small mechanical sound to his right. The dry, deliberate click of a crossbow easing to full draw.

He turned his head, very slowly, in the manner of a man who would like it on the record that he was being reasonable.

A woman crouched on the broken edge of the balcony not six feet away—weathered, still, and entirely unsurprised, the way only people who have survived a great deal manage to be. The crossbow she held on his face was old and well kept and perfectly steady, the bolt aimed at the point precisely between his eyes with an accuracy he could not help, even then, admiring.

"Don't," she said, "do any of that with your hands."

His options were not numerous.

"My good woman," he began, in his most reassuring voice, the one he had used to talk his way past border guards and angry husbands and one memorably literal-minded demon, "I think you'll find this is all a tremendous misunderstanding, and that I am, in point of fact, a person of significant—"

"You came up out of the dead-house," she said. Flat. Not a question. Her eyes flicked once to the moon-grey sword and back, and something in them changed when they returned—closed, decided, the way a door decides. "Up out of *that.* Through a door that doesn't open."

"It opened for me," he said, before he could think better of it. "Doors do, rather."

And there—though he had not yet arrived there himself—a stranger looked at Oscar and arrived somewhere first.

"Yeah," she said quietly. The crossbow did not waver. "That's the problem."

Far below, down in the dead grey streets of a city that had been sealed and silent longer than memory, something the size of nothing good began, unhurriedly, to move.
