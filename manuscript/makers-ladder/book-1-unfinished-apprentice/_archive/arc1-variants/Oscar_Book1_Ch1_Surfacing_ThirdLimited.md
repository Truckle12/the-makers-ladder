## Chapter One — Surfacing

Oscar came to in a stone basin the temperature of a grave, and his first conclusion—reached with the serene confidence of a man who had been correct about nearly everything for three hundred years—was that an error had been made somewhere, by someone, and that he would shortly be owed an apology.

It was not a bath. He wanted that established at once, if only to himself. There was nothing restful about it. He was lying in a shallow cradle the length of a man, carved end to end with figures so fine they did not look like writing so much as frost that had decided, against its nature, to mean something. The cold of the stone had gone all the way into him, past the skin, into the place where a person keeps the conviction that they are warm. Above him a ceiling climbed into dark he could not see the top of, and across the underside of it a faint silver script crawled like light moving under the skin of a frozen pond.

A working, then. There had been a working—an important one, the kind that mattered, the kind he did not explain to people who would only get it wrong. He had been mid-incantation, on a matter of—

The thought stopped. Not faded; *stopped*, the way a sentence stops when someone has torn the last word off the page.

He reached after it. It was right there, the way your own name can sit just behind your teeth and refuse to come forward when a stranger asks for it. He had been somewhere. The spires of home, perhaps, or the long cold galleries where the real work lived. There had been a reason, a brilliant and pressing reason, and a danger, and then—

A clean edge. Nothing past it.

This did not alarm him, or he declined to let it. He had spent his life prying at the seams between one plane and the next, and he knew what such travel did to time and to memory—ungentle things, both. He had overreached. That was all. The details would surface the moment he had the leisure to be clever about them, and until then he would simply proceed, which was the great advantage of a lie told to oneself: it kept a person moving, while the truth only sat there being correct and watching him die.

He sat up. And looked at his hands.

They were his hands. He knew them the way he knew his own handwriting—long-fingered, clever, the hands of a man who had never lifted anything heavier than an idea when there was an alternative. But they were wrong, in a way he could find no word for and therefore declined to look at directly. Too smooth. Unlined. *New*, the way a coin is new before the world has had its turn at it. He turned them over in the silver light, and for one bad moment had the sensation of studying an excellent drawing of his hands rather than the hands themselves.

"Fatigue," he told the empty dark, in the brisk tone he reserved for apprentices and the recently bereaved. "And cold. The cold makes a liar of the extremities."

The dark did not contradict him. That was the chief virtue of dark. It let a man be wrong in peace.

He called for Quill.

He did it without deciding to—the name was simply the first word his mouth reached for on opening, a reflex older than several kingdoms. Somewhere there ought to be an answering rustle, a weight settling onto his shoulder, a small disreputable creature of feathers and opinions arriving to be unimpressed with him. Quill had attended his finest hours and the considerably greater number of his worst ones, and had never in all that time failed to answer.

The silence that came back was the particular silence of a place where nothing living is going to answer, however long a man cares to wait for it.

"Asleep," Oscar decided. "Or off sulking. We'll discuss it."

Something cold that owed nothing to the basin moved through him, and he got to his feet faster than he meant to—because the alternative to standing was to go on sitting there noticing things, and Oscar had never once in three centuries chosen noticing over action when action could be had instead.

The chamber assembled itself around him as he rose, and it was vast, and it was old in the specific way of things made before their makers had names that anyone now alive would know. Cradles stood in rows across the floor, scores of them, most of them empty, every one carved with the same frost-fine script. The walls went up and kept going. The air tasted of stone, and beneath the stone of something flatter and colder, a mineral nothing he had no name for yet and would, before this was over, come to know intimately.

A sword lay across the lip of his cradle. Someone had set it there the way you leave a thing where a sleeper is sure to find it on waking.

It was his. He knew it before he touched it—a thin blade the pale grey of moonlight on still water, the hilt worn to the precise shape of a hand that was the precise shape of his. He had carried it longer than most of the cities he'd outlived. He took it up, and its weight settled something in him that the hands had knocked loose, and he allowed himself to think *there, you see, everything is where it ought to be*—which is, as a rule, the last thought a man has before everything stops being where it ought to be.

The voice came out of the walls.

Not an echo off them. The script *itself* brightened to speak, silver figures flaring across the stone in time with the words, so that the room said the thing and the sound was only how it arrived at him. It was not a living voice. It had been built to be calm, and it was calm, the way a frozen lake is calm.

*"The master has fallen. The line endures. Rise, and be set."*

Oscar had been addressed in his time by gods, by archmages, by an entity that was, strictly, a theorem with ideas above its station. He knew the voice of a thing accustomed to obedience, and he had never once enjoyed it.

"The master," he repeated. "Which master, precisely. I'll have a name. I am particular about whose deaths I'm informed of before I've had so much as a cup of something warm."

*"The master has fallen,"* the voice said again—identically, with the patience of a mechanism that has said a thing ten thousand times and is fully prepared to say it ten thousand more. *"Ascend. Be set. Inherit what is yours."*

"*Inherit.*" A small cold notion was assembling itself at the edge of his mind, and he stepped on it, because he did not care for the shape it was taking. "I think there's been a clerical error, friend. I am no one's heir. I am the apex of my own line. People inherit from *me*. It's rather the entire point of me."

The voice did not argue. Constructs never argued, and neither did walls; they simply went on being true at a man until he tired of being wrong. What it did instead was begin to fail.

The script overhead stuttered. Off in the dark to his left, a figure the size of a doorway guttered out and did not relight. Far above, a sound began that he felt in his teeth before his ears caught it—a long descending groan of stone under more weight than stone had agreed to, the noise a mountain makes when it begins to reconsider being a mountain. Dust came sifting down through the silver light. An empty cradle across the room split in half with a crack like a snapped bone.

"Ah," said Oscar. "We're collapsing. *Marvellous.*"

He raised his free hand, reached into the cold place in the air where the work lived, and scored a figure he had scored ten thousand times—the simplest thing, a hard bright lance of force to throw falling stone aside before it became falling stone *upon him*. It was the first real figure he had ever learned. He had done it half-asleep. He had once done it *fully* asleep, to the enduring resentment of a man who'd tried to rob him in an inn.

It came apart in his fingers like wet paper.

He felt it go, and it was the *feeling* that stayed with him, long after—not a fizzle, not a misfire, nothing so honest as failure. It was the sensation of putting his weight on a thing that had borne his weight his whole life and finding it gone *thin*, a rope worn down to two threads, taking the shape of the figure for half a breath and then sagging out of it, the force he'd called bleeding away into nothing before it could amount to anything at all. The light of it sputtered, small and embarrassed, and died in his palm.

He looked at the hand. The new, smooth, wrong hand.

"That," he informed it, with enormous and entirely manufactured calm, "is not how that works."

The ceiling disagreed by letting fall a piece of itself the size of a horse, into the cradles where he had been standing a breath before.

What came next was mostly running, and a great deal of being undignified—two pursuits Oscar was rarely obliged to combine. He reached the foot of a great stair that wound up the curve of the wall toward the dark and the noise, and went up it, because up was where exits lived and down, in his long and bitter experience, was where regrets were kept, and his were already overstocked.

He had climbed perhaps a third of it when the chamber sent something to stop him.

It came away from the wall. He had taken the dark mass for a buttress, some honest load-bearing piece of the architecture, and understood his mistake only when the buttress stepped down onto the stair above him and turned. It had been standing there the whole while—patient as the voice—waiting for a sleeper to rise and try to leave unset. A warden: stone and old script, a tall blunt approximation of a man with no face and no use for one, the figures across it glowing the same dying silver as the walls. It lifted an arm that ended in a thing which was not a hand and was very much a weapon, and the air around it bent the way air bends near a furnace, except that what rolled off it was cold.

Oscar did the sensible thing and attempted to reduce it to gravel from a courteous distance.

The figure for that was a more ambitious affair—layered force folded back upon itself until stone simply gave up the idea of being stone—and it was well within his powers. It failed worse than the lance had. He got perhaps half of it laid into the cold before the whole structure sagged and ran out of his mind like water from a cracked jar, and the warden, wholly unreduced, swung for his head.

But a man does not survive three hundred years by being good at only one thing.

He called the Measure.

It was not a figure a man threw, the Measure; it was a figure a man *became*—a working held burning inside the body, sung rather than written, and it was paid for in a different coin than the rest of the craft. It did not draw from the deep well that had just betrayed him twice. It drew from something nearer the surface, nearer the self, and so when he reached for it—half braced to find that thread worn through as well, half braced to feel his own self come apart in his hands like the lance had—

it took.

It took, and the relief of it nearly dropped him on the stairs as surely as the warden's arm would have. The Measure came down over him like a second and sharper skin. The world slowed by precisely the degree that he quickened; the falling dust hung in the air; the warden's strike, which had been an instant from removing the more ornamental third of his skull, arrived instead with all the urgency of a slow afternoon, and he was no longer standing where it fell.

There was no magic Oscar loved better, and he had loved a great deal of magic, some of it against all advice. The Measure was the place where the blade and the work stopped being two things. The warden was strong, and tireless, and immune to pain, to fear, to the small private humiliation of missing—but it was *built*, and a built thing fights the way it was built to fight, the same shapes over and over, world without end. Oscar had spent centuries learning to read shapes. He let it school him through three exchanges—the downstroke, the cross, the heavy overhead it loved best—and on the fourth, as the overhead came down, he was no longer beneath it. He was *inside* its reach, close against the trunk of it where the long arm could put no weight behind the blow, and the moon-grey blade slid into the seam beneath its shoulder, into the narrow place where the script ran thinnest. He cut the figure, and not the stone.

That was the whole trick of it, when the deep work would not come. You could not always break a thing. But everything the work had made, the work also *held together*—and a held thing can be unstitched, if a man can only find the stitch. He found the stitch. The arm dropped off the warden and burst on the stair; the silver light ran out of the rest of it like water from a tipped basin; and it went from warden to statue to a heap of cold rubble inside a single held breath.

Oscar stood over the wreck of it in the slow amber of the Measure, his heart slamming, and let himself think—too soon, far too soon—*there. Still myself. Whatever's wrong, it isn't wrong with the part that counts.*

He let the Measure go. That was its own small grief, every time: the world snapping back up to its dull ordinary speed, the dull ordinary heaviness flooding back into his arms. He climbed over the ruin and went on up.

The rest of the climb was not, regrettably, uneventful.

Near the top, where the stair gave onto a vestibule of cracked arches, one cradle stood apart from all the others, alone in an alcove of its own. And someone was rising out of it.

*Someone. Rising.* Both words strained at more than they could carry.

There was a figure sitting up in the cradle, reaching for the edge the way Oscar had reached for the edge—and it was coming apart in the doing of it. Not bleeding. *Shedding.* Where the hand gripped the stone the hand was going to frost, pale flakes lifting from it and drifting unhurried up into the cold, and the arm above the hand was thinning, losing its grip on the argument for its own existence, the silver script that should have bound it flickering and letting go a thread at a time. The mouth was open on a word it could not get to the front of, a word it kept beginning and beginning and never reaching. The eyes found him across the vestibule and fixed there, and held.

It reached for him. The reaching hand was already half gone to frost.

Oscar did not go to it.

He told himself the reasons, and the reasons were even true: that there was nothing to be done; that the thing was past help; that the tower was coming down and grief was a luxury for men not presently being buried alive. All true. None of it the reason.

He did not go because some animal thing in him, some part that lived well beneath the clever centuries and the dry voice and the long habit of being the sharpest mind in any room, looked at that frost-shedding figure alone in its cradle and *knew* it—and ran, before the rest of him could turn and ask what it was that it had recognized.

"A malfunction," he said to the empty stair, climbing. His voice came out smaller than he'd sent it. "A failed working. The place is rotten with them. Nothing whatsoever to do with—nothing."

Below him, faint now and growing fainter, the dying walls went on murmuring. *The master has fallen. The line endures.*

"Be quiet," Oscar said.

It was not quiet. But it was fading, and he was climbing, and the vestibule narrowed ahead of him to a single great seal of a door, round as a coin and graven from edge to edge with the frost-script. When he laid his wrong new hand against it, the figures beneath his palm *brightened*—eager, glad, the way no lock has ever brightened for any hand but the one that holds its key.

The door knew him.

That was only sensible, he told himself. He was a man of consequence; doors *ought* to know him; if anything it was overdue. He told himself this even as the cold notion he had trodden on downstairs got back to its feet, walked to the center of his mind, and sat down where he could not help but look at it.

The seal turned. The door drew back. And the breath of air that came through it was not the mineral cold of the tower at all but something older and wider—dust, and deep stone, and the flat enormous silence of a great space sealed up a very long time ago.

Oscar stepped through with the sword in his hand, expecting open sky. Expecting the spires of home, or a courtyard, or at the very worst a long and tiresome flight of further stairs.

He stepped out onto a balcony of carved stone, and the world dropped away beneath him into a city.

A city. Underground. Beneath a sky of solid rock so high and so far above that its darkness could have passed for an honest night, if night were ever made of mountain. Streets, and towers, and the long pale bones of bridges; roofs beyond roofs marching off into a gloom he could find no end to; all of it grey, all of it dead, all of it furred here and there with the same soft frost that had been eating the figure in the cradle. A buried city, sealed beneath the skin of the world, vast past any reckoning he could put to it—and so old that Oscar, who was old, who had known this continent when half its kingdoms were still arguments and the other half were still trees, could not place a single line of it.

He stood there with the dead city spread out below him and felt, for the first time in longer than he could have said, entirely and honestly lost. Not turned-around lost. Not which-corridor lost. Lost the way a sentence is lost when someone has torn the last word off the end of it.

"Right," he said quietly—to the absent familiar, to the fallen master, to the impossible city that had no business existing under the bones of the earth. "Right. We find out where we are, and who's to blame, and then there will be words. A great many sharp ones."

And it was at this moment, with the wreck of his certainty still ringing in him, that he became aware of a small, mechanical, deliberate sound off to his right: the dry click of a crossbow being drawn to the full.

He turned his head, very slowly, in the manner of a man hoping to have it entered into the record that he was being entirely reasonable.

A woman was crouched on the broken lip of the balcony, not six feet from him—weathered, motionless, and wholly unsurprised, in the way of people who have outlived enough that surprise has stopped being worth the effort. The crossbow she held level with his face was old, and well kept, and did not move at all; the bolt sat aimed at the exact point between his eyes with a competence that, even then, even there, he could not entirely keep himself from admiring.

"Don't," she said, "do any of that with your hands."

His options, he reflected, were not many.

"My good woman." He reached for the warm and reasonable voice, the one that had carried him past border guards and outraged husbands and one regrettably literal-minded demon. "I think you'll find this is all a considerable misunderstanding, and that I am, as it happens, a person of no small—"

"You came up out of the dead-house." Flat. Not a question. Her gaze dropped once to the moon-grey sword in his hand and came back up, and something in her face shifted when it returned—closed, and settled, the way a door settles when it has decided to stay shut. "Up out of *that*. Through a door that doesn't open."

"It opened for me," he said, before the wiser parts of him could intervene. "Doors do, rather."

He watched her hear it. He watched something arrive behind her eyes—some conclusion she had reached and he, plainly, had not, a place she had got to first and was now waiting, not unkindly, for him to catch up to.

"Yeah," she said, very quietly. The crossbow did not waver. "That's the problem."

And far below them, down in the dead grey streets of a city that had lain sealed and silent longer than memory ran, something the size of nothing good began, without the least hurry, to move.
