## Chapter One — Surfacing

If this account ends mid-sentence, Quill, you are to assume I have been crushed, dissolved, or otherwise inconvenienced beyond the point of dictation, and you are to bill the responsible parties for the interruption. Generously. I have always believed in itemized grief.

I record this from inside a tower I do not remember entering, which I want noted, for the record, as not my fault.

Let me describe my circumstances with the precision they deserve.

I was lying in a basin. Not a bath—I want to be clear that there was nothing restful about it. It was a shallow stone cradle the length of a man, carved with a density of figures so fine they looked less like writing than like frost that had decided to mean something. The cold of it had gone all the way into me. When I opened my eyes I was staring up at a ceiling I could not see the top of, just dark and the suggestion of more dark, and a faint silver script crawling across the stone overhead like the underside of a frozen pond.

My first coherent thought was: *this is an unusual place to take a nap.*

My second thought, arriving with the dignity of a man who has spent three centuries being the cleverest person in most rooms, was that there had been a working. An important one. I had been mid-incantation on a matter of—

And there the thought simply stopped. Like a sentence with the last word torn off.

I reached for it. The memory was *right there*, the way a name you've known your whole life can sit just behind your teeth and refuse to come forward. I had been somewhere. Doing something that mattered. The spires of home, perhaps, or the long galleries where I kept my real work, the kind I did not show to people who would only misunderstand it. There had been—

Nothing. A clean edge. I have meddled with planes above my station often enough to know that travel between them does ungentle things to time and to memory both, and so I told myself, sensibly, that I had simply overreached, and that the details would surface once I'd had a moment to be brilliant about it. This is the sort of lie one tells oneself in order to keep moving. I recommend it. It is far more useful than the truth, which tends to sit there being correct while you die.

I sat up.

That was when I noticed my hands.

They were my hands. I want to be precise: they were unmistakably *mine*, long-fingered and clever, the hands of someone who has never lifted anything heavier than an idea if he could help it. But they were wrong in a way I could not name and so refused to dwell on. Too smooth. Unlined. New, almost, the way a coin is new before the world gets at it. I turned them over and the silver light from above slid across them and for one ugly instant I had the sensation of looking at a very good drawing of my hands rather than the hands themselves.

"Fatigue," I informed the empty room, in the firm tone I use on apprentices. "And cold. The cold does strange things to the extremities."

The room did not argue. Rooms rarely do. It is one of their better qualities.

I called for Quill.

I should explain, for whoever inherits these notes, that Quill is my familiar, a creature of opinions and feathers who has accompanied my finest hours and most of my worst ones, and who can usually be relied upon to be perched somewhere nearby looking unimpressed with me. It is a habit older than some kingdoms, calling for Quill when I wake. The word was out of my mouth before I'd decided to say it.

Silence answered. The particular silence of a place where nothing alive is going to answer, no matter how long you wait.

"Asleep, no doubt," I said. "Or off being useless somewhere. We'll discuss it."

But something cold that had nothing to do with the basin moved through me, and I stood up faster than I meant to, because the alternative to standing was to keep sitting there noticing things, and I have never once in my life chosen noticing over action when action was available.

The chamber resolved around me as I rose. It was enormous and it was old in the specific way of things built before the people who built them had names that anyone now living would recognize. Other cradles stood in rows, scores of them, most empty, all carved with that same frost-fine script. The walls went up into dark. The air tasted of stone and of something underneath the stone, a flat mineral cold that I associate, now, with the moment before bad weather and did not yet have a name for.

Resting across the lip of my own cradle, where a sensible person would put a thing they wanted a sleeper to find, lay a sword.

It was mine. I knew it the way you know your own reflection: a thin blade the pale grey of moonlight on water, the hilt worn to the shape of a hand that was the shape of my hand. I had carried that sword for longer than most cities had stood. I picked it up and the weight of it settled some part of me that the hands had unsettled, and I thought, *there, you see, everything is where it should be,* which is the kind of thing you think roughly four seconds before everything stops being where it should be.

The voice came out of the walls.

I do not mean it echoed off the walls. I mean the figures themselves brightened as it spoke, the silver script flaring in time with the words, so that the room was talking and the sound was merely how it reached me. It was not a human voice. It had been built to sound calm, and it did, the way a frozen lake is calm.

*"The master has fallen,"* it said. *"The line endures. Rise, and be set."*

I have been spoken to by gods, by archmages, by at least one entity that was technically a mathematical theorem with delusions of personhood. I know the tone of a thing that expects to be obeyed. I did not care for it.

"The master," I repeated. "Which master, precisely? I'll want a name. I am extremely particular about whose deaths I'm informed of before I've had so much as a cup of anything warm."

*"The master has fallen,"* the voice said again, exactly as before, with the patience of a mechanism that has said a thing ten thousand times and will say it ten thousand more. *"Ascend, and be set, and inherit what is yours."*

"Inherit," I said. A small, cold suspicion was forming, and I crushed it, because I did not like its shape. "I think there's been a clerical error. I am no one's heir, my good wall. I am the apex of my own line. People inherit from *me*. It's rather the whole point of me."

The voice did not argue either. That is the thing about constructs and about walls. They never argue. They simply continue to be true at you until you stop being wrong, and I have always found that a deeply irritating quality in a conversational partner.

What it did instead was begin to fail.

The silver script overhead stuttered. A figure the size of a doorway, off in the dark to my left, guttered out entirely, and somewhere far above a sound began that I felt in my teeth before I heard it—a long, descending groan of stressed stone, the noise a mountain makes when it is reconsidering being a mountain. Dust sifted down through the silver light. One of the empty cradles across the room cracked clean in half with a report like a snapped bone.

"Ah," I said. "We're collapsing. Lovely. I'll just—"

I lifted my free hand, found the cold place in the air where the work lives, and scored a figure I have scored ten thousand times, a simple thing, a hard bright lance of force to clear a path through falling stone before it became falling stone *on me*. It is the first real figure I ever learned. I could do it asleep. I have, in fact, done it asleep, to the lasting resentment of a man who once tried to rob me in an inn.

The figure came apart in my fingers like wet paper.

I felt it go. That's the part I keep returning to. Not a fizzle, not a misfire—those I would understand, those happen to apprentices and the overconfident. This was the sensation of reaching for something that had always borne my weight and finding it simply *thin*, a rope worn down to two threads, taking the shape of the figure and then sagging out of it, the force I'd called for draining away into nothing before it could become anything at all. The light of it sputtered, sad and small, and died in my palm.

I stared at my hand. The new, smooth, wrong hand.

"That," I said, with enormous calm, "is not how that works."

The ceiling disagreed by dropping a piece of itself the size of a horse into the row of cradles beside me.

I will spare you the next several seconds, Quill, as they consisted largely of me running and being undignified, two activities I am rarely required to combine. I reached the base of a great curving stair that wound up the chamber's wall toward the dark and the noise, because up is where exits live and down, in my professional experience, is where regrets live, and I had quite enough of those queued already.

I made it perhaps a third of the way up before the chamber sent something to stop me.

It came off the wall. I had taken the dark shape there for a buttress, a column, some load-bearing piece of the architecture, and it was only when the buttress stepped down onto the stair ahead of me and turned that I understood it had been waiting, patient as the voice, for a sleeper to rise and try to leave without being *set*, whatever that meant. It was a warden. Stone and old script, a tall blunt suggestion of a man with no face and no need of one, the figures across its body glowing the same failing silver as the walls. It raised an arm that ended in something that was not a hand and was very much a weapon, and the air around it bent the way air bends near great heat, except that what poured off it was cold.

I did the sensible thing. I tried to reduce it to gravel from a respectful distance.

The figure for that is more complicated—a layered working, a press of force folded back on itself until stone simply gives up the idea of being stone—and it is well within my abilities. Was. Is. Will be again, I promise you, if I have to claw it back out of the dark one figure at a time.

It came apart too. Worse than the first. I got perhaps half of it scored into the cold before the whole construction sagged and ran out of my mind like water through a cracked jar, and the warden, entirely unreduced, swung at my head.

I have not survived three hundred years by being good at only one thing.

I called the Measure.

You know how it goes, Quill, you've watched me do it a hundred times and pretended to be bored. The Measure is not a figure you throw; it is a figure you *become*, a working held lit inside the body, sung rather than scored, and it costs you in a different currency than the rest of the work. It does not draw on the deep well that had just failed me twice. It draws on something nearer the surface, nearer the self, and so when I reached for it—half expecting that thread to be worn through as well, half expecting to feel my own self come apart like the lance of force—

It took.

It *took*, and the relief of it nearly put me on the stairs as surely as the warden's arm would have. The Measure settled over me like a second, sharper skin. The world slowed by the exact degree that I sped up; the falling dust hung; the warden's strike, which had been about to remove the more decorative third of my skull, arrived instead with all the menace of a slow afternoon. I was already not where it landed.

There is no magic in me I love more than the Measure, and I say that as a man who has loved a great deal of magic, some of it inadvisably. It is the place where the blade and the work become the same gesture. The warden was strong and it was tireless and it did not feel pain or fear or the particular embarrassment of missing, but it was *built*, and a thing that is built fights the way it was built to fight, the same patterns over and over, and I have spent centuries learning to read patterns. I let it teach me its rhythm across three exchanges—down, across, the heavy overhead it favored—and on the fourth, when the overhead came, I was not under it. I was inside its reach, where the arm could not bring its weight to bear, and the moon-grey blade went into the seam below where its shoulder met the rest of it, into the place where the script ran thinnest, and I cut the figure rather than the stone.

That is the trick of it, when the deep work won't come. You cannot always break the thing. But everything made of the work is *held together* by the work, and a held thing can be unstitched if you can find the stitch. I found the stitch. The warden's arm dropped off it and shattered on the stair, and the silver light bled out of the rest of it, and it went from a warden to a statue to a heap of cold rubble in the space of a held breath, and I stood there in the slowing-down of the Measure with my heart going like a hammer and thought, with great and entirely premature satisfaction:

*There. I am still myself. Whatever is wrong is not wrong with the part that matters.*

I let the Measure go, which is its own small grief every time, the world snapping back up to its ordinary speed and the ordinary dullness coming back into my limbs, and I climbed over the warden and kept going up.

I want to tell you the rest of the climb was uneventful. It was not.

The thing I passed—I have argued with myself about whether to record it at all, Quill, because I do not understand it and I dislike committing to parchment things I do not understand; it sets a bad precedent. But you would want it noted, you ghoulish little creature, so here.

Near the top of the stair, where it opened into a vestibule of cracked arches, one of the cradles had been set apart from the others, alone in an alcove, and someone was rising out of it.

I say someone. I say rising. Both words are doing more work than they can bear.

There was a figure in the cradle, sitting up the way I had sat up, reaching for the edge the way I had reached for the edge. And it was coming apart as it moved. Not bleeding—*shedding*. Where its hand gripped the stone, the hand was going to frost, pale flakes lifting off it and drifting up into the cold air, unhurried, almost gentle, and the arm behind the hand was thinning, losing the argument for its own existence, the silver script that should have held it together flickering and letting go. Its face—I did not let myself look long at its face. I had the impression of a mouth open around a word it could not finish, a word it kept trying and trying to begin, and of eyes that found me across the vestibule and held on, and of an expression I have spent every night since failing not to name.

It reached toward me. The reaching hand was already half gone to frost.

I did not go to it. I want that recorded plainly, because I have done a great many things I'm proud of and that is not among them. I told myself there was nothing to be done, that the thing was beyond help, that the tower was coming down and grief is a luxury for people who are not currently being buried alive. All of which was true. None of which is why I didn't go.

I didn't go because some animal part of me, some part below the clever centuries and the arch voice and the three hundred years of being the smartest man in the room, looked at that frost-shedding figure in its lonely cradle and *recognized something*, and bolted before the rest of me could ask what.

"It was a malfunction," I told the empty stair, climbing. My voice did not sound the way I wanted it to. "A failed working. The place is full of them. Nothing to do with—nothing."

*The master has fallen,* the dying walls murmured, far below now, fainter. *The line endures.*

"Be quiet," I said.

It was not quiet. But it was fading, and I was climbing, and ahead of me the vestibule narrowed to a single great seal of a door, round as a coin and graven edge to edge with the frost-script, and when I set my wrong new hand against it the figures under my palm *brightened*, eager, welcoming, the way no lock has ever brightened for anyone but the one who holds the key.

The door knew me.

I told myself this was only sensible. I am a person of considerable importance; doors *should* know me; if anything it was overdue. I told myself this even as the cold suspicion I had crushed downstairs uncrushed itself and stood up in the middle of my mind and would not sit back down.

The seal turned. The door drew back. And the smell that came through it was not the mineral cold of the tower but something vaster and older and stranger—dust and deep stone and the particular flat silence of a very great space that has been shut up for a very long time.

I stepped through, sword in hand, expecting open sky. Expecting the spires of home, perhaps, or a courtyard, or the worst case a long and tedious flight of more stairs.

I stepped out onto a balcony of carved stone, and the world fell away beneath me into a city.

A city. Underground. Beneath a sky of solid rock so high and so distant that the dark of it could have been night if night were made of mountain. Streets and towers and the long bones of bridges, rooftops upon rooftops marching away into a gloom I could not see the end of, all of it grey, all of it dead, all of it furred here and there with that same pale frost that had taken the figure in the cradle. A buried city, sealed under the skin of the world, vast past any reckoning, and so old that I—who am old, who knew this continent when half its kingdoms were arguments and the other half were trees—did not recognize a single line of it.

I stood there with the dead city spread below me and I felt, for the first time in longer than I can tell you, genuinely and entirely lost. Not turned-around lost. Not which-corridor lost. Lost the way a word is lost when the last word of the sentence has been torn off.

"All right," I said softly, to Quill who was not there, to the master who had fallen, to the city that had no business existing. "All right. We'll simply find out where we are, and who's responsible, and then I'll have a great many sharp things to say to all of them."

It was at this point, Quill, that I became aware of a small mechanical sound directly to my right. The dry, deliberate click of a crossbow being eased to full draw.

I turned my head, very slowly, in the manner of a man who would like it on the record that he is being reasonable.

There was a woman crouched on the broken edge of the balcony not six feet from me, weathered and still and entirely unsurprised, the way only people who have survived a great deal manage to be unsurprised. The crossbow she had trained on my face was old and well kept and absolutely steady, and the bolt at the end of it was pointed at the spot directly between my eyes with an accuracy I could not help, even then, even there, admiring.

"Don't," she said, "do any of that with your hands."

I considered my options. They were not numerous.

"My good woman," I began, in my most reassuring voice, the one I have used to talk my way past border guards and angry husbands and one memorably literal-minded demon. "I think you'll find this is all a tremendous misunderstanding, and that I am, in point of fact, a person of significant—"

"You came up out of the dead-house," she said. Flat. Not a question. Her eyes flicked, once, to the moon-grey sword in my hand, and back to my face, and something in them changed when they came back—closed, decided, the way a door decides. "Up out of *that*. Through a door that doesn't open."

"It opened for me," I said, before I could think better of it. "Doors do, rather."

I want it noted, Quill, for whatever court eventually adjudicates the disaster my life is about to become, that this was the precise moment I watched a stranger look at me and arrive somewhere I had not yet arrived myself.

"Yeah," she said quietly. The crossbow did not waver. "That's the problem."

Behind her, far down in the dead grey streets, in a city that had been sealed and silent for longer than memory, something the size of nothing good began, unhurriedly, to move.
