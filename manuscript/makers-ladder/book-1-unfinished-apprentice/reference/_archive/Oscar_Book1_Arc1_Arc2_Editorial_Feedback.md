# Oscar — Book 1: Arc 1 & Arc 2 Editorial Feedback

## Scope

This document bundles editorial feedback for:

- **Arc 1 — The Buried City**
- **Arc 2 — Marrow**

The focus is developmental editing, prose-level patterning, character voice, fantasy/progression expectations, and concrete revision priorities.

---

# Big-Picture Read

You have a real book here, not just a premise. The strongest thing is that the magic, character arc, and plot are all speaking the same language: writing, editing, setting, inheritance, unfinished people, chosen identity, and power as something aimed rather than possessed.

The main danger is not that the story lacks depth. It is that the prose sometimes keeps explaining the depth after the reader has already felt it.

**Revision mantra:**

> Trust the scene. Trust the reader. Keep the best line, cut the echo.

# Arc 1 — The Buried City

## Overall Editorial Read

Arc 1 is strong and structurally clean. It has one contained engine: Oscar wakes, meets the crew, descends, confronts the font, refuses the offered identity, and emerges changed.

The arc has a very clear emotional thesis:

> Oscar is not the man he remembers; he becomes real by choosing others over completion.

Arc 1 is more elegant than Arc 2 at the structural level because it has fewer moving pieces and a cleaner symbolic throughline. The dead city, the Redactor, the font, the cradles, Oscar’s missing familiar, and Wick’s first lesson all point toward the same central question:

> What makes a person real?

## What Arc 1 Does Especially Well

### 1. Chapter 1 is a strong opener

Oscar waking in the basin works. The first paragraph gives us voice, arrogance, disorientation, and danger immediately. The “apology would shortly be owed” line is exactly the kind of arrogance that feels fun rather than irritating.

The missing Quill beat is also excellent. It humanizes Oscar before the reader fully understands him. His empty-shoulder gesture is one of the best emotional details in the whole draft.

### 2. The magic system is introduced well

The lance explanation is clear enough early on: magic is argument, figure, current, and will. The difference between the deep current and the Measure is also useful. That gives readers a functional sense of Oscar’s power loss without turning the chapter into a glossary.

### 3. The Redactor is a standout monster

The Redactor is not just scary; it is conceptually tied to the book’s identity theme. A thing that “edits” people and cities is perfect for a protagonist who may himself be a revised copy.

This is top-tier fantasy monster design.

### 4. Wick and Oscar’s first teacher/student beat is excellent

The “power isn’t the rare thing, the rare thing is the aiming” moment is probably one of the emotional anchors of the whole book. It pays off again when Oscar realizes he was also describing himself.

That is clean, satisfying character-and-magic integration.

### 5. The Arc 1 ending is genuinely strong

The final dream of the older Oscar’s life, Quill, the warm gallery, and the realization that this happy life was real but “entirely, perfectly, not his” is a very good hook.

It hurts in the right way.

The final note about other cradles and other sleepers also widens the series premise without feeling like pure exposition dumping.

## Biggest Arc 1 Problems

### 1. The prose is already flirting with over-explanation

This is less severe than in Arc 2, but the habit starts here. The prose often explains the meaning of a moment immediately after the scene has already made it clear.

**Example type:**

> The font offered to make him whole, and the price was simply to be no one new.

That is a strong line. But surrounding it are several paragraphs saying adjacent versions of the same thing: finished, completed, set, faithful, perfect, not new, copied, restored.

**How to fix it:**

Keep the best formulation and cut the echo. The reader does not need every synonym for the idea once the emotional choice is clear.

### 2. Oscar’s interior narration can become too essay-like during action

Chapter 8 is emotionally strong, but during the collapsing-city climax, Oscar has a lot of beautifully articulated realization while the world is literally falling apart.

Some of that works because he is a scholar and this is his POV. But trim the philosophical layering by about 10–15%.

In action, the reader needs:

1. What is happening?
2. What choice does Oscar have?
3. What does it cost?
4. What changes because of the choice?

You sometimes add a fifth layer: what the choice means, how it mirrors previous lessons, how Oscar understands it, and how the reader should understand it. That fifth layer is where you can cut.

### 3. Kessa is compelling but slightly under-emotional

Kessa reads well: practical, lethal, tired, watchful. The Doss knife detail helps a lot. But compared to Cael and Wick, she is more functional than emotionally exposed.

**How to fix it:**

Add one more small crack from her before the end of Arc 1. Not a speech. Something physical.

Possible options:

- After Oscar saves them, she almost says Doss’s name and does not.
- She checks the wrong knife again.
- She steps into a formation position Doss used to take, then corrects herself.
- She accepts Oscar’s help while making it clear she hates needing it.

### 4. The crew accepts Oscar slightly fast

This mostly works because the city forces them together. But emotionally, by the end of Arc 1, Cael’s hand-offer is doing a lot.

It is a beautiful beat, but Kessa’s acceptance could use one extra moment of reluctance or practicality.

**Possible small addition:**

> “He walks in my steps until I say otherwise.”

That preserves her distrust while still allowing the group to form.

### 5. The final paragraph may be slightly too expository

The ending hook about other cradles, other dead-houses, and other fonts is good. But it may be slightly too direct. It moves from Oscar’s intimate emotional ache to a narrator-like series premise.

**How to fix it:**

Keep the cradles/other sleepers idea, but make it feel more like dread than a series bible reveal. Let the reader feel the scale rather than receive the map.

## Arc 1 Revision Priorities

1. **Trim Chapter 8 by 10–15%.** Keep the choice, cost, and payoff. Cut repeated explanations of “set,” “finished,” and “whole.”
2. **Preserve the “aiming” motif.** This is one of the book’s strongest thematic/magic-system bridges.
3. **Add one more Kessa grief beat.** Very small, preferably physical, connected to Doss or to her fear of taking another wrong road.
4. **Reduce “three centuries” repetition.** It works, but across Arc 1 and Arc 2 it becomes one of Oscar’s most repeated self-definitions.
5. **Tighten the final omniscient hook.** Keep the cradles/other sleepers idea, but make it more ominous and less explanatory.

# Arc 2 — Marrow

## Overall Editorial Read

Arc 2 is strong and more commercially propulsive than Arc 1. Marrow gives the story a concrete social problem, the Tithe gives Oscar an external enemy tied directly to his identity mystery, and Wick, Cael, and Kessa all get meaningful progression beats rather than standing around while Oscar solves everything.

The arc also does a good job turning “magic ruins mystery” into “economic exploitation, debt, ownership, and rebellion,” which gives the fantasy setting adult weight.

The biggest issue to fix is **repetition of insight**. The prose often lands a great thematic or emotional point, then restates it in slightly different language two or three more times. This is especially noticeable around Oscar’s identity anxiety, Wick’s hidden fire/door, the Tithe’s debt machine, and the idea that “the lie was not the danger, the lie was that it was forever.”

These are good ideas. The revision should trust them more.

The second biggest issue is that the final public reversal in Chapters 16–17 is emotionally satisfying but a little too clean once it begins. The setup is strong, Sarn is effective, and Oscar’s speech has power, but the crowd turns almost exactly when the chapter needs them to turn.

Add one sharper complication, one cost, or one person in the crowd who resists the truth before the dam breaks.

## What Is Working

### 1. Oscar’s central tension remains compelling

His arrogance is not just flavor; it is a defense mechanism for a man who may not actually own the life he remembers. That is the right kind of arrogance.

It is funny, useful, and sometimes ugly. The best version appears when he wants to say something clever and chooses not to, or when someone else punctures him before he can become unbearable.

### 2. Marrow is a strong setting

The town built into the bones of old ruins is vivid and thematically useful. It is not just a cool fantasy location; it mirrors the whole arc.

Everyone is living inside the remains of a dead power structure, economically and literally. That is excellent fantasy design.

### 3. The Tithe is a better antagonist than a generic evil guild

The debt/credit/factor system is clear, hateful, and believable.

“The arithmetic does the cruelty” is one of the arc’s strongest ideas. It fits Oscar’s mind and gives him a reason to both admire and despise the system.

### 4. Wick’s chapter is one of the strongest sections

Her POV has a different rhythm from Oscar’s. The emphasis on holding back being harder than burning is emotionally clear and genre-satisfying.

It also reframes her power in a way that makes her progression feel earned, not just bigger.

### 5. The progression beats are satisfying

Oscar’s lance holding again, Wick aiming cleanly, Cael receiving the shield, Kessa’s silent boots, Oscar learning a binding from the grimoire: these are all good progression fantasy payoffs.

They are concrete upgrades attached to character identity, not random loot.

### 6. The crew dynamic is working

Kessa reads risk, Cael protects, Wick burns/learns, Oscar interprets. That is a clean party structure, and the arc repeatedly gives each of them a job only they can do.

## Biggest Arc 2 Problems

### 1. The thematic points are often repeated after they land

**Issue:**

The arc frequently explains an insight, dramatizes it, then explains it again. This happens with Oscar’s borrowed life, Wick’s dangerous inner power, the Tithe’s debt machine, and the lie of the good claim.

**Why it matters:**

Adult fantasy readers like layered meaning, but they do not want to feel the book is underlining its own thesis. The more often Oscar explains the meaning of a moment, the less room the reader has to feel smart for seeing it.

**Passages to look for:**

Search for phrases and structures like:

- “that was the thing”
- “that was the truth of him”
- “he understood two things”
- “the lie was...”
- “the arithmetic...”
- “he filed it away”
- “the list was getting long”
- “not X, but Y”

These are not bad individually. They are part of your style. But there are too many of them doing the same kind of interpretive labor.

**How to fix it:**

For each major insight, allow yourself one clean articulation, then let action carry the rest.

For example, the Tithe reveal can follow this pattern:

1. Oscar realizes the good claim monster could have been killed.
2. Brida reacts.
3. The public speech weaponizes that truth.

Right now, the idea is explained in Chapter 15, explained to Brida in Chapter 16, then explained again to the crowd in Chapter 17. Keep all three beats, but compress the second one.

Brida does not need the full public version before the public version.

### 2. Oscar’s voice is strong but over-patterned

**Issue:**

Oscar has a very identifiable narration pattern: clever observation, abstract moral insight, self-correction, ironic aside. It is entertaining, but by Chapters 14–17 the rhythm becomes predictable.

**Why it matters:**

Oscar’s arrogance works best when it surprises us or reveals vulnerability. When every paragraph arrives with the same elegant turn, the arrogance starts to feel like the prose showing off rather than Oscar protecting himself.

**Specific pattern to reduce:**

> He saw X. Not X, exactly, but a cousin of X. He had spent three centuries doing Y, and now, a week old, found Z. He filed it away under things to be suspicious of in himself.

Good voice. Too much of the same move.

**How to fix it:**

Give Oscar more blunt sentences at moments of stress. Let him occasionally think something ugly, small, or practical without polishing it into a theorem.

Especially in action scenes, reduce the interior essaying. His wit should sharpen under pressure, not broaden into explanation.

### 3. The final uprising is satisfying but too frictionless once it starts

**Issue:**

The public square sequence has a strong setup: Sarn wants an example, Oscar turns the stage against them. But after Oscar begins speaking, the crowd’s emotional arc moves almost exactly as needed.

**Why it matters:**

A rebellion scene needs volatility. The crowd should not feel like an audience waiting for the protagonist’s thesis. It should feel afraid, divided, angry, and dangerous.

**How to fix it:**

Add one of these:

- A salvager shouts that Oscar is lying because their child died in the claim and they cannot bear the truth.
- A Tithe plant in the crowd accuses Oscar of stealing the haul for himself.
- A cord member kills or injures a townsman before the crowd turns, making the victory cost something.
- Brida speaks one line from the stage before Sarn orders her death, giving the town permission to believe.

This would make the uprising feel less like Oscar persuading the town and more like Oscar striking a match in a room already full of gas.

### 4. Wick’s mystery is compelling but over-described

**Issue:**

The “door,” the “glad ancient attention,” and the thing that wants Wick are effective. But the same core sensation is described multiple times: in Chapter 12, at the pit, after the pit, during Oscar’s concern, and again through the grey token.

**Why it matters:**

Mystery gets weaker when the prose circles it too often without adding new information. The first reveal is excellent. Later references should either add a new fact or stay quieter.

**How to fix it:**

After the first major reveal, switch to symptoms rather than explanation:

- Wick cannot taste food.
- The token is warm only when no one looks.
- Her shadow bends toward old stone.
- She avoids Oscar’s eyes.
- The mark at her throat goes cold instead of hot.

Let the reader infer the same dread from concrete details.

### 5. Sarn is effective but slightly too perfectly composed

**Issue:**

Sarn’s bored accountant menace is good. The ledger, scar, and arithmetic voice all work. But Sarn is almost always exactly as controlled, clever, and rhetorically neat as the scene needs.

**Why it matters:**

A villain becomes more memorable when their control has texture. Sarn currently reads as a very good antagonist function. Give them one strange human detail or one crack that is not just “the mask drops.”

**How to fix it:**

Give Sarn a behavioral tell:

- They hate dirty hands.
- They refuse to step over blood.
- They over-tip ledgers when angry.
- They compulsively correct names.
- They genuinely believe the Tithe keeps Marrow alive.

Even better: make their ideology slightly persuasive. Let them claim Marrow collapses without the Tithe’s order. That gives Oscar something harder to defeat than greed.

### 6. The action scenes sometimes explain the tactics too fully

**Issue:**

The fights are clear, but Oscar often explains the tactic before, during, and after it. The hush fight especially has strong pieces, but the prose occasionally slows to make sure we understand the plan.

**Why it matters:**

In action, clarity matters, but over-clarity drains urgency. Readers can infer more than you think.

**How to fix it:**

In combat, keep explanation to:

1. Problem
2. Plan
3. Consequence

Cut repeated tactical interpretation once the reader has the map.

## Line-Level Prose Notes

### Repeated sentence rhythms

You use a lot of long, rolling sentences with interruptions: em dashes, parentheticals, reversals, and “not X but Y” constructions.

This creates a strong literary fantasy voice, but it can become samey. Add more hard short sentences in moments of fear.

**Example rhythm to reduce:**

> It was not horror, exactly, but a cousin of it.  
> It was not dead work, but sleeping.  
> It was not clean and not bloodless.  
> Not the danger; the danger was real. The lie was...

Keep the structure where it hits hardest. Cut it where it is only adding style.

### Overused punctuation

The em dash is doing a lot of work. It fits Oscar’s mind, but across a whole arc it becomes visible.

Replace some em dashes with periods. Let some thoughts end cleanly.

### Overused rhetorical structures

“Because of course...” appears often enough that it risks becoming a tic.

Same with:

- “that was the thing”
- “he filed it away”
- “the list was getting long”
- “the way X does Y”
- “not X, but Y”

These are good in moderation. In revision, search for them and cut 30–40%.

### Over-explaining

The Tithe’s debt structure is explained well in Chapter 11. Later, you can rely on the reader’s understanding.

The public speech should not need to restate every piece of the economics; it should weaponize the emotional truth.

### Abstract emotional language

You sometimes name emotions after already showing them: fear, dread, hope, relief, grief.

The scenes are strongest when the emotion is embodied:

- Brida gripping the ladle
- Cael touching the token
- Wick’s hand going to the pocket
- Oscar covering the loop on his sword

### Prose that sounds impressive but says little

Phrases like “a dark he could not see the bottom of” are evocative, but they recur in different forms. Once or twice, great. Repeatedly, it becomes atmospheric filler.

Replace some with specific unknowns:

- Who issued the writ?
- What does “set” mean?
- Why alive?
- Why undamaged?
- Why does the southern house know more about Oscar than Oscar does?

### Dialogue polish

Oscar should sound polished. Sarn can sound polished.

But occasionally Kessa, Cael, and Brida also produce very clean thematic summaries. Brida’s grief lands best when plain. Kessa lands best when blunt. Cael lands best when simple and morally direct.

## Character Voice Notes

### Oscar

Compelling, but needs slightly more variation.

His arrogance is best when it is clearly compensating for fear or shame. It is weakest when he gives yet another elegant internal analysis of the same wound.

Let him be arrogant through action sometimes:

- making a risky assumption
- correcting someone at the wrong moment
- quietly enjoying a tactical victory
- underestimating a mundane person and being punished for it

Also let him be petty once or twice. That will make the vulnerability feel less curated.

### Wick

Very strong.

Her POV feels younger, more impulsive, and more embodied than Oscar’s. Her devotion to Oscar is emotionally believable because he gives her a framework for strength instead of treating her as a hazard.

Watch the risk of making her admiration too complete. A good apprentice should sometimes misunderstand, resent, or challenge the teacher.

### Kessa

Distinct and useful.

She has the best compression of speech. Lines in the spirit of “with respect, you talk like a man who’s never paid for anything in his life” are exactly where her voice shines.

Preserve that.

Give her one private cost in Arc 2 if possible, because she is currently very competent but less emotionally exposed than Cael and Wick.

### Cael

Very good moral center.

His shield progression is excellent because it literalizes who he is. Be careful not to make every Cael line a benediction.

Let him be tired, irritated, or physically practical more often. His holiness is more persuasive when it is not always phrased as wisdom.

### Brida

Works well as the human face of Marrow. Her grief is concrete.

The only caution: do not let her become merely the symbolic widow of the good claim. A small personal detail unrelated to her husband would help her feel fully alive.

### Sarn

Good antagonist presence.

Needs one extra dimension beyond “bored, clever, ledger-minded.” Even a small belief like “without the Tithe, Marrow starves” would make them more dangerous.

## Fantasy / Progression Reader Expectations

### Magic-system clarity

Good and improving.

Oscar reads workings, identifies seams, uses figures, spends from a well, and can mend old-world objects. Wick has raw heat and must learn aim/containment. Cael uses granted warmth/protection.

These are distinct enough.

The next revision should make costs more consistently visible:

- fatigue
- reserve depletion
- risk of overreach
- emotional aftereffects
- visible bodily consequences

### Power level

Mostly clear.

Oscar is no longer helpless, but he is not restored to god-mode. Wick is potentially terrifying but limited by control. Cael’s shield is powerful but draining. Kessa remains mundane but extremely high-skill.

That balance is good.

### Stakes

Strong.

Personal stakes:

- Oscar is wanted alive and unset.
- Oscar may not own his memories.
- Quill may still exist somewhere.
- Wick may be tied to something ancient and hungry.

Social stakes:

- Marrow is enslaved by debt.
- The Tithe profits from fear and death.
- The southern house is extracting old-world relics and marked people.

Future stakes:

- The southern house now knows about Oscar.
- The Redactor may still be loose.
- Other cradles and other sleepers exist.

### Mystery

Strong, but some mysteries are being verbalized too often.

The “unset/set” question is excellent. The grey token is a good hook. The southern house is a good forward engine.

### Forward momentum

Very strong from Chapter 11 onward. Chapter 10 is more transitional but necessary.

Arc 2 ends with a clear vector south, which is exactly what a progression/adventure reader wants.

### Character growth

Excellent.

Oscar moves from self-protective cleverness toward chosen responsibility. Wick moves from dangerous overflow toward controlled agency. Cael moves from guilt over Doss toward active protection. Kessa moves from survival-first pragmatism toward hope.

This is probably the combined arcs’ greatest strength.

### Payoff and hook

Arc 1 pays off Oscar’s first identity choice and opens the larger mystery of the cradles.

Arc 2 pays off Marrow and opens the southern-house plot effectively.

Sarn’s escape is smart because it prevents the local victory from feeling final.

# Combined Arc 1 + Arc 2 Comparison

## Arc 1 is more elegant

Arc 1 has less plot sprawl, fewer moving pieces, and a cleaner symbolic structure. The dead city, the font, the Redactor, Oscar’s missing familiar, and the cradles all support one core question.

## Arc 2 is more commercially propulsive

Arc 2 gives the reader more external stakes, faction politics, loot/progression, public conflict, and a broader world. It is more likely to satisfy progression fantasy readers looking for forward movement and expanding systems.

## Together, they work

Arc 1 is the mythic origin arc.

Arc 2 is the “now enter the world” arc.

That is the right sequence.

The main revision challenge is making sure Arc 2 does not feel like it is restating Arc 1’s identity themes with different scenery. Arc 2 should evolve the question:

- Arc 1 asks: **What is Oscar?**
- Arc 2 should ask: **Who wants Oscar, and what does the world do to people like him?**

# Prioritized Revision Checklist for a Writing Agent

## Highest Priority

1. **Cut repeated thematic explanation across both arcs.**
   - Keep the best line.
   - Cut the echo.
   - Trust the scene and the reader.

2. **Trim Chapter 8 of Arc 1 by 10–15%.**
   - Preserve Oscar’s choice.
   - Preserve the font’s temptation.
   - Preserve the “aiming” payoff.
   - Cut repeated explanations of being set/finished/whole.

3. **Compress Arc 2’s repeated Tithe explanation.**
   - Chapter 11 explains the machine.
   - Chapter 15 reveals the lie.
   - Chapter 17 weaponizes the truth.
   - Chapter 16 should not fully rehearse the public speech.

4. **Add friction to the Arc 2 uprising.**
   - Include one dissenting voice, one Tithe counterclaim, or one visible cost before the crowd turns.

5. **Give Sarn one ideological or behavioral dimension.**
   - They should not just be a perfect ledger-villain.
   - Let them believe they preserve order.

## Medium Priority

6. **Vary Oscar’s internal rhythm.**
   - More blunt thoughts under stress.
   - Fewer elegant explanatory reversals.
   - More arrogance through action, not just commentary.

7. **Reduce repeated phrases and structures.**
   - Search for “that was the thing,” “filed it away,” “because of course,” “three centuries,” “not X but Y,” and repeated em-dash interruptions.
   - Cut 30–40% of the repeated instances.

8. **Make Wick’s hidden power more concrete after the first reveal.**
   - Use symptoms and behavior instead of repeatedly describing the ancient glad reaching thing.

9. **Add one more Kessa emotional beat in Arc 1 or Arc 2.**
   - Keep it physical and understated.
   - Avoid a speech.

10. **Make magic costs more consistently visible.**
   - Especially after Oscar learns or uses new bindings.
   - Especially after Wick overreaches.
   - Especially after Cael’s shield use.

## Lower Priority but Valuable

11. **Give Brida one personal detail unrelated to her husband.**
   - This will prevent her from functioning only as “the widow of the claim.”

12. **Add a recurring sensory marker unique to Marrow.**
   - Scale bells.
   - Red cord fibers in gutters.
   - Sour tanning vats.
   - Chalk debt marks on doors.
   - The sound of ledgers snapping shut.

13. **Tighten the final omniscient hook of Arc 1.**
   - Keep the dread of other cradles.
   - Reduce the sense of narrator-style series setup.

14. **Keep the progression rewards tied to character identity.**
   - Cael’s shield works because it expresses who he is.
   - Kessa’s boots work because they sharpen what she already does.
   - Oscar’s grimoire works because it gives him a path to rebuild himself.
   - Wick’s aim works because it turns fear into control.

# Optional Sample Rewrite: Arc 2 Brida Explanation

Goal: preserve Oscar’s style but avoid fully repeating the later public speech.

## Original Issue

The Brida explanation beat risks becoming a rehearsal for Oscar’s public speech. It explains the Tithe’s lie in full before the crowd scene gets to reveal it.

## Suggested Direction

Let Oscar give Brida the core truth. Let Brida’s reaction carry the rest.

## Sample Rewrite

> “We took the good claim,” Oscar said.
>
> Brida went still.
>
> “We killed what was guarding it.” He set the branded board on her table, seal-up, and watched the lamplight catch in the burned grooves. “There was a monster down there. Real enough. Old enough. It nearly killed us. But it could die, Brida. That is the part they lied about.”
>
> Her eyes moved from the seal to his face.
>
> “The claim was dangerous,” he said. “Your husband knew that when he went down. But it was not holy. It was not fate. It was not the weather. It was a thing with a body, and the Tithe let it keep eating because fear is cheaper than guards and debt is easier to collect from widows.”
>
> For a moment she did not breathe.
>
> Then her good hand closed around the edge of the table, hard enough to whiten the knuckles. “Them,” she said.
>
> Oscar nodded. “Them.”

# Final Editorial Note

The book’s core is working. The protagonist is compelling, the party has chemistry, the magic system has thematic identity, and the world is widening in the right direction.

The manuscript does not need more cleverness. It already has plenty.

It needs selective restraint.

Cut the extra explanation, sharpen the repeated rhythms, add a little more friction to the cleanest victories, and preserve the emotional/magical spine that is already there.
