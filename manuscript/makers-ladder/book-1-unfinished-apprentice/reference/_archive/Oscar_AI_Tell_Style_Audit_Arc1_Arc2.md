# Oscar Book 1 — AI-Tell & Artificial-Prose Style Audit

## Purpose

This document bundles the AI-tell/style feedback for **Book 1, Arc 1: The Buried City** and **Book 1, Arc 2: Marrow**.

The goal is **not** to accuse the manuscript of being AI-written. The goal is to identify recurring prose patterns that may make the writing *feel* AI-assisted to readers, editors, or agents, especially when patterns cluster across chapters.

The manuscript has a strong, distinctive style: literary, cold, witty, arrogant, wounded, and thematically coherent. The main risk is not generic prose. The main risk is **over-consistency**: too many passages using the same polished sentence architecture, emotional interpretation, em-dash rhythm, and abstract/thematic closure.

---

# High-Level Diagnosis

## Overall AI-Tell Risk

**Arc 1: Medium-high**  
**Arc 2: Medium**

Arc 1 has the higher risk because it is claustrophobic, interior, mythic, and heavily filtered through Oscar’s elevated voice. The buried city setting supports that style, but there are fewer mundane interruptions, social textures, or messy human details to break up the polish.

Arc 2 still has the same stylistic habits, but Marrow introduces more social friction, practical setting texture, dialogue, and external conflict. That helps humanize the prose. However, the arc still relies heavily on the same rhetorical devices: em-dash reversals, “the way…” comparisons, “not X but Y” contrasts, elegant final-clause landings, and abstract emotional insight.

## Core Risk

The prose often feels **too perfectly interpreted**.

A vivid event happens, then the narration explains its emotional, symbolic, or thematic meaning in a polished way. Many of these lines are strong individually. The problem is accumulation. Across many chapters, readers may begin to feel that every scene beat has been pre-digested into theme.

The revision goal should not be to flatten the style. The goal should be to add:

- more physical mess
- more uneven human reaction
- more practical interruption
- more character-specific voice variation
- fewer polished explanatory landings
- less dependence on em dashes and repeated rhetorical templates

---

# Shared Pattern Clusters Across Arc 1 and Arc 2

## 1. Em-Dash Reliance

The manuscript uses em dashes frequently and often well. They fit Oscar’s interrupted thoughts, self-corrections, reversals, and moments of panic.

However, they are overused as a default rhythm for drama and explanation.

Common pattern:

> X — Y

or:

> Not X — but Y

or:

> He reached — not for X, but for Y

This creates a repeated dramatic pause that may start to feel artificial, especially when paired with italic emphasis and polished final clauses.

### Global Instruction

Cut roughly **30–40%** of em dashes across both arcs.

Keep em dashes for:

- interrupted dialogue
- unfinished thoughts
- genuine mental rupture
- Oscar correcting himself in real time
- sudden reversals during action

Replace weaker em dashes with:

- periods for harder impact
- commas for softer insertions
- colons for explanation
- sentence breaks for dramatic emphasis

---

## 2. Repetitive Sentence Architecture

The manuscript repeatedly uses several strong rhetorical shapes:

### “Not X. Not Y. But Z.”

This structure is effective, but highly visible.

Examples of the pattern:

> Not faded; stopped.

> Not a barrier. Not resistance.

> Not horror, exactly, but a cousin of it.

> Not the deep work.

> Not with knives. With the truth.

### “That was the thing / trouble / horror / lie / part.”

This pattern creates polished interpretive pivots.

Examples:

> That was the thing.

> That was the horror.

> That was the trouble.

> That was the lie.

These lines often work. But they repeat enough that they become a house rhythm.

### “The way…” comparisons

The manuscript leans heavily on “the way…” similes and analogies.

Examples of the type:

> the way a sentence stops when someone has torn the last word off the page

> the way a frozen lake is calm

> the way a man chops wood

> the way a reader loses their place

> the way a name turns over behind your teeth

Many are beautiful. The issue is frequency.

### Global Instruction

For each chapter:

- cut or revise one-third of “the way…” comparisons
- keep only the sharpest “not X but Y” contrasts
- reserve “That was the thing” style pivots for major revelations
- vary paragraph endings so fewer land on polished abstract conclusions

---

## 3. Over-Polished Emotional Interpretation

The prose often converts emotion into elegant insight before the reader has fully felt the physical experience.

This is especially true for Oscar, whose intelligence naturally turns pain into analysis. That is good characterization. But when the technique appears constantly, the emotion can feel arranged rather than lived.

### Common Risk Pattern

1. A frightening or emotional thing happens.
2. Oscar notices it.
3. The narration interprets it perfectly.
4. The paragraph ends on a thematic insight.

### Global Instruction

Before major insights, add more body-first reaction:

- bad breath in cold air
- numb hands
- throat closing
- knees weakening
- a failed joke
- a wrong first assumption
- a physical mistake
- a mundane annoyance
- a too-small response to a huge moment

Let the body react before the mind explains.

---

## 4. Abstract Noun Density

The manuscript frequently uses abstract nouns such as:

- dark
- silence
- truth
- fear
- memory
- weight
- shape
- line
- wrongness
- wanting
- power
- grief
- absence
- whole
- thing

These are part of the book’s mythic vocabulary, and many are necessary. But when multiple abstractions stack in a paragraph, the prose can feel generalized.

### Global Instruction

When a paragraph contains three or more abstract anchors, replace at least one with a concrete sensory or physical detail.

Example:

Instead of only:

> The dark ate every one.

Try something more embodied:

> She said it twice more. By the third time, she did not believe it.

---

## 5. Rhythmic Sameness

Many paragraphs rise and fall in a similar shape:

- descriptive clause
- interpretive turn
- polished final phrase

This gives the book grandeur, but also predictability.

### Global Instruction

Vary paragraph endings. Let more paragraphs end on:

- physical action
- awkward silence
- incomplete observation
- a mundane object
- a practical problem
- dialogue that does not fully resolve the emotion

Examples of grounding endings:

- Kessa checking a bolt
- Wick wiping soot from her mouth
- Cael failing to stand smoothly
- Oscar noticing his sleeve is torn
- someone stepping in old water
- a joke failing to land

---

## 6. Dialogue That Can Become Too Clean

Dialogue is often strong, especially Oscar’s. The risk is that characters sometimes speak in complete, emotionally precise thoughts at exactly the right moment.

### Global Instruction

Add more imperfect dialogue:

- interruptions
- half-answers
- wrong first words
- repeated words
- blunt practical comments
- embarrassment after sincerity
- characters dodging the emotional point

This is especially important for Wick and Cael.

---

## 7. Lack of Human Mess

The manuscript is thematically coherent. Almost every image points toward identity, writing, debt, completion, inheritance, or being set.

That coherence is impressive. It also risks feeling engineered.

### Global Instruction

Add small contradictions and mundane interruptions:

- bad stew
- old boot mud
- ink stains
- broken straps
- smoke in the eyes
- hunger
- fatigue mistakes
- awkward silence
- someone misunderstanding a poetic line
- someone saying something too simple for the moment

Human mess will make the elevated style feel earned.

# Arc 1 — The Buried City Audit

## AI-Tell Risk Summary

**Risk: Medium-high.**

Arc 1 is strong and distinctive, but the prose is highly controlled from beginning to end. The buried city is mythic, cold, and interior, which supports the elevated style. But across nine chapters, the lack of mundane texture and the frequency of polished interpretive narration create the highest AI-tell risk in the current manuscript.

The arc’s biggest issue is not that it sounds generic. It is that it sounds **too consistently literary**.

## Em Dash Audit

Approximate count: **302 em dashes** across Arc 1.  
Approximate density: **11.8 em dashes per 1,000 words.**

### Approximate Chapter Breakdown

| Chapter | Approx. Em Dashes | Risk |
|---|---:|---|
| Ch. 1 — Surfacing | 42 | High |
| Ch. 2 — The Salvage Crew | 24 | Medium |
| Ch. 3 — The Dead Don’t Knock | 40 | High |
| Ch. 4 — What the Fire Showed | 37 | Medium-high |
| Ch. 5 — Old Hands | 27 | Medium |
| Ch. 6 — The Redactor | 29 | Medium-high |
| Ch. 7 — The Heart of the City | 25 | Medium-high |
| Ch. 8 — Setting | 40 | High |
| Ch. 9 — The Thaw | 26 | Medium-high |

### Arc 1 Em Dash Instruction

Cut roughly **80–100 em dashes** across Arc 1.

Prioritize cuts in:

- Chapter 1
- Chapter 3
- Chapter 4
- Chapter 8

Keep dashes for Oscar’s real-time denial and fractured memory. Remove them where they are only creating stylish pause.

## Arc 1 Repetition Audit

### Repeated Concepts

Oscar repeatedly:

- reaches for power and finds it gone
- reaches for Quill and finds absence
- refuses to finish a thought
- notices his hands/face are wrong
- realizes the city knows him
- realizes Kessa has already figured something out
- converts fear into analysis

These beats are core to the arc, but they need more variation.

### Specific Revision Instruction

Do not always name “three centuries.” Sometimes use memory fragments instead:

- a half-remembered gallery
- the shape of an old desk
- a scar that should be there
- Quill’s talons in wool
- a spell he knows by hand but not history

This will keep the identity crisis from becoming repetitive.

## Most Artificial-Sounding Arc 1 Passages

### 1. “The chief advantage of a lie told to oneself…”

This is a strong Oscar line, but it is highly aphoristic and quote-ready.

Recommendation: **Keep it**, because it establishes Oscar well, but avoid stacking too many similar aphorisms near it.

### 2. Chapter 1 magic explanation

The magic explanation is clear and lyrical, but very refined. Add one practical, ugly, or bodily detail to ground it.

Possible grounding details:

- his finger cramps during the failed working
- the failed light smells faintly metallic
- he tastes blood after trying
- his hand makes the right motion too perfectly and humiliates him

### 3. “You learn a thing twice…”

The line is good, but very polished for a combat scene.

Recommendation: Either shorten it or move part of the reflection after the fight.

### 4. Redactor logic

The Redactor is excellent. The danger is over-explanation.

Keep:

> You can't kill a thing that treats its own body as a draft.

But trim some of the surrounding explanation. Let the horror of the bolt falling through the edited gap carry more weight.

### 5. Chapter 8 climax

Chapter 8 is emotionally strong but the most AI-tell-risky chapter in Arc 1. It is almost entirely grand, interpretive, and perfectly shaped.

Add chaos:

- Kessa runs out of bolts
- Cael’s grip hurts Oscar
- Wick asks the wrong question at the right time
- Oscar loses the thread for half a second
- a stone clips someone
- the font’s light makes it hard to see
- Oscar thinks one petty, stupid thought before the heroic one

## What To Preserve in Arc 1

Preserve:

- Oscar’s defensive arrogance
- the Quill absence motif
- the “city as sentence” concept
- the Redactor as editor/corrector monster
- Kessa’s practical authority
- Cael’s plain moral center
- Wick learning aim instead of control
- Oscar spending the inheritance instead of keeping it
- the final reveal that there are other cradles and other dead-houses

## Arc 1 Revision Instructions

### Cut

- excess em dashes
- repeated “three centuries” phrasing
- explanatory follow-up after strong images
- some “That was the thing” pivots
- some “the way…” comparisons

### Vary

- paragraph endings
- sentence rhythm
- emotional processing
- how Oscar avoids thoughts
- how each character responds to fear

### Make More Specific

Add sensory details:

- old ice smell
- wet wool
- grit under fingernails
- stale breath in sealed stone
- metal taste after magic
- numb feet
- stiff leather
- flint failing before the fire catches

### Roughen

Let Oscar’s wit fail occasionally. Let him be petty, irritated, physically uncomfortable, or wrong.

## Arc 1 Sample Humanizing Rewrite

Target: Chapter 8 climax.

```markdown
The font came up through the crack.

For one breath, Oscar had it.

Not a trickle. Not the thin scraped thing he had been nursing since the cradle. The whole current surged into his hands and up his arms, hot and cold together, so much of it that his teeth locked and his knees nearly went. He could have kept it. He knew that at once. The font wanted him to know.

Step in. Be finished. Be what you remember.

His hands stopped shaking.

That was almost enough to undo him.

Then Cael made a sound behind him. Not a word. Just a strained breath through clenched teeth. The hands on Oscar’s shoulders tightened, and the warmth holding him together flickered.

Across the chamber, Kessa fired again. The bolt hit the Redactor and vanished through the neat little absence it made of itself. She cursed this time. Short, ugly, frightened. Then she reached for another bolt.

There was not another bolt.

Wick stood beside the broken basin with both hands still raised, white-faced, waiting for him to tell her whether she had killed him.

Oscar looked at the three of them and understood, with a clarity that had nothing elegant in it, that he was not holding the font alone.

Cael was holding him.

Kessa was holding the seconds.

Wick had opened the door.

All he had was the pen.

So he wrote.
```

# Arc 2 — Marrow Audit

## AI-Tell Risk Summary

**Risk: Medium.**

Arc 2 benefits from Marrow’s social texture: money, debt, salvage, boarding houses, corded enforcers, factors, markets, and townspeople. This gives the prose more grounded friction than Arc 1.

The risk remains because the narration still often uses the same polished pattern:

- vivid beat
- interpretation
- thematic explanation
- elegant final clause

The strongest AI-tell risk appears in:

- Chapter 12 — Wick’s POV, when her interiority becomes too polished
- Chapter 15 — The Good Claim, where action logic becomes highly essayed
- Chapter 16 — The Tithe lie explanation, where the theme is very directly stated

## Em Dash Audit

Approximate count: **284 em dashes** across Arc 2.  
Approximate density: **12 em dashes per 1,000 words.**

### Highest-Risk Chapters

| Chapter | Approx. Em Dashes | Risk |
|---|---:|---|
| Ch. 15 — The Good Claim | 58 | High |
| Ch. 12 — Kindling | 37 | Medium-high |
| Ch. 14 — The Descent | 36 | Medium-high |
| Ch. 17 — The Example | 38 | Medium-high |
| Ch. 11 — A Day’s Grace | 32 | Medium-high |

### Arc 2 Em Dash Instruction

Cut roughly **30–40%** of em dashes.

Prioritize cuts in:

- Chapter 15
- Chapter 12
- Chapter 14
- Chapter 17

Keep dashes for live speech, sudden interruption, Oscar’s avoidance, and Wick’s panic. Replace explanatory dashes with periods or colons.

## Arc 2 Repetition Audit

### Repeated Structures

Watch especially:

- “That was the thing.”
- “That was the part.”
- “That was the lie.”
- “Not X, but Y.”
- “the way…” comparisons
- paragraph endings that summarize the theme

### Repeated Arc Concepts

Arc 2 strongly repeats:

- arithmetic
- debt
- ledgers
- cost
- lies
- value
- proof
- being wanted
- being set/unset

This is good thematic work, but it is very coherent. Consider giving certain metaphor fields to specific characters:

- Sarn/Tithe: ledgers, sums, debt, arithmetic
- Oscar: proof, grammar, history, identity
- Kessa: terrain, exits, risk, leverage
- Cael: bodies, vows, mercy, hard things
- Wick: heat, hunger, fear, wanting, shame

This will reduce the sense that the whole book speaks in one metaphor system.

## Most Artificial-Sounding Arc 2 Passages

### 1. Wick’s admiration of Oscar

Risky line type:

> He was magnificent. He was the most magnificent thing she had ever seen.

This is emotionally clear but too declarative and polished. For Wick, show admiration through fixation, embarrassment, imitation, or sensory detail.

### 2. Oscar’s mentor aphorisms

Risky line type:

> That’s the whole job, Wick. That’s the entire job. Everything else is just decoration.

This is a good line, but very quote-ready. Keep the idea, but roughen delivery in some places.

### 3. Tithe lie explanation

The Tithe material is compelling. The key idea is excellent:

> The Tithe did not invent the danger. They monetized it.

But the prose sometimes explains this too completely. Let Brida, Kessa, or the crowd assemble some of the truth themselves.

Keep:

> The lie is that it was forever.

Cut or condense some of the surrounding explanation.

### 4. Chapter 15 plan logic

The scene is exciting, but the narration explains the fight with very clean postmortem clarity while it is happening.

Add temporary confusion:

- Oscar is wrong for three seconds
- Kessa misunderstands the first instruction
- Wick acts before the explanation finishes
- Cael asks something blunt and practical
- someone is injured by the plan’s success

## What To Preserve in Arc 2

Preserve:

- Marrow as a town built on debt and salvage
- Sarn’s polite ledger cruelty
- Brida’s grief and practical kindness
- Wick’s “holding is harder than aiming” emotional core
- Oscar’s growing suspicion of his own comforting lies
- Cael receiving the shield and realizing it failed its previous owner
- Kessa’s unsentimental competence
- the Tithe’s lie about the good claim
- the southern house / loop mark connection
- the ghost-writ / unset mystery

## Arc 2 Revision Instructions

### Cut

- repeated explanatory moral summaries
- excess “That was the thing” transitions
- some “the way…” comparisons
- some perfect mentor aphorisms
- excess em dashes in Chapter 15

### Vary

- Wick’s POV sentence texture
- Oscar’s explanations during action
- dialogue roughness
- paragraph endings
- metaphor fields by character

### Make More Specific

Add mundane Marrow texture:

- cheap cord dye staining wrists
- bad stew crusted on bowls
- children watching under carts
- factor ink on thumbs
- salvage dust in coughs
- wet boots on boarding house floors
- old lamps patched with wire
- thin blankets in the loft
- forge grit in market air

### Roughen

Especially roughen Wick’s POV.

Wick should think in:

- heat
- hunger
- shame
- fear
- speed
- irritation
- wanting
- sudden fierce loyalty

She should not always think in Oscar-level thematic phrasing.

## Arc 2 Sample Humanizing Rewrite

Target: Wick alone after the diversion in Chapter 12.

```markdown
It worked.

That was the part Wick kept telling herself as she ran. It worked. The cord had gone after the fire. Kessa’s path was clear. Oscar was clear. Cael was clear.

Probably.

She slowed after the third turn because her side had started stabbing and because she had no idea where her feet were taking her. The tunnel sloped down. That seemed bad. Most things that went down in Marrow were bad. She put one hand on the wall and felt grit, old frost, something damp she decided very firmly was water.

“Good,” she whispered. “Fine. This is fine.”

Her voice sounded stupid down here. Too small. The dark took it and made nothing of it.

The heat in her chest pressed against her ribs, wanting out. She held it down with both fists clenched and her jaw aching. No sparks. Not one. Kessa had said it like not burning was easy, like Wick had ever done anything else her whole life.

She took another step.

Something scraped in the dark ahead.

Wick stopped breathing.

The scrape came again. Dry. Many little points touching stone. Not the cord. Not boots. Not people.

“Oh,” she said, because apparently that was all the cleverness she had in her.

The heat surged up hard enough to sting her eyes.

“No,” she told it. Then, louder, to the thing coming toward her: “No. You stay there.”

It did not stay there.
```

# Priority Revision Checklist

## Highest Priority

1. Cut 30–40% of em dashes.
2. Reduce “the way…” comparisons by about one-third.
3. Reduce “That was the thing / trouble / horror / lie” transitions.
4. Roughen Chapter 8 of Arc 1.
5. Roughen Wick’s POV in Arc 2, especially Chapter 12.
6. Add mundane physical texture to Marrow.
7. Let more emotional beats land through action before explanation.

## Medium Priority

1. Make character metaphor fields more distinct.
2. Give Oscar a few failed lines or wrong guesses.
3. Let Kessa stay practical rather than thematic.
4. Let Cael speak more plainly and less perfectly.
5. Make Wick more physically reactive and less essay-like.

## Lower Priority

1. Preserve the elevated style overall.
2. Do not overcorrect into bland minimalism.
3. Do not remove all em dashes.
4. Do not remove the sentence/editing metaphor system.
5. Do not flatten Oscar’s voice.

# Final Editorial Note

The manuscript does **not** have a generic AI voice problem. It has a **too-much-of-a-good-thing** problem.

The prose is often excellent. The voice is distinct. The magic system and identity themes are strong. But because the strongest techniques repeat so reliably, they begin to announce themselves.

The fix is not to make the writing less literary. The fix is to make it less uniformly literary.

Let the beautiful lines stay beautiful. Then surround them with more dirt, breath, awkwardness, hunger, interruption, bad timing, and blunt human reaction.

That contrast will make the best lines feel authored rather than manufactured.
